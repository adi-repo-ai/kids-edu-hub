=== a-letter-to-god ===
## The big idea
**A Letter to God** by **G.L. Fuentes** is a short story about a poor farmer whose faith in God is so complete that he writes a letter to Him asking for money. It is a story about **faith**, **kindness** and a delicious **irony**: the very people who help Lencho are the ones he ends up calling thieves.

> [!KEY] One-line summary
> A hailstorm destroys Lencho's crop; he writes to God for 100 pesos; the postmaster collects 70 pesos and sends it signed "God"; Lencho angrily accuses the post office employees of stealing the missing 30.

## Summary
- **The setting:** Lencho's house stands alone on the crest of a low hill, in a valley in Mexico. From there he can see his field of ripe corn dotted with flowers, which promises a good harvest. The only thing the earth needs is a downpour.
- **The rain and the hail:** The rain comes, and Lencho compares the big drops to **"new coins"** — ten-cent and five-cent pieces. But a strong wind brings huge **hailstones**, which he calls **"frozen pearls"**. For an hour the hail falls, and the field becomes white as if covered in salt. Lencho says the hail has left them worse off than a **plague of locusts**: "we'll go hungry this year".
- **The letter:** That night, Lencho's only hope is God, "whose eyes see everything, even what is deep in one's conscience". On Sunday he writes a letter asking God for **100 pesos** to sow his field again and live until the new crop comes. He addresses it **"To God"** and posts it.
- **The postmaster:** The postmaster, a fat, amiable man, first laughs, then is moved by such faith. Not wanting to shake it, he decides to answer the letter. He collects money from his employees, gives part of his salary, and asks friends to contribute. He manages to collect only **a little more than half — 70 pesos**. He puts the money in an envelope, signs it **"God"** and sends it.
- **The ending:** Lencho shows no surprise at receiving money (such is his confidence), but he becomes **angry** on counting it. He writes a second letter asking God for the remaining **30 pesos**, but requests that it not be sent by mail, because **"the post office employees are a bunch of crooks"**.

## Characters
| Character | Traits |
|---|---|
| **Lencho** | Hardworking farmer, knows his field well, has **unshakeable faith in God**, simple and naive, but also ungrateful and suspicious of people. |
| **The postmaster** | Kind, generous, sensitive; respects faith; helps without wanting credit. |
| **Lencho's family** | Wife and sons who work in the field; they share his despair after the hail. |

## Themes and literary devices
- **Faith:** Lencho's faith is total. He trusts God completely but does not trust human beings.
- **Kindness of strangers:** The postmaster and his staff give their own money to an unknown man.
- **Irony:** The people who helped him are called crooks. The postmaster was proud of his "good deed" while Lencho thought it was theft. This is **situational irony**.
- **Imagery and simile:** raindrops as "new coins", hail as "frozen pearls", the field white "as if covered with salt".
- **Contrast:** the hope of the morning vs the sorrow of the evening; physical storm vs the "storm" in Lencho's heart.

> [!REMEMBER] Numbers examiners ask
> Asked for: **100 pesos**. Received: **70 pesos**. Asked for again: **30 pesos**. The postmaster signed the letter **"God"**.

> [!MISTAKE] Lencho is not a cheat
> Some students write that Lencho was greedy. He asks only for what he needs to replant and survive. His flaw is naive mistrust of people, not greed.

## Message and value points
- Faith gives strength in hard times, but blind faith without common sense can lead us to wrong judgements.
- True charity is done quietly, without expecting thanks.
- We should be grateful for help and not judge people hastily.

> [!TIP] Likely questions
> "What was the irony in the story?" "Describe the postmaster's character." "How did the hailstorm affect Lencho?" "Why did Lencho get angry on receiving the money?" Always mention the 70/30 numbers and the phrase "bunch of crooks".

## Quick recap
- Author: G.L. Fuentes; set in rural Mexico.
- Rain = new coins; hail = frozen pearls; worse than locusts.
- Letter "To God" for 100 pesos; postmaster sends 70 pesos signed "God".
- Lencho calls post office employees "a bunch of crooks" — the central irony.

W[3]: Describe the character of the postmaster in "A Letter to God".

W[5]: "A Letter to God" is full of irony. Explain with examples.
A: The story's irony lies in the gap between what is expected and what happens. Lencho has unshakeable faith in God but no faith in human beings. The helpers are accused of theft, while God, who did not send any money, is trusted fully. Even the rain, first seen as "new coins", turns into destructive hail. The irony teaches that blind faith can make us unjust towards good people.

=== nelson-mandela-long-walk-to-freedom ===
## The big idea
This extract from **Nelson Mandela's autobiography, *Long Walk to Freedom*,** describes the day he became the **first Black President of South Africa** and his reflections on what freedom really means. It teaches that courage, sacrifice and love can triumph over hatred.

> [!KEY] Key facts
> - **Date:** 10 May 1994 — inauguration at the **Union Buildings in Pretoria**.
> - South Africa's **first democratic, non-racial government**.
> - Two Deputy Presidents: **Thabo Mbeki** and **F.W. de Klerk**.
> - Ended **apartheid**, a system of racial discrimination and segregation.

## Summary
- **The inauguration:** Leaders and dignitaries from more than 140 countries gathered. Mandela took the oath and pledged to liberate the people from bondage, poverty, deprivation and discrimination. He said: never again shall this beautiful land experience the oppression of one by another.
- **The display of the military:** Jets, helicopters and troop carriers flew in formation over the Union Buildings. The highest generals saluted Mandela and pledged loyalty — the same forces that a few years earlier would have arrested him. This showed the army's loyalty to democracy.
- **Two anthems:** Whites sang **"Nkosi Sikelel' iAfrika"** and Blacks sang **"Die Stem"**, the old anthem of the Republic — a symbol of unity, though neither group yet knew the other's song by heart.
- **The cost of freedom:** Mandela recalls that apartheid created one of the most extensive and inhumane societies the world had known. He says the **depths of oppression created heights of character** — leaders like **Oliver Tambo, Walter Sisulu, Chief Luthuli, Yusuf Dadoo, Bram Fischer and Robert Sobukwe**. A country's greatest wealth is its people.
- **Courage:** He learnt that courage was not the absence of fear but **the triumph over it**. The brave man is not one who does not feel afraid, but one who conquers that fear.
- **Love over hate:** No one is born hating another person because of the colour of his skin. If people can learn to hate, they can be taught to love, for love comes more naturally to the human heart.

## Twin obligations
Every man has **twin obligations**:
1. to his **family** — parents, wife and children;
2. to his **people, community and country**.
In a normal society a man can fulfil both. In apartheid South Africa, a Black man who tried to live as a human being was punished and isolated. Mandela was forced to choose his people over his family, which cost him time with his wife and children.

## Mandela's changing idea of freedom
| Stage | What freedom meant |
|---|---|
| **As a boy** | Freedom to run in the fields, swim in the stream, roast mealies, ride bulls — he thought he was born free. |
| **As a young man** | "Transitory freedoms": staying out at night, reading what he liked, going where he chose — and then basic freedoms like earning a living, marrying and having a family. |
| **Later** | He saw that his brothers and sisters were not free. The hunger for his own freedom became the **greater hunger for the freedom of his people**. He joined the **African National Congress**. |

> [!REMEMBER] Oppressor and oppressed
> Mandela says **the oppressor must be liberated just as surely as the oppressed**. A man who takes away another's freedom is a prisoner of hatred, locked behind bars of prejudice. Both are robbed of their humanity.

> [!MISTAKE] Who sang which anthem
> Do not swap them: the **whites** sang Nkosi Sikelel' iAfrika and the **blacks** sang Die Stem — each singing the other's anthem was the symbol of unity.

## Message and value points
- Freedom is indivisible; no one is free while others are in chains.
- Courage means conquering fear; love is more natural than hate.
- Great leaders sacrifice personal happiness for the common good.

> [!TIP] Likely questions
> Twin obligations; meaning of "the depths of oppression create heights of character"; why Mandela calls the oppressor a prisoner; how his idea of freedom changed. Quote one short phrase and explain it in your own words.

## Quick recap
- 10 May 1994, Union Buildings, Pretoria; first non-racial government.
- Deputy Presidents: Thabo Mbeki and F.W. de Klerk.
- Courage = triumph over fear; love comes more naturally than hate.
- Twin obligations: family and people/country.
- Freedom: boyhood illusion → personal freedom → freedom of all his people.

=== two-stories-about-flying ===
## The big idea
This chapter has two short stories. **His First Flight** by **Liam O'Flaherty** is about a young seagull who overcomes fear and learns to fly. **The Black Aeroplane** by **Frederick Forsyth** is a mysterious story about a pilot saved in a storm by a strange black aeroplane. Both are about **courage, fear and trust**.

## Part I: His First Flight
**Summary**
- The young seagull is alone on his ledge. His two brothers and his sister have already flown, but he is afraid — his wings seem too weak to support him over the vast sea.
- His parents scold him, threaten to let him **starve** unless he flies, and his family taunts him for being a coward.
- A full day passes; he is very hungry. He sees his family eating fish on the plateau. His mother picks up a piece of fish and flies towards him with it, then stops just out of reach.
- Maddened by hunger, he **dives at the food**. He falls outwards and downwards, screams with fear, then his wings spread outwards and he begins to **fly**. His family flies around him, praising him.
- He lands on the green sea and is surprised that he does not sink. His family feeds him with fish — he has made his first flight.

**Themes:** fear of the unknown; self-confidence; parents' "tough love"; hunger as motivation; the first step is the hardest.

> [!KEY] The turning point
> **Hunger** made him forget his fear. The mother's trick of holding back the fish was not cruelty but wise parenting.

**Character of the young seagull:** timid, lacking confidence, afraid at first, but capable — he only needed a push.

## Part II: The Black Aeroplane
**Summary**
- The narrator is flying his old **Dakota** aeroplane over France to England, at night. He is happy, thinking of his holiday and a big English breakfast.
- He sees huge **black storm clouds** ahead. He has very little fuel, so he cannot go around them. He decides to take a risk and fly into the storm.
- Inside the clouds everything goes black. The **compass** and other instruments stop working, and the **radio** goes dead. He is lost.
- Suddenly he sees another aeroplane with **no lights**, a black aeroplane. The pilot waves and signals him to follow. The narrator obeys "like an obedient child".
- He is guided out of the clouds and sees two lines of runway lights. He lands safely with almost **no fuel left**. When he turns to thank the other pilot, the black aeroplane has vanished.
- He asks the woman in the **control tower** who the pilot was. She says there was **no other aeroplane** in the sky that night. The mystery is never solved.

**Themes:** courage and risk; the mysterious and the supernatural; help in danger; trusting a guide.

> [!REMEMBER] The pilot's mistake
> The narrator says he should not have flown into the storm. He "took the risk" because of the attractions of home and a holiday — a lesson about impulsive decisions.

> [!MISTAKE] Not a dream
> Do not write that the pilot was dreaming. The story leaves the black aeroplane's identity a mystery; the point is the unexplained help.

## Comparing the two stories
| | His First Flight | The Black Aeroplane |
|---|---|---|
| Fear of | the vast sea, falling | the storm, being lost |
| Help came from | the mother seagull | a mysterious pilot |
| Theme | self-confidence | trust and the unexplained |

> [!TIP] Likely questions
> Why was the seagull afraid? How did his mother make him fly? Why did the pilot take the risk? What was strange about the black aeroplane? Who was the mysterious pilot — give your opinion.

## Quick recap
- His First Flight (Liam O'Flaherty): fear → hunger → dive → flight.
- The Black Aeroplane (Frederick Forsyth): Dakota, storm, dead instruments, mysterious guide, no plane on radar.
- Common thread: overcoming fear; help comes when we most need it.

=== from-the-diary-of-anne-frank ===
## The big idea
**Anne Frank** was a Jewish girl who kept a diary during the Second World War. This extract, from her diary entries of **June 1942**, shows why a lonely thirteen-year-old turned to a diary as a friend, and how she cleverly turned a teacher's punishment into a joke.

> [!KEY] Background facts
> - Born **12 June 1929** in Frankfurt, Germany; family moved to Amsterdam (Netherlands) in 1933 to escape the Nazis.
> - Got the diary as a gift on her **13th birthday**; she named it **Kitty**.
> - From July 1942 the family hid in the **"Secret Annexe"** above her father's office for about two years. They were betrayed in 1944; Anne died in a concentration camp (**Bergen-Belsen**) in early 1945.
> - Her father, **Otto Frank**, the only survivor, published the diary.

## Summary
- **Why a diary?** Anne says writing a diary is a strange experience — nobody will be interested in the musings of a thirteen-year-old girl. But she quotes the saying **"Paper has more patience than people."** She has loving parents, a sister of 16 and about thirty people she calls friends, but no **true friend** to share her innermost feelings. So the diary will be her friend, Kitty.
- **Her family:** Her father married at 36, her mother at 25; sister **Margot** was born in 1926 and Anne in 1929. Her grandmother died in January 1942 — Anne lights a candle for her on her birthday.
- **School worries:** The students are anxious about who will be promoted. Anne is not worried about herself except for maths.
- **Mr Keesing and the chatterbox:** Her maths teacher, **Mr Keesing**, an old fogey, is annoyed with her talking in class. He gives her extra homework:
  1. an essay on **"A Chatterbox"** — Anne argues that talking is a student's trait and she inherited it from her mother, who is equally talkative;
  2. **"An Incorrigible Chatterbox"** — she writes something original;
  3. **"'Quack, Quack, Quack,' Said Mistress Chatterback"** — with help from her friend **Sanne**, she writes a **poem** about a mother duck and a father swan who bit her ducklings to death because they quacked too much.
- Mr Keesing reads the poem in class, takes the joke in the right way, and from then on lets her talk and stops giving her extra homework.

## Character of Anne
- **Sensitive and lonely** — needs a true friend.
- **Witty and intelligent** — turns punishment into humour; argues logically.
- **Honest and frank** — writes about her feelings openly.
- **Mature beyond her years** — observes adults and herself sharply.

## Character of Mr Keesing
Strict but **fair and sporting**: he punished her with essays but appreciated her wit and changed his attitude.

> [!REMEMBER] The three topics, in order
> A Chatterbox → An Incorrigible Chatterbox → "Quack, Quack, Quack," Said Mistress Chatterback.

> [!MISTAKE] Margot vs Sanne
> Margot is Anne's sister; Sanne is the friend who helped her write the poem.

## Themes and value points
- A diary can be a trusted friend for expressing feelings.
- Humour and creativity can handle conflict better than defiance.
- The pain of loneliness even in a crowd; the cruelty of war and the Holocaust in the background.
- Good teachers can accept a joke and change their minds.

> [!TIP] Likely questions
> Why did Anne want to keep a diary? Why did she call it Kitty? How did she justify being talkative? What kind of teacher was Mr Keesing? "Paper has more patience than people" — explain.

## Quick recap
- Diary = Kitty, gift on 13th birthday; "Paper has more patience than people".
- Loneliness despite family and friends.
- Mr Keesing's three essays; the poem won him over.
- Background: Jewish family, hiding in the Secret Annexe, Holocaust.

=== glimpses-of-india ===
## The big idea
**Glimpses of India** has three short pieces that show the variety of India: **A Baker from Goa** (Lucio Rodrigues), **Coorg** (Lokesh Abrol) and **Tea from Assam** (Arup Kumar Datta). Each paints a region through its people, food, culture and landscape.

## Part I: A Baker from Goa
- The author remembers the **Portuguese** days in Goa and its famous loaves of bread. The Portuguese have left, but the **bakers (paders)** still continue their furnaces.
- The baker came twice a day with a jingling **bamboo staff** — the "jhang, jhang" sound woke the children. He carried a basket on his head and a bamboo stick to support it.
- Children rushed to him for **bread-bangles** without even brushing their teeth.
- Bread was essential: **marriage gifts** were meaningless without **sweet bread (bol)**; **cakes and bolinhas** for Christmas and festivals; **sandwiches** for a daughter's engagement.
- The baker wore a **kabai**, a single-piece long frock reaching down to the knees; later shirts and trousers shorter than full-length but longer than half pants.
- **Monthly accounts** were recorded on a wall in pencil.
- Baking was a profitable profession; the baker's family never starved. A **plump physique** was compared to a baker — "like a jackfruit".

## Part II: Coorg
- **Coorg (Kodagu)** is the **smallest district of Karnataka**, between Mysore and Mangalore — "a piece of heaven" that must have drifted from the kingdom of God.
- Evergreen rainforests, spices and **coffee plantations**; best season **September to March**.
- The **Kodavus** are fiercely independent people, possibly of **Greek or Arab** descent (one story says some of Alexander's army settled here). They wear the **kuppia**, a long black coat with an embroidered waist-belt, similar to the Arab kuffia.
- Famous for **hospitality** and **martial traditions**: the **Coorg Regiment** is one of the most decorated in the Indian Army; **General Cariappa**, the first Indian Army Chief, was a Coorgi. Kodavus are the only people in India allowed to carry firearms **without a licence**.
- **River Kaveri** gets its water from these hills; **mahseer** fish; kingfishers, squirrels, langurs; elephants bathed by mahouts.
- Adventure sports: **river rafting, canoeing, rappelling, rock climbing and mountain biking**. **Brahmagiri hills**, rope bridge to **Nisargadhama** island; **Bylakuppe** — India's largest Tibetan settlement, with Buddhist monks in red, ochre and yellow robes.

## Part III: Tea from Assam
- **Pranjol**, a youngster from Assam whose father manages a tea garden, and his classmate **Rajvir** travel by train to Pranjol's home at the **Dhekiabari Tea Estate**.
- A chai-wallah sells tea on the train. Rajvir remarks that over **80 crore cups of tea** are drunk every day throughout the world.
- Rajvir sees **sea of tea bushes** and tells two legends about tea:
  1. **Chinese:** Emperor **Shen Nung** noticed leaves from twigs burning under his pot had fallen into the boiling water and given it a delicious flavour.
  2. **Indian:** **Bodhidharma**, an ancient Buddhist monk, cut off his eyelids to stay awake while meditating; ten tea plants grew from them, whose leaves banished sleep.
- Tea was first drunk in **China (about 2700 BC)**; it came to **Europe in the 16th century** and was drunk more as **medicine** than as a beverage.

=== the-sermon-at-benares ===
## The big idea
**The Sermon at Benares** tells how **Gautama Buddha** taught that **death is universal** and that grief should not make us selfish. Through the story of **Kisa Gotami**, it shows how wisdom helps us accept loss.

> [!KEY] Buddha's life in brief
> - Born a prince, **Siddhartha Gautama**, in northern India (about 563 BC).
> - Lived a sheltered life as a prince and married a princess. At about **twenty-five** he saw a **sick man**, an **aged man**, a **funeral procession** and a **monk begging** for alms.
> - Left his princely life to seek enlightenment; after seven years of wandering he sat under a **peepal tree** (later called the **Bodhi tree**, Tree of Wisdom) and was **enlightened** — he became **the Buddha, the Awakened** or Enlightened One.
> - He preached his **first sermon at Benares** (Varanasi), the holiest bathing place on the Ganges.

## The story of Kisa Gotami
1. Kisa Gotami's **only son** died. Mad with grief, she carried the body from house to house asking for **medicine** to bring him back to life.
2. People thought she had lost her senses. A man sent her to the **Buddha**.
3. The Buddha asked her to bring **a handful of mustard seed** from a house where **no one had lost a child, husband, parent or friend**.
4. At every house people gladly offered mustard seed but said they had lost someone: "the living are few, but the dead are many".
5. Tired and hopeless, she sat by the wayside and watched the lights of the city flicker and go out. She realised that **human life is like these lights** — it burns and is extinguished.
6. She understood that she had been **selfish** in her grief, since **death is common to all**.

## The sermon's teachings
- The life of mortals is **troubled, brief and combined with pain**.
- Everyone who is born must die; just as ripe fruit falls early, mortals are always in danger of death.
- Like **earthen vessels made by a potter**, all lives end in being broken.
- Neither the young nor the old, the foolish nor the wise, can escape death.
- **Weeping and grieving bring no peace**; they only increase pain and harm the body. The wise do not grieve, knowing the terms of the world.
- **Whoever has overcome sorrow will become free from sorrow and be blessed.**

> [!REMEMBER] Similes in the sermon
> Ripe fruits falling, earthen vessels breaking, and city lights flickering out — all show that **death is inevitable**.

> [!MISTAKE] The Buddha did not refuse to help
> He did not trick Kisa Gotami cruelly; his request was a **lesson** to make her discover the truth herself.

## Values and message
- Accept death as a natural part of life.
- Grief turned inward becomes selfishness; sharing others' sorrows brings peace.
- Teaching through experience is more powerful than lecturing.

> [!TIP] Likely questions
> Why did the Buddha ask for mustard seed? What did Kisa Gotami realise? What did the Buddha see that made him leave home? "Kisa Gotami's grief was selfish" — explain. What lesson does the sermon teach?

- Siddhartha → saw sickness, age, death, a monk → enlightenment under the Bodhi tree → first sermon at Benares.
- Kisa Gotami's dead son; mustard seed from a house untouched by death.
- City lights flickering = human lives.
- Death is universal; overcoming sorrow brings peace.

**The Proposal** by Russian writer **Anton Chekhov** is a **one-act farce** (a comedy based on exaggerated, ridiculous situations). === dust-of-snow ===
## The big idea
**Dust of Snow** by the American poet **Robert Frost** shows how a tiny, ordinary moment in nature can lift a sad mood. A crow shakes snow onto the poet, and his gloomy day is saved.

## What happens in the poem
- The poet is standing under a **hemlock tree** in a sad, regretful mood.
- A **crow** sitting on the tree shakes down some **dust of snow** (fine particles of snow) on him.
- This small, unexpected moment changes his **mood** — it gives his heart a change and saves **some part of a day he had rued** (regretted).

The poem has only **two stanzas of four lines** and is written as **one single sentence**, which matches the quick, single moment it describes.

## Symbols and their meaning
| Symbol | Usual meaning | In the poem |
|---|---|---|
| **Crow** | bad luck, sorrow, ill omen | brings joy instead |
| **Hemlock tree** | poisonous plant, death, sadness | the source of the cheering snow |
| **Dust of snow** | cold, winter | freshness, purity, sudden joy |

> [!KEY] The irony
> Frost uses two usually **negative** symbols — the crow and the poisonous hemlock — to bring about a **positive** change. Happiness can come from the most unlikely places.

## Poetic devices
- **Rhyme scheme:** abab cdcd (crow/snow, tree/me; heart/part, mood/rued).
- **Alliteration:** "**h**as **g**iven **m**y **h**eart" → "has ... heart"; "**s**aved **s**ome part".
- **Symbolism:** crow, hemlock, snow.
- **Enjambment:** the sentence runs on across lines.
- **Imagery:** visual image of snow falling from a tree.
- Simple, conversational language; **"rued"** means regretted.

> [!MISTAKE] The poet's mood
> The poem does not say exactly why the poet was sad. Do not invent a reason; write that he was in a **depressed, regretful** mood.

## Message and value points
- Small moments in nature can heal and change our outlook.
- We should not let one bad moment ruin the whole day.
- Joy can come from unexpected, even "unlucky", sources.

> [!TIP] Likely questions
> What is a "dust of snow"? How did it change the poet's mood? Why does Frost use a crow and a hemlock rather than a sparrow and a rose? What is the rhyme scheme? Write the theme in two lines.

## Quick recap

## The big idea
**A Tiger in the Zoo** by **Leslie Norris** contrasts the life of a tiger locked in a zoo cage with the free life he should be living in the forest. It is a powerful poem about **freedom versus captivity**.

## Stanza by stanza
1. **In the cage:** The tiger **stalks** a few steps of his cage, his vivid stripes visible, his pads making **"quiet"** steps. He is full of **quiet rage**.
2. **Where he should be:** He should be **lurking in the shadow**, sliding through **long grass** near the **water hole**, where plump **deer** pass — hunting naturally.
3. **His power in the wild:** He should be **snarling** around houses at the jungle's edge, baring his white fangs and claws, **terrorising the village**.
4. **Reality:** But he is **locked in a concrete cell**, his strength behind bars, stalking the length of his cage and **ignoring visitors**.
5. **At night:** He hears the last voice at night, the **patrolling cars**, and stares with his brilliant eyes at the **brilliant stars** — longing for freedom.

> [!KEY] Structure based on contrast
> Stanzas 1, 4 and 5 = **captivity** (cage, concrete cell, bars). Stanzas 2 and 3 = **freedom in the wild** (shadow, long grass, water hole, jungle).

## Poetic devices
- **Rhyme scheme:** **abcb** in each stanza — only lines 2 and 4 rhyme (for example, cage/rage in stanza 1).
- **Alliteration:** "**s**liding through long grass"; "**b**rilliant eyes ... **b**rilliant stars"; "**q**uiet rage".
- **Transferred epithet:** "**quiet rage**" — rage is shown as quiet because he is helpless; "**velvet quiet**" — softness of pads transferred to the quiet.
- **Oxymoron/contrast:** "quiet rage".
- **Enjambment:** lines run into each other, especially in stanza 2.
- **Imagery:** vivid visual images of the jungle and the cage.
- **Repetition:** "brilliant" eyes and stars.
- **Personification** of the tiger as having human feelings of rage and longing.

> [!MISTAKE] The tiger is not happy or tamed
> He "ignores visitors" — this shows anger and indifference, not contentment.

## Theme and message
- Wild animals belong in the wild; caging them denies their nature.
- Zoos may protect animals, but they take away freedom and dignity.
- The poem raises awareness about **wildlife conservation** in natural habitats.

=== how-to-tell-wild-animals ===
## The big idea
**How to Tell Wild Animals** by **Carolyn Wells** is a **humorous** poem that pretends to teach us how to identify wild animals — but every method involves meeting the animal at very close, dangerous range. The fun lies in its **wordplay** and absurd situations.

## Animals and how to "tell" them
| Animal | How to recognise it (humorously) |
|---|---|
| **Asian Lion** | a large, tawny beast that **roars at you as you are dying** |
| **Bengal Tiger** | a noble wild beast with **black stripes on a yellow ground** that "greets" you while you roam — just notice if he eats you |
| **Leopard** | a beast whose hide is **peppered with spots**; you will know it as soon as it has **leapt on you** |
| **Bear** | a creature in your yard who **hugs you very, very hard** |
| **Hyena** | it comes with a **merry smile** |
| **Crocodile** | told apart from the hyena because it **weeps** (sheds false tears) |
| **Chameleon** | a creature with **no ears and no wings** that sits on a tree; if you **see nothing** on the tree, it is the chameleon (it changes colour) |

> [!KEY] Where the humour comes from
> To "tell" an animal you must already be attacked by it — which means it is too late! The playful tone makes deadly danger funny.

## Poetic devices
- **Rhyme scheme:** **ababcc** in each six-line stanza.
- **Poetic licence:** the poet bends spelling and grammar for rhyme and rhythm — "**dyin'**" to rhyme with "**Lion**", and "**lept**" instead of "leapt".
- **Pun / wordplay:** "**tell**" = identify (and also "tell apart"); the deadly "**hug**" of the bear; the hyena's "merry smile".
- **Idiom:** "**crocodile tears**" (false sorrow).
- **Imagery** and **humour** throughout; **enjambment**.

> [!MISTAKE] Not a science lesson
> The poem is not a real guide to identifying animals. Write that it is **humorous** and full of **wordplay**.

## Theme and value points
- Humour can make even frightening subjects enjoyable.
- Language is playful — puns and idioms can carry double meanings.
- Wild animals are dangerous; respect them from a distance.

=== the-ball-poem ===
## The big idea
In **The Ball Poem**, **John Berryman** describes a boy who loses his ball in the harbour. Through this small loss the poet explores a big idea: learning to cope with **loss** is a part of **growing up**.

## What happens
- A boy's **ball** bounces down the street and **into the water** of a harbour. He stands rigid, staring down into the harbour in **shaking grief**.
- The poet says it is **no use** giving him **another ball** or saying "there are other balls" — the boy is grieving for **this** ball, linked to his happy childhood days.
- The poet does not intrude; he wants the boy to learn on his own.
- The boy is learning the **"epistemology of loss"** — the knowledge (nature) of loss — how to stand up and bear it.
- **"Money is external"**: money can buy another ball, but it cannot buy back memories or the feelings attached to things lost.
- Every man must eventually lose things; the boy is learning what every person must learn — to **accept loss and move on**.

> [!KEY] Ball = childhood
> The ball symbolises the boy's **childhood, innocence and memories**. Losing it is his first lesson in the permanence of loss.

## Poetic devices
- **Free verse** — no regular rhyme scheme or metre (some irregular rhymes like ball/all).
- **Symbolism:** the ball (childhood, possessions, memories).
- **Imagery:** the ball bouncing merrily down the street and into the water.
- **Repetition:** "merrily bouncing, down the street, and then merrily over".
- **Rhetorical question:** "What is the boy now, who has lost his ball, what, what is he to do?"
- **Enjambment** throughout.

> [!MISTAKE] The poet does not console the boy
> The poet deliberately **does not** offer him another ball; he believes the boy must learn responsibility and acceptance by himself.

## Message and value points
- Loss is inevitable; maturity means accepting it.
- Material things can be replaced, but emotional value cannot be bought.
- Growing up involves learning responsibility for what we possess.

=== the-trees ===
## The big idea
**The Trees** by **Adrienne Rich** describes potted or indoor trees moving out of a house back into the **empty forest**. On the surface it is about nature reclaiming its space; at a deeper level it is about **freedom** — especially of women breaking out of confinement.

## Stanza-wise summary
1. **The empty forest:** The trees inside the house are **moving out into the forest**, which has been empty all these days — no bird could sit there, no insect could hide, the sun could not "bury its feet in shadow". By morning, the forest will be full again.
2. **The struggle to escape:** All night the **roots** work to disengage themselves from the cracks in the veranda floor; the **leaves** strain toward the glass; small **twigs** are stiff with exertion; the long-cramped **boughs** shuffle under the roof **like newly discharged patients**, half-dazed, moving to the clinic doors.
3. **The indifferent poet:** The poet sits inside with the doors open to the veranda, **writing long letters** in which she scarcely mentions the departure of the forest from the house. The night is fresh, the whole moon shines in a sky still open, and the smell of leaves and lichen reaches like a voice into the rooms.
4. **The escape:** Her head is full of whispers which tomorrow will be silent. She hears the **glass breaking** as the trees stumble forward into the night. The **wind rushes to meet them**. The **moon is broken like a mirror**, and its pieces flash in the crown of the tallest oak.

> [!KEY] Two levels of meaning
> - **Literal:** trees kept indoors by humans long for their natural home; without trees the forest is lifeless.
> - **Symbolic:** the trees stand for **women** confined to the home by a patriarchal society, struggling to break free and claim their place in the world.

- **Personification:** trees moving out, roots working, twigs stiff with exertion, boughs shuffling, wind rushing to meet them.
- **Simile:** "**like newly discharged patients**"; "the moon is broken **like a mirror**"; smell reaching "**like a voice**".
- **Metaphor:** "sun bury its feet in shadow".
- **Imagery:** visual (moon, glass breaking), olfactory (smell of leaves and lichen), auditory (glass breaking, whispers).
- **Symbolism:** trees (women/freedom), house (confinement), forest (freedom), broken moon (shattered old order).
- **Free verse** with **enjambment**.

> [!MISTAKE] The poet does not help or stop the trees
> She stays indoors writing letters and barely mentions the departure — this shows **detachment** or perhaps her own silent wish for freedom.

- Nature needs its freedom; cutting trees or caging nature makes the earth barren.

=== for-anne-gregory ===
## The big idea
**For Anne Gregory** by the Irish poet **W.B. Yeats** is a dialogue between the poet and a young girl with beautiful **yellow hair**. It explores whether people love us for our **outer beauty** or our **inner self** — and suggests that only **God** loves us for ourselves alone.

> [!KEY] Background
> Anne Gregory was the granddaughter of **Lady Gregory**, a close friend and patron of Yeats. Yeats wrote the poem for her when she was a young girl.

## Stanza by stanza
1. **The poet:** No young man, thrown into despair by those **"great honey-coloured ramparts at your ear"** (her beautiful golden hair), will ever love you for yourself alone and **not your yellow hair**.
2. **Anne's reply:** She says she can **dye her hair** — **brown, black or carrot** — so that young men will love her for herself alone and not her yellow hair.
3. **The poet again:** He heard an **old religious man** declare that he had found a text to prove that **only God** could love her for herself alone and not her yellow hair.

## Themes
- **Inner vs outer beauty:** People are usually attracted first by appearance.
- **True love is rare:** Unconditional love for the soul belongs only to God.
- **Youthful idealism vs mature wisdom:** Anne believes she can change people's attitude by changing her hair; the poet knows human nature does not change.

## Poetic devices
- **Dialogue form:** the poem is a conversation between the poet and Anne.
- **Metaphor:** **"honey-coloured ramparts"** — her hair is like the walls (ramparts) of a fort guarding her face/ears; its colour is like honey.
- **Rhyme scheme:** **abcbdb** in each stanza (despair/ear/hair rhyme on lines 2, 4 and 6).
- **Repetition** (refrain): "for yourself alone / And not your yellow hair".
- **Alliteration:** "**y**our **y**ellow hair"; "**y**oung men".
- **Synecdoche:** hair stands for physical beauty as a whole.

> [!MISTAKE] Anne's hair colours
> She offers to dye her hair **brown, black or carrot** — not grey or red. And it is an **old religious man** who found the text, not the poet himself.

## Message and value points
- Judge people by their character and inner qualities, not looks.
- Human love is often conditional; only divine love is unconditional.

=== tenses-and-modals ===
## The big idea
**Tenses** tell us **when** an action happens; **modals** tell us the speaker's **attitude** to an action — ability, permission, possibility, obligation and so on. In the board exam, these appear in **gap-filling**, **editing** and **omission** questions, so you need to spot the right form quickly.

## The twelve tenses at a glance
| | Simple | Continuous | Perfect | Perfect Continuous |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Present** | She **writes** | She **is writing** | She **has written** | She **has been writing** |
| **Past** | She **wrote** | She **was writing** | She **had written** | She **had been writing** |
| **Future** | She **will write** | She **will be writing** | She **will have written** | She **will have been writing** |

## When to use each tense
**Present tense**
- **Simple present:** habits, general truths, timetables. *The sun **rises** in the east. The train **leaves** at 6 p.m.*
- **Present continuous:** actions happening now or planned soon. *They **are playing** cricket. I **am meeting** him tomorrow.*
- **Present perfect:** a past action with a result now; with *just, already, yet, ever, never, since, for*. *I **have finished** my homework. She **has lived** here since 2015.*
- **Present perfect continuous:** an action that began in the past and is still going on. *It **has been raining** for two hours.*

**Past tense**
- **Simple past:** completed action at a definite past time. *Mandela **became** President in 1994.*
- **Past continuous:** an action in progress in the past, often interrupted. *I **was reading** when the lights **went** out.*
- **Past perfect:** the earlier of two past actions. *The train **had left** before we **reached** the station.*
- **Past perfect continuous:** an action continuing up to a point in the past. *He **had been waiting** for an hour when the bus came.*

**Future tense**
- **Simple future:** *I **will help** you.*
- **Future continuous:** *This time tomorrow, we **will be flying** to Delhi.*
- **Future perfect:** *By next June, she **will have completed** the course.*

> [!FORMULA] Quick patterns
> Continuous = **be + verb-ing**. Perfect = **have/has/had + past participle (V3)**. Perfect continuous = **have/has/had + been + verb-ing**.

> [!MISTAKE] "Since" vs "for"
> **Since** + a point of time (since Monday, since 2010). **For** + a length of time (for three days, for ten years).

> [!MISTAKE] No present perfect with a definite past time
> ✗ *I have seen him yesterday.* ✓ *I **saw** him yesterday.*

## Subject–verb agreement reminders
- Singular subject → singular verb: *Each of the boys **has** a book.*
- *Neither… nor / Either… or*: the verb agrees with the nearer subject. *Neither the teacher nor the students **were** present.*
- Uncountable nouns take singular verbs: *The news **is** good. Furniture **is** expensive.*

## Modals
Modals are helping verbs followed by the **base form** of the verb (no -s, -ed, -ing). *She can **swim**.* (not *can swims*)

| Modal | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **can** | ability, informal permission | *I can speak French. Can I go out?* |
| **could** | past ability, polite request, possibility | *She could run fast as a child. Could you help me?* |
| **may** | formal permission, possibility, wish | *May I come in? It may rain. May you live long!* |
| **might** | weaker possibility | *He might come late.* |
| **must** | strong obligation, necessity, strong deduction | *You must wear a helmet. He must be tired.* |
| **mustn't** | prohibition | *You mustn't smoke here.* |
| **should / ought to** | advice, moral duty | *You should respect elders. We ought to help the poor.* |
| **will / shall** | future, promise, determination | *I will help you. Shall we start?* |
| **would** | polite request, past habit, conditional | *Would you pass the salt? He would walk daily.* |
| **need (not)** | necessity / absence of necessity | *You needn't hurry.* |
| **dare** | courage | *How dare you!* |
| **used to** | past habit | *I used to live in Pune.* |

> [!KEY] "Must not" vs "need not"
> *You **must not** go* = you are forbidden. *You **need not** go* = it is not necessary, but you may.

> [!EXAMPLE] Gap-filling (board style)
> The doctor told him that he ___ (advice) eat less sugar. → **should**
> ___ I borrow your pen, please? (formal permission) → **May**
> She ___ (ability, past) read when she was four. → **could**
> The ground is wet; it ___ (deduction) have rained. → **must**

> [!EXAMPLE] Editing with tenses
> *Yesterday I go to the market and buy apples.* → *Yesterday I **went** to the market and **bought** apples.*
> *He is working here since 2019.* → *He **has been working** here since 2019.*

> [!TIP] Exam strategy
> Look for **time markers** (yesterday, since, by next year, now) to choose the tense, and for the **function** (advice, permission, obligation) to choose the modal. Keep the tense consistent in a paragraph unless time changes.

## Quick recap
- 12 tenses: present/past/future × simple/continuous/perfect/perfect continuous.
- Since = point of time; for = period.
- Modals + base verb; can/could (ability), may/might (permission/possibility), must (obligation/deduction), should/ought to (advice), need not (no necessity).

=== reported-speech ===
## The big idea
**Direct speech** gives a speaker's exact words in quotation marks. **Reported (indirect) speech** reports the meaning in our own words, without quotes. When the **reporting verb** is in the past tense, we usually shift **tenses, pronouns, and time and place words**.

> [!EXAMPLE] Direct vs reported
> Direct: Lencho said, "I need a hundred pesos."
> Reported: Lencho said that **he needed** a hundred pesos.

## Rule 1: Tense changes (when the reporting verb is past)
| Direct | Reported |
|---|---|
| simple present (*do*) | simple past (*did*) |
| present continuous (*is doing*) | past continuous (*was doing*) |
| present perfect (*has done*) | past perfect (*had done*) |
| simple past (*did*) | past perfect (*had done*) |
| will | would |
| can | could |
| may | might |
| shall | should |
| must | had to (or must) |

**No change** when: the reporting verb is in the present/future (*He says, "I am tired" → He says that he is tired*), or the reported words state a **universal truth / habitual fact** (*The teacher said, "The earth revolves round the sun" → The teacher said that the earth revolves round the sun*).

## Rule 2: Pronouns
Change pronouns according to who is speaking and who is listening. *She said to me, "**I** like **your** dress." → She told me that **she** liked **my** dress.*

## Rule 3: Time and place words
| Direct | Reported |
|---|---|
| now | then |
| today | that day |
| tomorrow | the next day / the following day |
| yesterday | the previous day / the day before |
| this / these | that / those |
| here | there |
| ago | before |
| next week | the following week |

## Rule 4: Statements
Reporting verb: **said / told** (+ object), connector **that**.
- *He said, "I am going to Goa." → He **said that** he **was** going to Goa.*
- *She said to me, "I will call you tomorrow." → She **told me that** she **would** call me **the next day**.*

## Rule 5: Questions
- Reporting verb: **asked / enquired / wanted to know**.
- The question becomes a **statement order** (subject before verb); remove the question mark.
- **Yes/No questions** → use **if / whether**. *He said to me, "Are you ready?" → He asked me **if I was** ready.*
- **Wh- questions** → keep the **wh-word**. *She said, "Where do you live?" → She asked **where I lived**.*

> [!MISTAKE] Don't keep question order
> ✗ *He asked where was I going.* ✓ *He asked where **I was** going.*

## Rule 6: Commands and requests (imperatives)
- Reporting verb: **ordered, commanded, told, requested, advised, begged, warned**.
- Use **to + base verb**; for negative, **not to + base verb**.
- *The teacher said, "Sit down." → The teacher **ordered** us **to sit** down.*
- *She said, "Please help me." → She **requested** me **to help** her.*
- *Mother said, "Don't touch the stove." → Mother **warned** me **not to touch** the stove.*

## Rule 7: Exclamations and wishes
- Reporting verb: **exclaimed with joy/sorrow/surprise, wished, prayed**.
- *He said, "What a beautiful sight!" → He **exclaimed with wonder** that it was a very beautiful sight.*
- *They said, "Good morning, sir." → They **wished** him good morning.*

> [!EXAMPLE] Board-style dialogue to report
> Valli: "How much is the fare to town?" Conductor: "Thirty paise."
> → Valli **asked** the conductor **how much the fare to town was**. The conductor **replied that it was** thirty paise.

> [!TIP] Exam strategy
> In CBSE "reported speech in context" questions, read the dialogue, identify the **type** of sentence (statement/question/command), choose a suitable **reporting verb**, then change tense, pronoun and time words in that order. Write the complete sentence.

## Quick recap
- Past reporting verb → shift tense back one step; will → would, can → could.
- Change pronouns, time and place words (now → then, tomorrow → the next day).
- Statements: said/told that. Questions: asked + if/whether or wh-word, statement order. Commands: ordered/requested + (not) to + verb.
- No tense change for universal truths or present reporting verbs.

=== letter-writing ===
## The big idea
In the CBSE Class 10 exam you write one **formal letter** (about **100–120 words**, 5 marks) — a letter of **complaint**, **enquiry**, **placing an order**, or a **letter to the editor**. Marks are given for **format**, **content**, **organisation** and **accuracy**, so a correct layout alone can earn you easy marks.

## The standard format

> [!FORMULA] Formal letter layout (top to bottom, all left-aligned)
> 1. **Sender's address** (in the exam use "Examination Hall" or the given address)
> 2. **Date** (e.g. 18 October 20XX)
> 3. **Receiver's designation and address** (The Manager, ABC Books, …)
> 4. **Subject:** one line, to the point
> 5. **Salutation:** Sir / Madam (or Dear Sir / Madam)
> 6. **Body:** 3 paragraphs — introduction (purpose), details, conclusion (request/action expected)
> 7. **Complimentary close:** Yours faithfully / Yours sincerely
> 8. **Signature, name** (and designation if given)

> [!MISTAKE] Use the name given in the question
> If the question says "You are Rohan/Rohini of 12, MG Road", sign with **that name and address** — do not write your own. Also, never write "Yours faithfully" with a comma error or "Your's".

## Language tips
- Be **polite, formal and concise**; no slang, contractions or emotional outbursts.
- Start with the purpose: *I am writing to draw your attention to… / I wish to enquire about… / I would like to place an order for…*
- End with action: *I hope you will take prompt action. / I look forward to an early reply.*
- Use passive and formal phrases: *It has been observed that…; Kindly…; I would be grateful if…*

## Types of formal letters
**1. Letter of complaint** — to a company, shop or official about a defective product, poor service or civic problem. Include: what was bought/what the problem is, when, bill/order number, the inconvenience caused, and what you want (replacement, refund, repair, action).

**2. Letter of enquiry** — to ask for information (courses, tours, admissions, products). Include: what you want to know in clear points (fees, duration, eligibility, facilities) and a request for a brochure/early reply.

**3. Letter placing an order** — to a supplier. Include: a **table/list** of items with quantity and specifications, mode of payment, delivery date and address, discount if any, and a request to send items in good condition.

**4. Letter to the editor** — to raise public awareness about an issue (stray animals, noise pollution, water-logging). Begin with *Through the columns of your esteemed newspaper, I wish to…*; describe the problem, causes, effects and suggestions.

> [!EXAMPLE] Model letter of complaint
> Examination Hall
> ABC Nagar, Delhi
> 18 October 20XX
>
> The Manager
> Smart Electronics
> Karol Bagh, New Delhi
>
> **Subject: Complaint regarding a defective water purifier**
>
> Sir
>
> I am writing to complain about the water purifier (Model AquaPure X2, Bill No. 4512) that I purchased from your store on 5 October 20XX.
>
> Within a week of installation, the purifier began leaking and the filtered water has an unpleasant smell. Despite two calls to your service centre, no technician has visited. This has caused great inconvenience, as my family now has to buy bottled water.
>
> As the product is under warranty, I request you to replace it or refund the amount at the earliest. A copy of the bill is enclosed. I hope for a prompt response.
>
> Yours faithfully
> Rohan


> [!EXAMPLE] Opening lines to remember
> - Enquiry: *I came across your advertisement in The Hindu dated… offering a summer course in robotics. I would be grateful if you could provide the following details.*
> - Order: *I would like to place an order for the following books for our school library.*
> - Editor: *Through the columns of your esteemed daily, I wish to draw the attention of the authorities towards the menace of stray dogs in our locality.*

> [!TIP] Scoring checklist
> Correct format (with subject) ✓ · Purpose in the first line ✓ · All details from the question used ✓ · Three clear paragraphs ✓ · Polite closing with action requested ✓ · 100–120 words ✓

## Quick recap
- Sender's address → date → receiver's address → subject → salutation → body (3 paras) → Yours faithfully → name.
- Complaint: problem + details + demand. Enquiry: list of questions. Order: list of items + delivery terms. Editor: issue + effects + suggestions.
- Formal, concise and polite; use the name and address given.

=== analytical-paragraph ===
## The big idea
An **analytical paragraph** (about **100–120 words**, 5 marks) describes and interprets information given as a **chart, graph, table or cue points**. You do not give your personal opinion; you **present the facts clearly, compare them and draw a conclusion** from the data.

## Structure

> [!FORMULA] Analytical paragraph template
> 1. **Title** (optional but useful): e.g. *Growth of Internet Users in India (2015–2023)*
> 2. **Introduction (1–2 sentences):** what the chart shows, the period and the units. *The bar graph illustrates the number of… between… and…*
> 3. **Body (3–5 sentences):** the **overall trend**, the **highest and lowest** values, **comparisons** and any **sudden change**, with exact figures.
> 4. **Conclusion (1–2 sentences):** summarise the main trend or a reasonable inference from the data.

## Useful vocabulary
| Purpose | Words and phrases |
|---|---|
| Introduce | The graph/chart/table **depicts, illustrates, shows, presents, compares** |
| Rise | rose, increased, grew, climbed, surged, soared, a sharp/steady/gradual rise |
| Fall | fell, declined, dropped, decreased, plunged, a significant/slight decline |
| No change | remained stable/constant, levelled off, plateaued |
| Up and down | fluctuated |
| Peak/low | reached a peak of, hit a low of |
| Compare | while, whereas, in contrast, compared to, twice as many as, the same as |
| Proportion | a quarter, one-third, nearly half, the majority, a small minority |
| Conclude | Overall, In conclusion, It is evident that, The data clearly indicate |

> [!MISTAKE] Don't list every number
> Group the data and pick out the **key features**. A paragraph that just reads out every bar like a list loses marks for analysis.

> [!MISTAKE] Keep tenses consistent
> Use the **past tense** for completed periods (*rose from 20% in 2015 to 45% in 2020*) and the **present** for what the chart shows (*The graph shows…*). Avoid personal opinions like *I think*.

> [!EXAMPLE] Model analytical paragraph
> **Question:** The bar graph shows the percentage of students in a school using different modes of transport in 2023: school bus 40%, cycle 25%, private car 20%, walking 10%, public transport 5%.
>
> **Answer — Modes of Transport Used by Students (2023)**
> The bar graph illustrates the percentage of students of a school using various modes of transport in 2023. The **school bus** was clearly the most popular choice, used by **40%** of students. **Cycling** came second at **25%**, followed by **private cars** at **20%**. In contrast, only **10%** of students **walked** to school, and **public transport** was the least preferred, at a mere **5%**. Thus, eco-friendly modes like cycling and walking together accounted for over one-third of students. Overall, it is evident that most students rely on the school bus, and there is scope to encourage more students to cycle or use public transport.

> [!EXAMPLE] Describing a line graph (trend sentences)
> *Sales of electric scooters **rose steadily** from 2,000 units in 2019 to 8,000 in 2021, **surged** to 20,000 in 2022 and then **levelled off** in 2023.*
> *The literacy rate in the state **increased from 60% to 78%** over two decades — a **rise of 18 percentage points**.*

> [!TIP] Scoring checklist
> Clear introduction naming the chart and period ✓ · Overall trend ✓ · Highest and lowest with figures ✓ · Comparisons using linkers ✓ · Logical conclusion ✓ · No opinions ✓ · 100–120 words ✓

## Quick recap
- Introduce → trend and key figures → comparisons → conclusion.
- Use trend verbs (rose, declined, fluctuated, peaked) and linkers (whereas, in contrast).
- Be factual and selective; don't copy every number; no "I think".
