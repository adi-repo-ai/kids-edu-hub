=== the-rise-of-nationalism-in-europe ===
## The big idea
Before the 1800s, Europe was not a map of "nation-states". It was a patchwork of kingdoms, duchies and multi-ethnic empires ruled by dynasties. People felt loyal to a king or a region, not to a "nation". This chapter shows how the idea that **people who share a language, culture and history should have their own state** spread across Europe in the nineteenth century, and how it built new countries like **Italy** and **Germany**.

> [!KEY] Nation-state
> A state in which the majority of citizens (not only rulers) develop a sense of **common identity and shared history or descent**. This identity is created through struggles, the actions of leaders and common people.

In 1848 the French artist **Frédéric Sorrieu** made a set of four prints showing his dream of a world of "democratic and social republics". Peoples of Europe and America march past the Statue of Liberty, offering homage; the shattered symbols of absolutism lie in the dust. It is a picture of the hope this chapter is about.

## The French Revolution and the idea of the nation
The first clear expression of nationalism came with the **French Revolution of 1789**. Sovereignty moved from the monarch to the **people**. The revolutionaries built a collective identity through:

- the ideas of **la patrie** (the fatherland) and **le citoyen** (the citizen)
- a new **tricolour flag** replacing the royal standard
- the **Estates General** renamed the **National Assembly**, elected by active citizens
- new hymns, oaths and martyrs commemorated in the name of the nation
- a centralised administration, uniform laws, abolition of internal customs duties, and a **uniform system of weights and measures**
- **French** promoted as the common language; regional dialects discouraged

**Napoleon** destroyed democracy in France but made administration rational and efficient. His **Civil Code of 1804** (the **Napoleonic Code**) abolished privileges based on birth, established equality before the law and secured the right to property. He simplified administrative divisions, abolished the feudal system and freed peasants from serfdom and manorial dues. In towns, guild restrictions were removed, and transport and communication improved. But soon people in conquered areas turned hostile because of higher taxes, censorship and forced conscription into French armies.

## The making of nationalism in Europe
In the early 1800s, the **aristocracy** was socially and politically dominant, while a growing **middle class** of industrialists, businessmen and professionals pushed for national unity.

> [!KEY] Liberalism
> From the Latin *liber*, meaning free. For the middle classes it meant **freedom for the individual and equality before the law**, government by consent, a constitution and representative government. Economically it meant **freedom of markets** and abolition of state restrictions on movement of goods and capital.

In 1834 a customs union or **Zollverein** was formed at the initiative of **Prussia** and joined by most German states. It abolished tariff barriers and reduced the number of currencies from over thirty to two. The railways further stimulated a sense of economic national unity.

**A new conservatism after 1815.** After Napoleon's defeat in 1815, European governments followed **conservatism**, believing that traditional institutions (monarchy, Church, social hierarchies, property, family) should be preserved. At the **Congress of Vienna (1815)**, hosted by the Austrian Chancellor **Duke Metternich**, the Treaty of Vienna undid most changes of the Napoleonic wars: the Bourbon dynasty was restored in France, and states were set up on France's borders to prevent future expansion. Censorship laws controlled newspapers and books.

**The revolutionaries.** Secret societies trained revolutionaries. **Giuseppe Mazzini**, born in Genoa in 1807, founded **Young Italy** in Marseilles and **Young Europe** in Berne. Metternich called him "the most dangerous enemy of our social order".

**The age of revolutions (1830–1848).**
- **July 1830**: in France, the Bourbon kings were overthrown and a constitutional monarchy with **Louis Philippe** at its head was installed. Metternich once remarked, "When France sneezes, the rest of Europe catches cold."
- **Greek war of independence (from 1821)**: Greece had been part of the **Ottoman Empire**. Poets and artists hailed Greece as the cradle of European civilisation; the English poet **Lord Byron** died in the war in 1824. The **Treaty of Constantinople (1832)** recognised Greece as an independent nation.

**The romantic imagination.** **Romanticism** was a cultural movement that used art, poetry, stories and music to build a feeling of a shared collective heritage. The German philosopher **Johann Gottfried Herder** claimed that true German culture was to be discovered among the common people, *das volk*, through folk songs, folk poetry and folk dances. The **Grimm brothers** collected folktales. In **Poland**, partitioned among Russia, Prussia and Austria, nationalism was kept alive through music and language; **Karol Kurpinski** celebrated the national struggle through operas and polonaise and mazurka folk dances. Using Polish in Church gatherings became a symbol of struggle against Russian dominance.

**Hunger, hardship and 1848.** The 1830s were years of economic hardship. In 1845, weavers in **Silesia** revolted against contractors who had drastically reduced payments. In 1848, food shortages and unemployment brought Paris onto the roads; Louis Philippe fled, and a republic was proclaimed with suffrage to all adult males above 21.

**The 1848 revolution of the liberals.** In the German regions, elected representatives met in the **Frankfurt Parliament** on **18 May 1848** in the Church of St Paul: 831 representatives drafted a constitution for a German nation headed by a monarchy subject to a parliament. When they offered the crown to **Friedrich Wilhelm IV, King of Prussia**, he rejected it, and the parliament was dissolved. Its social base was mostly middle class, it resisted workers' and women's demands (women were only allowed to watch from the visitors' gallery), so it lost popular support.

## Unification of Germany and Italy
> [!KEY] Germany: "blood and iron" (1871)
> After 1848, nationalism moved away from democracy and revolution. **Prussia** took leadership of German unification. Its chief minister **Otto von Bismarck** carried out the process with the help of the Prussian army and bureaucracy. **Three wars over seven years**, with **Austria, Denmark and France**, ended in Prussian victory. In **January 1871**, the Prussian king **William I** was proclaimed **German Emperor** in a ceremony at **Versailles** (the Hall of Mirrors).

> [!KEY] Italy (1861)
> Italy was divided into **seven states**; only **Sardinia-Piedmont** was ruled by an Italian princely house. The north was under the Austrian Habsburgs, the centre under the Pope, and the south under the Bourbon kings of Spain.
> - **Mazzini** had tried to build a unitary Italian republic (Young Italy), but uprisings in 1831 and 1848 failed.
> - **King Victor Emmanuel II** of Sardinia-Piedmont took the lead. Chief Minister **Count Cavour** made a diplomatic alliance with France and defeated the Austrian forces in 1859.
> - In **1860**, **Giuseppe Garibaldi** led his volunteers, the **Red Shirts**, into South Italy and the **Kingdom of the Two Sicilies**, driving out the Spanish rulers.
> - In **1861**, **Victor Emmanuel II** was proclaimed **king of united Italy**.

**The strange case of Britain.** In Britain, nation-state formation was not a sudden upheaval but a long process. The **Act of Union (1707)** between England and Scotland created the United Kingdom of Great Britain, in effect letting England dominate Scotland. **Ireland** was forcibly incorporated in **1801**. A new "British nation" was forged by propagating a dominant English culture: the British flag (Union Jack), the national anthem (God Save Our Noble King) and the English language.

Artists personified nations as female figures, giving an abstract idea a concrete form. **Marianne** represented France (red cap, tricolour, cockade) and **Germania** represented Germany.

## Nationalism and imperialism
By the last quarter of the nineteenth century, nationalism became narrow and aggressive. The **Balkans** (modern Romania, Bulgaria, Albania, Greece, Macedonia, Croatia, Bosnia-Herzegovina, Slovenia, Serbia and Montenegro), inhabited by people broadly called **Slavs**, were under the Ottoman Empire. As Ottoman power declined, Balkan states fought one another and big powers (Russia, Germany, England, Austro-Hungary) competed for control. The region became a "**powder keg**", and this rivalry led to the **First World War in 1914**. Anti-imperial movements in colonies later borrowed the idea of the nation-state.

=== nationalism-in-india ===
## The big idea
In India, as in many colonies, modern nationalism grew out of the **anti-colonial movement**. Different groups (peasants, tribals, workers, businessmen, students, women) suffered under British rule in different ways. In the 1920s and 1930s, **Mahatma Gandhi** and the **Congress** tried to forge these groups into one movement. The chapter follows the **Non-Cooperation** and **Civil Disobedience** movements and asks: how did people come to feel they belonged to one nation?

## The First World War, Khilafat and Non-Cooperation
**The war (1914–1918)** created a new economic and political situation:
- Huge rise in **defence expenditure**, financed by war loans and increased taxes; customs duties raised and **income tax** introduced.
- **Prices doubled** between 1913 and 1918, causing extreme hardship.
- **Forced recruitment** of soldiers in villages caused widespread anger.
- In 1918–19 and 1920–21 crops failed, and an **influenza epidemic** followed; 12 to 13 million people died of famine and epidemic.

**The idea of satyagraha.** Gandhi returned to India in **January 1915** from South Africa, where he had fought racist rule with **satyagraha**, which stresses the **power of truth** and the need to search for truth. Without seeking vengeance or being aggressive, a satyagrahi can win the battle through non-violence by appealing to the conscience of the oppressor.

> [!KEY] Gandhi's first satyagrahas in India
> - **1917 Champaran (Bihar)**: inspired peasants to struggle against the oppressive **plantation (indigo)** system.
> - **1917 Kheda (Gujarat)**: supported peasants who could not pay revenue because of crop failure and plague; demanded relaxation of revenue collection.
> - **1918 Ahmedabad**: organised a satyagraha among **cotton mill workers**.

**The Rowlatt Act (1919)** was hurriedly passed through the Imperial Legislative Council despite united opposition of Indian members. It gave the government enormous powers to repress political activities and allowed **detention of political prisoners without trial for two years**. Gandhi launched a nationwide satyagraha, with a hartal on **6 April 1919**.

**Jallianwala Bagh (13 April 1919).** In **Amritsar**, martial law had been imposed and **General Dyer** took command. On the day of **Baisakhi**, a crowd (many from villages unaware of martial law) gathered in the enclosed ground of Jallianwala Bagh. Dyer blocked the exit points and opened fire, killing hundreds. His object was to "produce a moral effect" of terror. Crowds took to the streets in many north Indian towns; the government responded with brutal repression.

**Khilafat.** After the Ottoman Turkey's defeat in the war, there were rumours of a harsh peace treaty on the Ottoman emperor, the spiritual head of the Islamic world (the **Khalifa**). A **Khilafat Committee** was formed in Bombay in **March 1919**, led by the brothers **Muhammad Ali and Shaukat Ali**. Gandhi saw this as a chance to bring Hindus and Muslims together in a unified movement.

At the **Calcutta session (September 1920)** Gandhi convinced leaders of the need for non-cooperation in support of Khilafat and swaraj; at the **Nagpur session (December 1920)** the programme was adopted.

> [!KEY] Why non-cooperation?
> In his book **Hind Swaraj (1909)**, Gandhi declared that British rule was established in India with the cooperation of Indians, and had survived only because of this cooperation. If Indians refused to cooperate, British rule would collapse within a year, and swaraj would come.

**Stages proposed:** surrender of government-awarded titles; boycott of civil services, army, police, courts, legislative councils, schools and foreign goods; then a full civil disobedience campaign if the government used repression.

## Differing strands within the Non-Cooperation Movement (from January 1921)
**In the towns:** the middle class led the way. Students left government schools, headmasters and teachers resigned, lawyers gave up practice. Council elections were boycotted in most provinces except **Madras**, where the **Justice Party** (party of non-Brahmans) contested. Foreign cloth was burnt in bonfires; the **import of foreign cloth halved between 1921 and 1922**, from ₹102 crore to ₹57 crore. The movement slowed because **khadi was more expensive** than mill cloth and **alternative Indian institutions** (schools, courts) were not available quickly enough.

**In the countryside:**
- **Awadh**: peasants were led by **Baba Ramchandra**, a sanyasi who had earlier been to **Fiji** as an indentured labourer. They protested against talukdars and landlords who demanded high rents and begar (forced labour). **Nai-dhobi bandhs** were organised. By October 1920, the **Oudh Kisan Sabha** was set up headed by **Jawaharlal Nehru**, Baba Ramchandra and a few others.
- **Gudem Hills (Andhra Pradesh)**: tribal peasants, whose forest grazing and collecting rights had been closed off, launched a militant guerrilla struggle led by **Alluri Sitaram Raju**, who claimed special powers yet praised Gandhi. He was captured and executed in 1924.
- **Assam plantations**: under the **Inland Emigration Act of 1859**, plantation workers were not allowed to leave the tea gardens without permission. Hearing of the movement, thousands defied the authorities and headed home, believing Gandhi Raj was coming.

> [!REMEMBER] Withdrawal
> In **February 1922**, after a peaceful demonstration turned violent at **Chauri Chaura** (Gorakhpur) and a police station was set on fire, Gandhi called off the movement. Some leaders, **C.R. Das and Motilal Nehru**, then formed the **Swaraj Party** to contest council elections and argue for swaraj inside the councils.

## Towards Civil Disobedience
Two factors shaped Indian politics in the late 1920s: the **worldwide economic depression** (falling agricultural prices from 1926, collapsing after 1930) and the **Simon Commission**. Set up by the Tory government in Britain, the Statutory Commission under **Sir John Simon** arrived in **1928** to look into the functioning of the constitutional system. It had **not a single Indian member**, so it was greeted with the slogan "**Go back Simon**".

In **October 1929**, Viceroy **Lord Irwin** announced a vague offer of "dominion status" and a Round Table Conference. In **December 1929**, the **Lahore Congress** under **Jawaharlal Nehru** formalised the demand for **Purna Swaraj** (complete independence). **26 January 1930** was declared **Independence Day**.

**The Salt March.** Salt was consumed by rich and poor alike, so a tax on it was an oppressive face of British rule. On **31 January 1930**, Gandhi sent Irwin a letter with **eleven demands**, the most stirring being abolition of the salt tax. When Irwin did not respond, Gandhi started his march from **Sabarmati** with **78 volunteers**, walking over **240 miles** in **24 days** to the coastal town of **Dandi**, where on **6 April 1930** he ceremonially violated the law by making salt from boiling seawater.

> [!KEY] How Civil Disobedience differed from Non-Cooperation
> People were now asked **not only to refuse cooperation** with the British, but also to **break colonial laws**: salt law, forest laws, refusal to pay revenue and chaukidari taxes, and picketing of liquor shops.

When **Abdul Ghaffar Khan** (a devout disciple of Gandhi) was arrested in April 1930 and Gandhi himself a month later, there was violence. Gandhi called off the movement and signed the **Gandhi–Irwin Pact on 5 March 1931**, agreeing to attend the **Second Round Table Conference** in London (December 1931). The negotiations broke down; Gandhi returned disappointed and relaunched the movement, but by **1934** it had lost momentum.

## How participants saw the movement
- **Rich peasants** (Patidars of Gujarat, Jats of Uttar Pradesh): hit by falling prices and high revenue; they wanted revenue reduced. They were disappointed when the movement was called off in 1931 without revision of revenue rates.
- **Poor peasants**: wanted unpaid rent to landlords remitted; Congress was unwilling to support no-rent campaigns for fear of upsetting rich peasants and landlords.
- **Business classes**: wanted protection against imports and a rupee–sterling exchange ratio that discouraged imports. **Purshottamdas Thakurdas** and **G.D. Birla** attacked colonial control; they formed the **Indian Industrial and Commercial Congress (1920)** and the **Federation of the Indian Chamber of Commerce and Industries (FICCI, 1927)**. They were worried by the spread of militant activities and the growing influence of socialism.
- **Industrial workers**: barring the Nagpur region, participated only partly; Congress did not include their demands so as not to alienate industrialists.
- **Women**: participated in large numbers in protest marches, salt manufacture and picketing, though Gandhi saw their duty mainly at home.

**Limits of the movement.** **Dalits** ("depressed classes") were ignored by Congress for long. **Dr B.R. Ambedkar** organised them into the **Depressed Classes Association (1930)** and clashed with Gandhi at the Second Round Table Conference by demanding **separate electorates** for dalits. When the British accepted this, Gandhi began a fast unto death. The result was the **Poona Pact of September 1932**, which gave depressed classes **reserved seats** in provincial and central legislative councils, but voted by the general electorate. Many **Muslim** leaders felt alienated after the decline of Non-Cooperation–Khilafat and growing communal clashes. **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** was willing to give up separate electorates if Muslims were assured reserved seats in the Central Assembly and representation in proportion to population in Muslim-dominated provinces (Bengal and Punjab). At the **All Parties Conference in 1928**, **M.R. Jayakar** of the Hindu Mahasabha strongly opposed compromise.

## The sense of collective belonging
Nationalism spreads when people believe they are part of the same nation. This was built through:

- **Images**: the identity of India came to be visually associated with **Bharat Mata**. **Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay** wrote **'Vande Mataram'** in the 1870s as a hymn to the motherland, later included in his novel **Anandamath**. **Abanindranath Tagore** painted Bharat Mata as an ascetic figure, calm, composed, divine and spiritual.
- **Folklore**: nationalists recorded folk tales, songs and legends. **Rabindranath Tagore** collected ballads, nursery rhymes and myths in Bengal; **Natesa Sastri** published *The Folklore of Southern India* in four volumes.
- **Flags**: during the Swadeshi movement in Bengal, a tricolour (**red, green and yellow**) was designed with **eight lotuses** representing eight provinces of British India and a **crescent moon** for Hindus and Muslims. By **1921**, Gandhi designed the Swaraj flag: a tricolour (red, green and white) with a **spinning wheel** in the centre, representing the Gandhian ideal of self-help.
- **Reinterpretation of history**: nationalist writers urged readers to take pride in India's great achievements in the past and struggle to change the miserable conditions under British rule.

=== the-making-of-a-global-world ===
## The big idea
"Globalisation" sounds modern, but people, goods, ideas, germs and money have been moving across the world for thousands of years. This chapter traces that story: from the **silk routes** of the pre-modern world, through the nineteenth-century world economy and the disaster of the **Great Depression**, to the rebuilding after the Second World War.

## The pre-modern world
- **Silk routes**: the name points to the importance of West-bound **Chinese silk** cargoes. Routes linked China, Asia, Europe and northern Africa by land and sea. Chinese pottery, Indian textiles and spices, and precious metals from Europe travelled along them. **Buddhist** preachers, Christian missionaries and later Muslim preachers also used them.
- **Food travels**: noodles may have travelled west from China to become **spaghetti**. Many common foods, such as **potatoes, soya, groundnuts, maize, tomatoes, chillies and sweet potatoes**, came from the Americas after **Columbus** reached there (1492). In Ireland, the poor depended so heavily on potatoes that when a disease destroyed the crop in the mid-1840s, hundreds of thousands died in the **Irish Potato Famine (1845–1849)**.
- **Conquest, disease and trade**: in the sixteenth century, Europeans conquered the Americas. Their most powerful weapon was not guns but germs such as **smallpox**, to which local people had no immunity. Precious metals, especially **silver**, from Peru and Mexico enhanced Europe's wealth.

## The nineteenth century (1815–1914)
Economists identify **three types of flows** in international economic exchanges:
1. **Trade** (mainly in goods like cloth or wheat)
2. **Labour** (migration of people in search of employment)
3. **Capital** (short-term or long-term investments over long distances)

**A world economy takes shape.** In Britain, population growth increased the demand for food grains. Under pressure from landed groups, the government restricted import of corn through the **Corn Laws**. When these were **abolished**, cheaper food was imported; British agriculture could not compete, and people migrated to cities or overseas. Food production expanded in Eastern Europe, Russia, America and Australia, needing railways, ports, and huge investment from London. Nearly **50 million people emigrated** from Europe to America and Australia in the nineteenth century.

**Role of technology.** Railways, steamships and the telegraph were key. **Refrigerated ships** made it possible to transport perishable foods (like meat) over long distances, reducing costs in Europe.

**Late nineteenth-century colonialism.** In **1885** big European powers met in **Berlin** to complete the carving up of Africa. In the 1890s, **rinderpest** (cattle plague) arrived in Africa with infected cattle imported to feed Italian soldiers in Eritrea. It killed 90 per cent of cattle, destroying African livelihoods and forcing Africans into the labour market, which helped colonisers.

> [!KEY] Indentured labour: "a new system of slavery"
> A **bonded labourer under contract** to work for an employer for a specific amount of time, to pay off his passage to a new country or home. In the nineteenth century, hundreds of thousands of Indian and Chinese labourers went to plantations, mines and road and railway projects. Indians went mainly to the **Caribbean islands (Trinidad, Guyana, Surinam), Mauritius and Fiji**, and Ceylon and Malaya. Most came from present-day eastern **Uttar Pradesh, Bihar**, central India and the dry districts of **Tamil Nadu**. Recruitment was by **agents** who often gave false information. Living and working conditions were harsh, with few legal rights. Workers created new cultures: **Hosay** in Trinidad (from Muharram), **Rastafarianism** (made famous by **Bob Marley**), and **'chutney music'** in Trinidad and Guyana. Indentured labour was **abolished in 1921**.

**Indian entrepreneurs abroad**: **Shikaripuri shroffs** and **Nattukottai Chettiars** financed export agriculture in Central and Southeast Asia; Hyderabadi Sindhi traders set up emporia at busy ports.

**India in the world economy.** British cotton manufactures flooded India, while India exported raw cotton, opium (to China) and wheat. Britain had a **trade surplus** with India, which was used to balance its deficits with other countries and to pay **"home charges"** (pensions of British officials, interest on India's external debt).

## The inter-war economy
**The First World War (1914–1918)** was fought between the **Allies** (Britain, France, Russia; later the US) and the **Central Powers** (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Ottoman Turkey). It was the first **modern industrial war**, using machine guns, tanks, aircraft and chemical weapons on a massive scale. Over 9 million died. Britain borrowed heavily from US banks, and the **US turned from an international debtor into an international creditor**.

**Rise of mass production and consumption.** The US economy recovered quickly. **Henry Ford** adapted the assembly line of a Chicago slaughterhouse to his car plant in **Detroit**, producing the **T-Model Ford**. He doubled wages to **$5 a day** and banned trade unions. Mass production lowered costs; hire-purchase (credit) fuelled a boom in cars, refrigerators, washing machines and radios.

> [!KEY] The Great Depression (around 1929 to the mid-1930s)
> Most parts of the world saw catastrophic declines in production, employment, incomes and trade.
> **Causes:** (1) **Agricultural overproduction** led to falling prices; farmers produced more to maintain income, making prices fall further. (2) In the mid-1920s many countries had financed investments through **loans from the US**; when US overseas lending fell sharply after 1928, countries dependent on those loans faced crisis. (3) The US raised import duties, which hurt world trade. Thousands of US banks went bankrupt.

**India and the Depression.** India's **exports and imports nearly halved between 1928 and 1934**. Wheat prices in India fell by **50 per cent** between 1928 and 1934. Peasants and farmers suffered more than urban dwellers because agricultural prices fell sharply while the colonial government refused to reduce revenue demands. India became an exporter of **precious metals, especially gold**, as peasants sold their savings. Urban fixed-income groups (salaried, landowners living on rent) were relatively better off as prices fell.

## Rebuilding a world economy: the post-war era
The **Second World War (1939–1945)** was fought between the **Axis powers** (Nazi Germany, Japan and Italy) and the **Allies** (Britain, France, the Soviet Union and the US). About **60 million people** died.

**Lessons from the inter-war years**: (1) an industrial society cannot be sustained without mass consumption, so governments must keep employment high; (2) a country's economic links with the outside world must be managed.

> [!KEY] Bretton Woods (July 1944)
> The UN Monetary and Financial Conference at **Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, USA** set up the **International Monetary Fund (IMF)** to deal with external surpluses and deficits of member nations, and the **International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD, the World Bank)** to finance post-war reconstruction. They are called the **Bretton Woods twins**. Both began operations in **1947**. The system was based on **fixed exchange rates**: national currencies were pegged to the **US dollar**, and the dollar was anchored to gold at **$35 per ounce**. The US has an effective right of veto over key IMF and World Bank decisions.

The Bretton Woods system brought unprecedented growth of trade and incomes for Western industrial nations and Japan (the "golden age"). **Decolonisation** followed; most developing countries formed the **G-77** to demand a **New International Economic Order (NIEO)**: real control over natural resources, more development aid, fairer prices for raw materials and better access for their manufactured goods.

**End of Bretton Woods and globalisation.** From the 1960s, rising costs of overseas involvement weakened the US dollar; the system of fixed exchange rates collapsed and **floating exchange rates** were introduced. From the late 1970s, MNCs shifted production to low-wage Asian countries; **China** (after its 1949 revolution) became an attractive destination for investment because of its low wages.

> [!MISTAKE] Don't mix up the wars' sides
> WWI: Allies vs **Central Powers**. WWII: Allies vs **Axis** powers. And the IMF and World Bank were set up at Bretton Woods in 1944 but began work in 1947.

=== the-age-of-industrialisation ===
## The big idea
We usually picture industrialisation as giant factories, smoking chimneys and steam engines. But this chapter shows that industrial change was slow and uneven. Even before factories, there was large-scale production for international markets. Hand labour remained important for a long time, and in colonies like India, industrialisation took a different path shaped by colonial rule.

## Before the Industrial Revolution
> [!KEY] Proto-industrialisation
> A phase of **large-scale industrial production for an international market**, which was **not based on factories**. It existed in England and Europe even before factories began.

In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, merchants from towns moved to the **countryside**, supplying money to peasants and artisans to produce for international markets. Why? In towns, powerful **trade and craft guilds** (associations of producers that trained craftspeople, controlled production, regulated competition and prices) and rulers restricted the entry of new people into trade. Poor cottagers in the countryside, who no longer had access to the common lands, welcomed the extra income.

Under this system, a close relationship developed between town and countryside. A merchant clothier in England bought wool from a **wool stapler**, carried it to spinners, then weavers, **fullers** and dyers; finishing was done in London before export. Proto-industrial workers were controlled by merchants but the family farm remained the base.

## The coming up of the factory
- The earliest factories in England came up by the **1730s**, but their number multiplied only in the late eighteenth century.
- **Cotton** was the first symbol of the new era. Its production boomed: in **1760**, Britain imported **2.5 million pounds** of raw cotton; by **1787** this had soared to **22 million pounds**.
- **Richard Arkwright** created the **cotton mill**: costly new machines were set up and managed under one roof, allowing careful supervision of the production process, quality control and regulation of labour.
- **James Watt** improved the steam engine produced by **Newcomen** and patented the new engine in **1781**; his industrialist friend **Matthew Boulton** manufactured the new model.
- By the **1840s**, cotton was the leading sector; after that, **iron and steel** led the way with railway expansion.

> [!MISTAKE] "Factories replaced traditional industry overnight"
> Not true. Even in Britain, the most dynamic industries (cotton and metals) accounted for less than a fifth of workers. **Technological change was slow**: new technology was expensive and machines often broke down. Ordinary and small innovations in non-mechanised sectors (food processing, building, pottery, glass, furniture) were the basis of growth.

## Hand labour and steam power
In Victorian Britain, there was **no shortage of human labour**. Poor peasants and vagrants moved to cities in large numbers in search of jobs. With plenty of labour available at low wages, industrialists had no problem of labour shortage or high wage costs. They did not want machines that needed huge capital investment.

- In many industries the demand for labour was **seasonal** (gas works, breweries, book-binders, printers, shipyards at certain seasons). Industrialists preferred hand labour, employing workers for the season.
- A range of products could be produced only with hand labour; machines produced uniform, standardised goods for a mass market. In Victorian Britain, the **upper classes** (aristocrats and bourgeoisie) **preferred things produced by hand**, symbolising refinement and class; handmade products were better finished and individually designed.
- In countries with labour shortages, like the **USA**, industrialists were keen on mechanised power.

**Life of the workers.** Getting a job depended on networks of friendship and kin. Many job-seekers had to wait weeks, sleeping under bridges or in night shelters. Work was seasonal, and wages rose in the early nineteenth century but prices rose faster, so real wages fell. When the **Spinning Jenny** (devised by **James Hargreaves in 1764**) was introduced in the woollen industry, women who survived on hand spinning **attacked the new machines**, fearing unemployment. After the 1840s, building activity (roads, railway stations, tunnels) opened up new jobs.

## Industrialisation in the colonies
**The age of Indian textiles.** Before machine industries, **silk and cotton goods from India dominated the international textile market**. Armenian and Persian merchants took goods from Punjab to Afghanistan, eastern Persia and Central Asia. Pre-colonial ports included **Surat** on the Gujarat coast (linking to the Gulf and Red Sea ports), **Masulipatnam** on the Coromandel coast, and **Hoogly** in Bengal (trade links with Southeast Asian ports).

By the 1750s this network was breaking down as European companies gained power. **Bombay and Calcutta** grew as new ports, under colonial control.

**What happened to weavers?** After the East India Company established political power (after the 1760s and 1770s), it set out to eliminate competition and gain direct control over weavers:
- It appointed paid servants called **gomasthas** to supervise weavers, collect supplies and examine the quality of cloth.
- It prevented weavers from dealing with other buyers through a system of **advances**; weavers who took loans had to hand over cloth to the gomastha.
- Gomasthas were outsiders who acted arrogantly and punished weavers for delays, often by flogging. In many places in **Carnatic and Bengal**, weavers deserted villages, revolted along with village traders, or refused loans and took up agriculture.

**Manchester comes to India.** In 1772, Henry Patullo, a Company official, said demand for Indian textiles could never reduce. But by the beginning of the nineteenth century, exports of Indian textiles declined. Manchester goods flooded Indian markets: by **1850**, cotton piece-goods formed over **31 per cent** of the value of Indian imports; by the **1870s**, over **50 per cent**. Weavers faced two problems: their **export market collapsed** and the **local market shrank**. By the 1860s, they also faced a **shortage of raw cotton**, because the American Civil War cut off US cotton supplies to Britain, and Britain turned to India.

**Early entrepreneurs** made money through trade with China (opium) and other ventures: **Dwarkanath Tagore** in Bengal; **Dinshaw Petit** and **Jamsetjee Nusserwanjee Tata** in Bombay. Business groups like the **Birlas** traded with China too. Many were squeezed out by European Managing Agencies (Bird Heiglers & Co., Andrew Yule, Jardine Skinner & Co.), which mobilised capital and controlled companies.

**Where did workers come from?** Most came from neighbouring districts; e.g., over 50 per cent of Bombay mill workers in 1911 came from **Ratnagiri**. Industrialists usually employed a **jobber** to get new recruits. The jobber was an old and trusted worker who got people from his village, helped them settle and gave them money in times of crisis, but soon demanded money and gifts and controlled their lives.

**Peculiarities of industrial growth.** Early Indian mills produced **coarse cotton yarn** rather than fabric, exported to China. With the **Swadeshi movement**, nationalists mobilised people to boycott foreign cloth. During the **First World War**, Manchester imports declined and Indian mills supplied war needs (jute bags, army uniforms, tents, leather boots), so new factories came up. After the war, Manchester could never recapture its old position in India.

**Small-scale industries predominate.** Handloom production actually expanded in the twentieth century, helped by new technology like the **fly shuttle** (a mechanical device used for weaving, moved by ropes and pulleys, that increased productivity and made wide cloth possible). By the 1940s, over 35 per cent of handlooms were fitted with fly shuttles in Travancore, Madras, Mysore, Cochin and Bengal.

## Market for goods
Producers used **advertisements** to make products appear necessary and desirable.
- Manchester industrialists put **labels** on cloth bundles; "**Made in Manchester**" in bold indicated quality.
- Labels carried **images of Indian gods and goddesses** (Krishna, Saraswati), to give divine approval and make goods familiar to buyers.
- **Calendars** were printed to popularise products: unlike newspapers and magazines, calendars were used even by people who could not read; they hung in tea shops and homes all year.
- Figures of **emperors and nawabs** suggested royal quality.
- Indian producers used nationalist messages: "if you care for the nation, buy products that Indians produce" (**Swadeshi**).

=== print-culture-and-the-modern-world ===
## The big idea
It is hard to imagine a world without printed material. Yet print is only a few centuries old. This chapter traces the development of print from **East Asia** to **Europe** and **India**, and shows how print changed the way people thought: it spread new ideas, created debates, and helped shape religion, reform, revolution and nationalism.

## The first printed books
- **China**: the earliest kind of print technology was a system of **hand printing**. From **AD 594** onwards, books were printed by rubbing paper against the inked surface of **woodblocks**. Both sides of the thin, porous sheet could not be printed, so the traditional Chinese **'accordion book'** was folded and stitched at the side. China was the major producer of printed material for a long time, because its bureaucracy recruited through **civil service examinations**, which needed textbooks. By the seventeenth century, print diversified: merchants used it, reading became a leisure activity, and rich women began to read and even publish poetry and plays. Shanghai became the hub of new print culture with Western mechanical presses.
- **Japan**: Buddhist missionaries from China introduced hand-printing technology into Japan around **AD 768–770**. The oldest Japanese book, printed in **AD 868**, is the Buddhist **Diamond Sutra**, containing six sheets of text and woodcut illustrations. In late eighteenth-century Edo (later Tokyo), the illustrated work of **Kitagawa Utamaro** contributed to an art form called **ukiyo** ("pictures of the floating world"), depicting ordinary human experiences.

## Print comes to Europe
- In **1295**, **Marco Polo** returned to Italy after many years in China and brought back the knowledge of woodblock printing. Soon Italians produced books with woodblocks, and the technology spread.
- Aristocratic circles and monastic libraries still preferred handwritten manuscripts on expensive **vellum** (a parchment made from animal skin). But hand-copying was expensive, laborious and time-consuming, and manuscripts were fragile, so demand for cheaper books grew.

> [!KEY] Gutenberg and the printing press
> **Johann Gutenberg**, son of a merchant, grew up on a large agricultural estate and saw wine and olive presses. He learnt to polish stones, became a master goldsmith and made lead moulds for trinkets. Using this knowledge, he adapted existing technology to design his innovation: the olive press provided the model for the printing press, and moulds were used to cast metal types of letters. By **1448**, Gutenberg perfected the system. The first book he printed was the **Bible**: about **180 copies** were printed and it took **three years**. Between 1450 and 1550, printing presses were set up in most countries of Europe.

## The print revolution and its impact
The **print revolution** was not just a new way of producing books; it transformed the lives of people, changing their relationship to information and knowledge.

**A new reading public.** Printing reduced the cost of books. Earlier, ordinary people lived in an **oral culture**: they heard sacred texts read out and listened to ballads. Now, a **reading public** emerged. Since literacy was low, publishers printed popular ballads and folk tales with pictures, read aloud at gatherings in villages and taverns. The hearing public and reading public became intermingled.

**Religious debates and the fear of print.** Not everyone welcomed print; many feared that uncontrolled printed words would spread rebellious and irreligious thoughts.
- In **1517**, the religious reformer **Martin Luther** wrote **Ninety-Five Theses** criticising many practices and rituals of the Roman Catholic Church. A printed copy was posted on a church door in Wittenberg. His writings were reproduced in vast numbers; this led to a division within the Church and the **Protestant Reformation**. Luther said, "**Printing is the ultimate gift of God and the greatest one.**"
- **Menocchio**, a sixteenth-century miller in Italy, began reading books available in his locality and reinterpreted the Bible, formulating a view of God and Creation that enraged the Church. He was hauled up twice by the **Roman Catholic Inquisition** and executed. From **1558**, the Church maintained an **Index of Prohibited Books**.

**The reading mania.** By the end of the eighteenth century, literacy rates in parts of Europe were as high as 60 to 80 per cent. Pedlars called **chapmen** sold **chapbooks** (pocket-size books) in England; France had the **Bibliothèque Bleue**, cheap small books printed on poor-quality paper and bound in blue covers. Almanacs, romances, ballads and folk tales spread. Newspapers and journals combined information about current affairs with entertainment.

**"Tremble, therefore, tyrants of the world!"** By the mid-eighteenth century, people believed books could change the world. The French novelist **Louise-Sébastien Mercier** declared: "The printing press is the most powerful engine of progress and public opinion is the force that will sweep despotism away."

> [!KEY] Print culture and the French Revolution: three arguments
> 1. Print **popularised the ideas of Enlightenment thinkers** (Voltaire, Rousseau) who attacked tradition, superstition and despotism, and argued for the rule of reason.
> 2. Print created a **new culture of dialogue and debate**; all values, norms and institutions were re-evaluated.
> 3. By the 1780s, there was an outpouring of literature that **mocked the royalty** and criticised their morality, raising hostility against the monarchy.
> But note: print did not directly shape minds; people read selectively and interpreted ideas in their own way.

**The nineteenth century.** Mass literacy spread as primary education became compulsory in the late nineteenth century. **Children** became an important category of readers; a children's press devoted to literature for children alone was set up in France in 1857, and the **Grimm Brothers** in Germany compiled peasant folk tales. **Women** became important readers and writers; famous novelists included **Jane Austen, the Brontë sisters and George Eliot**. **Lending libraries** (from the seventeenth century) became instruments for educating white-collar workers, artisans and lower-middle-class people; some workers even wrote political tracts and autobiographies. **Technological changes**: by the mid-nineteenth century, **Richard M. Hoe** of New York perfected the power-driven cylindrical press, capable of printing **8,000 sheets per hour**. Publishers sold cheap paperback editions, serialised novels in magazines, and in the 1930s Depression, cheap paperbacks were released.

## Print comes to India
**Manuscripts before print.** India had a rich tradition of handwritten manuscripts in Sanskrit, Arabic, Persian and vernaculars, copied on palm leaves or handmade paper. They were expensive and fragile, and could not be read easily as script was written in different styles.

> [!KEY] Early printing in India
> - The printing press first came to **Goa** with **Portuguese missionaries** in the mid-sixteenth century. Jesuit priests learnt **Konkani** and printed several tracts; by 1674, about 50 books had been printed in Konkani and Kanara.
> - **Catholic priests** printed the first **Tamil book in 1579 at Cochin**, and in **1713** the first **Malayalam** book.
> - By 1710, **Dutch Protestant missionaries** had printed 32 Tamil texts.
> - From **1780**, **James Augustus Hickey** began to edit the **Bengal Gazette**, a weekly magazine that described itself as "a commercial paper open to all, but influenced by none". He published gossip about Company officials; **Governor-General Warren Hastings** persecuted him.
> - The first Indian newspaper was the weekly **Bengal Gazette**, brought out by **Gangadhar Bhattacharya**, who was close to Rammohun Roy.

**Religious reform and public debate.** Print fuelled intense debates over widow immolation, monotheism, Brahmanical priesthood and idolatry.
- **Raja Rammohun Roy** published the **Sambad Kaumudi** from **1821**; Hindu orthodoxy commissioned the **Samachar Chandrika** to oppose his opinions.
- From **1822**, two Persian newspapers, **Jam-i-Jahan Nama** and **Shamsul Akhbar**, were published. The same year, a Gujarati newspaper, the **Bombay Samachar**, was launched.
- The **Deoband Seminary**, founded in **1867**, published thousands of **fatwas** telling Muslim readers how to conduct themselves in everyday life and explaining the meaning of Islamic doctrines.
- The first printed edition of **Ramcharitmanas** of Tulsidas came out from Calcutta in **1810**. By the mid-nineteenth century, cheap lithographic editions flooded north Indian markets; the **Naval Kishore Press at Lucknow** and the **Shri Venkateshwar Press in Bombay** published numerous religious texts in vernaculars.

**New forms of publication.** The **novel** became popular; also lyrics, short stories, essays about social and political matters. **Raja Ravi Varma** produced images for mass circulation. By the 1870s, caricatures and cartoons appeared in journals, commenting on social and political themes.

**Women and print.** Liberal husbands and fathers began educating women at home. **Rashsundari Debi**, a young married girl in an orthodox household, learnt to read secretly in her kitchen and later wrote her autobiography **Amar Jiban**, published in **1876**, the **first full-length autobiography published in the Bengali language**. **Kailashbashini Debi** wrote about women's experiences; in Maharashtra, **Tarabai Shinde** and **Pandita Ramabai** wrote with passionate anger about the miserable lives of upper-caste Hindu women, especially widows.

**Print and the poor.** Very cheap small books were sold at crossroads. Public libraries were set up from the early twentieth century. **Jyotiba Phule**, the Maratha pioneer of "low caste" protest movements, wrote about the injustices of the caste system in his **Gulamgiri (1871)**. In the twentieth century, **B.R. Ambedkar** in Maharashtra and **E.V. Ramaswamy Naicker (Periyar)** in Madras wrote powerfully on caste. **Kashibaba**, a Kanpur mill worker, wrote and published **Chhote Aur Bade Ka Sawal** in **1938** to show the links between caste and class exploitation.

## Print and censorship
- Before 1798, the colonial state under the East India Company was not too concerned with censorship. By the **1820s**, the **Calcutta Supreme Court** passed regulations to control press freedom, and the Company encouraged newspapers that celebrated British rule.
- In **1835**, faced with urgent petitions by editors of English and vernacular newspapers, Governor-General **Bentinck** agreed to revise press laws. **Thomas Macaulay**, a liberal colonial official, formulated new rules that restored earlier freedoms.
- After the **Revolt of 1857**, attitudes changed; enraged Englishmen demanded a clampdown on the "native" press.
- In **1878**, the **Vernacular Press Act** was passed, **modelled on the Irish Press Laws**. It gave the government extensive rights to **censor reports and editorials** in the vernacular press. The government kept regular track; if a report was judged seditious, the newspaper was warned, and if the warning was ignored, the press could be seized and the printing machinery confiscated.
- Nationalist newspapers grew in number across India. When Punjab revolutionaries were deported in **1907**, **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** wrote with great sympathy about them in his **Kesari**; this led to his imprisonment in **1908**, which provoked widespread protests.

> [!MISTAKE] Two "Bengal Gazettes"
> Hickey's Bengal Gazette (1780) was English-run. The first Indian newspaper, also called Bengal Gazette, was brought out by **Gangadhar Bhattacharya**. Don't credit Hickey with being the first Indian newspaper editor.

=== resources-and-development ===
## The big idea
Everything in our environment that can be used to satisfy our needs, provided it is **technologically accessible, economically feasible and culturally acceptable**, is a **resource**. Resources are not free gifts of nature: they become resources only through **human activities**. Because humans have used them greedily, we need planning and conservation, so that development is **sustainable**.

## Types of resources
| Basis | Types | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Origin** | Biotic (from the biosphere, have life) / Abiotic (non-living) | Humans, flora, fauna, fisheries / rocks, metals |
| **Exhaustibility** | Renewable (can be renewed or reproduced) / Non-renewable (take millions of years) | Solar, wind, water, forests / fossil fuels, minerals |
| **Ownership** | Individual, Community, National, International | Plots, houses / village ponds, grazing grounds / roads, canals, minerals / oceanic resources beyond 200 nautical miles |
| **Status of development** | Potential, Developed, Stock, Reserves | see below |

- **Potential resources**: found in a region but not yet utilised (e.g., wind and solar energy in Rajasthan and Gujarat).
- **Developed resources**: surveyed, and their quality and quantity determined for use.
- **Stock**: materials that can satisfy human needs but we lack the appropriate technology to use them (e.g., using **hydrogen** from water as energy).
- **Reserves**: a subset of stock that can be used with existing technology but whose use has not yet started (e.g., water in dams kept for the future).

> [!KEY] Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ)
> India has the right to exploit resources in the ocean up to **200 nautical miles (about 370 km)** from its coast. Oceanic resources beyond this belong to **international institutions**.

## Development of resources and sustainable development
Indiscriminate use of resources has caused (1) **depletion** of resources to satisfy the greed of a few, (2) **accumulation** of resources in a few hands, dividing society into rich and poor, and (3) **global ecological crises** like global warming, ozone layer depletion, pollution and land degradation.

> [!KEY] Sustainable development
> Development should take place **without damaging the environment**, and development in the present should **not compromise the needs of future generations**.

**Rio de Janeiro Earth Summit (June 1992)**: over 100 heads of state met in Brazil to address environmental protection and socio-economic development. They signed the **Declaration on Global Climatic Change and Biological Diversity** and adopted **Agenda 21**, which aims at achieving global sustainable development, combating environmental damage, poverty and disease through global cooperation. Every local government should draw its own local Agenda 21.

Other milestones: the **Club of Rome** advocated resource conservation in **1968**; **Schumacher's** book *Small is Beautiful* (1974) presented Gandhian philosophy; the **Brundtland Commission Report (1987)**, *Our Common Future*, introduced the concept of sustainable development. Gandhiji said: "**There is enough for everybody's need and not for anybody's greed.**"

## Resource planning
India has enormous diversity: some regions are rich in some resources but poor in others. **Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh** are rich in minerals and coal; **Arunachal Pradesh** has abundant water but lacks infrastructure; **Rajasthan** is rich in solar and wind energy but lacks water; **Ladakh** has a rich cultural heritage but is deficient in water, infrastructure and some vital minerals.

**Steps of resource planning:**
1. **Identification and inventory** of resources across regions (surveying, mapping, qualitative and quantitative estimation).
2. **Evolving a planning structure** with appropriate technology, skill and institutions.
3. **Matching resource development plans with overall national development plans.**

India has made efforts towards resource planning since the **First Five Year Plan**. Colonies were exploited because colonisers had better technology, which shows that resources contribute to development only with technology and institutions.

## Land resources and land use
India's land under different relief features: about **43% plains** (agriculture and industry), **30% mountains** (perennial rivers, tourism, ecology) and **27% plateaus** (minerals, fossil fuels, forests).

**Land use pattern.** India's total geographical area is **3.28 million sq km**, but land use data is available for only about **93%** (most of the north-east except Assam, and parts of Jammu and Kashmir occupied by Pakistan and China, have not been surveyed). Land use categories: forests; land not available for cultivation (barren and wasteland; non-agricultural uses); other uncultivated land (permanent pastures, culturable waste, tree crops); fallow lands (current fallow, left uncultivated for less than a year; other fallow, 1 to 5 years); and **net sown area**.

> [!REMEMBER] Facts examiners like
> - Net sown area is over **80%** of total area in **Punjab and Haryana**, and less than **10%** in **Arunachal Pradesh, Mizoram, Manipur and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands**.
> - The **National Forest Policy (1952)** outlined a desired **33%** of geographical area under forest. Actual forest area is far below this.
> - **Wasteland** includes rocky, arid and desert areas, and land put to non-agricultural uses (settlements, roads, railways, industry).

**Land degradation.** About **130 million hectares** of land are degraded in India (NCERT figure). Causes:
- **Mining**: in **Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh and Odisha**, deforestation due to mining and abandoned deep scars.
- **Overgrazing**: in **Gujarat, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra**.
- **Over-irrigation**: in **Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh**, causing waterlogging and increase in **salinity and alkalinity**.
- **Mineral processing**: grinding of limestone for cement and calcite and soapstone for ceramics generates huge dust that retards infiltration of water into soil.
- **Industrial effluents** as waste.

**Conservation measures**: afforestation and proper management of grazing; planting **shelter belts**; control of overgrazing; stabilisation of sand dunes by growing thorny bushes; proper management of wastelands; control of mining; proper discharge of industrial effluents after treatment.

## Soil types and conservation
Soil is the most important renewable natural resource. It takes **millions of years** to form soil up to a few centimetres deep. Factors: relief, parent rock, climate, vegetation, other life forms and time; also changes in temperature, running water, wind, glaciers, decomposers.

> [!KEY] Major soils of India (with map areas)
> **1. Alluvial soil**: the most widely spread and important soil; the entire **northern plains** (deposited by the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra), also **Rajasthan and Gujarat** through a narrow corridor, and the **eastern coastal plains**, especially the deltas of the **Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri**. Consists of sand, silt and clay. By age: **Bangar** (old alluvial, more **kanker** nodules) and **Khadar** (new alluvial, more fertile, finer particles). Very fertile; rich in **potash, phosphoric acid and lime**; ideal for sugarcane, paddy, wheat and other cereals and pulses. Intensively cultivated and densely populated.
> **2. Black soil (regur)**: made of extremely fine clayey material, formed from **lava rocks (Deccan trap)**. Covers the plateaus of **Maharashtra, Saurashtra, Malwa, Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh**, extending south-east along the **Godavari and Krishna** valleys. Ideal for **cotton** (also called **black cotton soil**). Well known for its **capacity to hold moisture**. Rich in **calcium carbonate, magnesium, potash and lime**, but **poor in phosphoric contents**. Develops **deep cracks** in hot weather, which helps aeration; sticky when wet.
> **3. Red and yellow soil**: develops on **crystalline igneous rocks** in areas of low rainfall in the **eastern and southern parts of the Deccan plateau**; also parts of **Odisha, Chhattisgarh**, southern parts of the middle Ganga plain and along the piedmont zone of the Western Ghats. **Reddish** due to diffusion of **iron** in crystalline and metamorphic rocks; looks **yellow when hydrated**.
> **4. Laterite soil**: from the Latin *later* (**brick**). Develops under **high temperature and heavy rainfall**, resulting from intense **leaching**. Humus content low (bacteria destroy it in high temperature). Found in **Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Madhya Pradesh** and hilly areas of **Odisha and Assam**. With manures and fertilisers, suitable for **tea and coffee** (Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu); red laterite soils in Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh and Kerala suit **cashew nut**.
> **5. Arid soil**: red to brown, generally **sandy** and **saline**; in some areas salt is obtained by evaporating water. Lower horizons have **kankar** due to increasing calcium content downwards, which restricts infiltration of water. Found in **western Rajasthan**; can be cultivated after proper irrigation.
> **6. Forest soil**: in hilly and mountainous areas with sufficient rain forests. Loamy and silty in valley sides, coarse-grained in upper slopes. Acidic with low humus in snow-covered Himalayan areas; fertile in lower valley parts.

**Soil erosion** is the denudation of the soil cover and subsequent washing down. Causes: deforestation, overgrazing, construction and mining, defective farming methods (wrong ploughing), and natural forces (wind, glacier, water).
- **Gullies**: running water cuts through clayey soils making deep channels. Land becomes unfit for cultivation and is called **bad land**; in the **Chambal basin** such lands are called **ravines**.
- **Sheet erosion**: water flows as a sheet over large areas down a slope, washing away the topsoil.
- **Wind erosion**: wind blows loose soil off flat or sloping land.

> [!KEY] Soil conservation methods
> - **Contour ploughing**: ploughing along contour lines decelerates the flow of water down slopes.
> - **Terrace cultivation**: steps cut out on slopes restrict erosion; practised in the **western and central Himalayas**.
> - **Strip cropping**: large fields divided into strips, with strips of grass left between crops to break the force of wind.
> - **Shelter belts**: planting lines of trees to create shelter; contributed to stabilisation of sand dunes and the desert in **western India**.
> - Plugging gullies and making checks.

> [!MISTAKE] Black soil and phosphorus
> Black soil is rich in calcium carbonate, magnesium, potash and lime, but **poor** in phosphoric content. Alluvial soil is the one rich in phosphoric acid.

=== forest-and-wildlife-resources ===
## The big idea
We share this planet with millions of other living beings. Forests and wildlife give us oxygen, food, medicine, timber and a stable climate. India is one of the world's richest countries in biological diversity, but much of it is under threat. This chapter explains why, and how governments and **local communities** are trying to conserve it.

## Flora and fauna in India
India is one of the **mega-biodiversity countries** of the world, with nearly **8 per cent** of the total number of species in the world (estimated at about 1.6 million). At least **10 per cent** of India's recorded wild flora and **20 per cent** of its mammals are on the threatened list.

> [!KEY] IUCN categories of species (with Indian examples)
> - **Normal species**: populations considered normal for survival: cattle, sal, pine, rodents.
> - **Endangered species**: in danger of extinction: **blackbuck, crocodile, Indian wild ass, Indian rhino, lion-tailed macaque, sangai** (brow-antlered deer in Manipur).
> - **Vulnerable species**: population has declined to levels from where it is likely to move into the endangered category: **blue sheep, Asiatic elephant, Gangetic dolphin**.
> - **Rare species**: species with small populations that may move into endangered/vulnerable categories: **Himalayan brown bear, wild Asiatic buffalo, desert fox and hornbill**.
> - **Endemic species**: found only in particular areas, usually isolated by natural or geographical barriers: **Andaman teal, Nicobar pigeon, Andaman wild pig, mithun** in Arunachal Pradesh.
> - **Extinct species**: not found after searches of known or likely areas: **Asiatic cheetah, pink head duck**.

> [!REMEMBER] The Asiatic cheetah
> The world's fastest land mammal was declared **extinct in India in 1952**. (Cheetahs from Africa were reintroduced in Kuno National Park, Madhya Pradesh, in 2022, but that is a different subspecies.)

## Causes of depletion
- **Colonial period**: expansion of railways, agriculture, commercial and scientific forestry and mining.
- **Agricultural expansion**: between 1951 and 1980, over 26,200 sq km of forest area was converted to agricultural land.
- **Shifting cultivation** (slash and burn) in tribal belts of the north-east and central India.
- **Large-scale development projects**: since 1951, over **5,000 sq km** of forest was cleared for river valley projects; the **Narmada Sagar Project** in Madhya Pradesh would inundate about **40,000 hectares** of forest.
- **Mining**: for example, the **Buxa Tiger Reserve** in West Bengal is seriously threatened by ongoing **dolomite mining**, disturbing the natural habitat of many species and blocking the migration route of the great Indian elephant.
- **Enrichment plantations**: large areas of natural forest replaced by single commercially valuable species (teak in south India; chir pine in the Himalayas).
- Grazing, fuelwood collection, poaching, pollution and forest fires, and the unequal consumption of resources (the richest 5% of society may cause more ecological damage than the poorest 25%).

> [!EXAMPLE] The Himalayan yew
> The Himalayan yew (*Taxus wallachiana*), a medicinal plant found in Himachal Pradesh and Arunachal Pradesh, yields **taxol**, a chemical used as an anti-cancer drug. Over-exploitation has made it endangered; thousands of trees have dried up.

Loss of forests also has cultural consequences: **women** in poor households often have to walk long distances to collect fuel, fodder and water, increasing their workload and health problems.

## Conservation of forests and wildlife
Conservation preserves ecological diversity and life-support systems: water, air and soil. It also preserves the genetic diversity of plants and animals for better growth of species and breeding.

> [!KEY] Key measures by the government
> - **Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972**: an all-India list of protected species was published; hunting was banned; trade in wildlife was restricted; habitats were given legal protection; national parks and wildlife sanctuaries were set up.
> - **Project Tiger (1973)**: launched because tiger numbers had dropped to about 1,827 in 1973. Threats: poaching for trade in skins and bones, shrinking habitat, depletion of prey. Examples of tiger reserves: **Corbett National Park (Uttarakhand), Sunderbans National Park (West Bengal), Bandhavgarh National Park (Madhya Pradesh), Sariska Wildlife Sanctuary (Rajasthan), Manas Tiger Reserve (Assam), Periyar Tiger Reserve (Kerala)**.
> - Projects for other species, such as the Indian elephant, rhino, crocodile, and the Asiatic lion of Gir.

**Types of forests (by government classification):**
| Type | What it is | States where it dominates |
|---|---|---|
| **Reserved forests** | More than **half** of total forest land; regarded as the most valuable for conservation | Jammu and Kashmir, Andhra Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, West Bengal, Maharashtra |
| **Protected forests** | About **one-third** of forest area; protected from further depletion | Bihar, Haryana, Punjab, Himachal Pradesh, Odisha, Rajasthan |
| **Unclassed forests** | Other forests and wastelands belonging to government and private individuals and communities | North-eastern states and parts of Gujarat |

Reserved and protected forests are called **permanent forest estates**. **Madhya Pradesh** has the largest area under permanent forests (75% of its total forest area).

## Community and conservation
Local communities have often protected forests better than laws.

- **Sariska Tiger Reserve (Rajasthan)**: villagers fought against mining by citing the Wildlife Protection Act.
- **Alwar district (Rajasthan)**: inhabitants of five villages declared **1,200 hectares** of forest as the **Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri'**, with their own rules banning hunting and protecting wildlife against outside encroachments.
- **Chipko Movement** in the Himalayas: villagers (many women) hugged trees to resist deforestation; it also showed community afforestation with indigenous species can succeed.
- **Beej Bachao Andolan** in **Tehri** and **Navdanya**: showed that adequate farm production is possible without synthetic chemicals, through diversified crops.
- **Joint Forest Management (JFM)**: a programme involving local communities in managing and restoring degraded forests. It was first passed as a resolution in **Odisha in 1988**. Communities get benefits such as non-timber forest produce and a share in timber harvested.

**Sacred groves and traditions.** Nature worship is an age-old tribal belief that all creations of nature have to be protected; such beliefs preserved virgin forests called **sacred groves** (forests of God and Goddesses). The **Mundas and Santhals** of Chota Nagpur worship **mahua** and **kadamba** trees; tribals of Odisha and Bihar worship **tamarind and mango** trees during weddings. The **peepal** and **banyan** are considered sacred by many. The **Bishnois** of Rajasthan protect **blackbuck (chinkara), nilgai and peacocks**, which can be seen in herds around their villages.

> [!MISTAKE] Endangered vs vulnerable
> The **Asiatic elephant** and **Gangetic dolphin** are **vulnerable**, not endangered; the blackbuck and Indian rhino are **endangered**. Mithun is **endemic** (Arunachal), not extinct.

=== water-resources ===
## The big idea
Three-fourths of the Earth is covered with water, yet only a small fraction is usable fresh water. Water is a **renewable** resource, renewed through the **hydrological cycle**, but its availability varies over space and time. Over-use, pollution and unequal access cause **water scarcity**. This chapter explores scarcity, the debate over large dams, and traditional **rainwater harvesting** methods.

## Water scarcity
> [!KEY] How much water is there?
> **96.5 per cent** of the total volume of the world's water is in the **oceans**; only **2.5 per cent** is **freshwater**. Nearly **70 per cent** of this freshwater occurs as **ice sheets and glaciers** in Antarctica, Greenland and mountain regions, and a little less than **30 per cent** is stored as **groundwater**. India receives nearly **4 per cent** of the global precipitation and ranks **133rd** in the world in terms of water availability per person per annum.

**Water scarcity is not only about too little water.** Causes:
- **Large and growing population**: more water for domestic use and more food production.
- **Over-exploitation for irrigation**: to facilitate higher food grain production, water resources are over-exploited for dry-season agriculture; many farmers have their own wells and tube-wells, causing **falling groundwater levels**.
- **Urbanisation and industrialisation**: industries need water and power (hydroelectric). Cities with large populations and urban lifestyles have their own groundwater pumping devices.
- **Poor quality of water**: even where there is enough water, it may be **polluted** by domestic and industrial wastes, chemicals, pesticides and fertilisers, making it hazardous for use.
- **Unequal access** among different social groups.

> [!EXAMPLE] Scarcity in a water-rich area
> A region may have ample water and yet face scarcity because the water is polluted, or because it is unequally distributed. That is why conserving and managing water is a must.

## Multi-purpose river projects and integrated water resources management
**History of hydraulic structures in India:**
- **1st century BC**: **Sringaverapura near Prayagraj (Allahabad)** had a sophisticated water harvesting system channelling the floodwater of the river Ganga.
- During **Chandragupta Maurya's** time, dams, lakes and irrigation systems were extensively built.
- Evidence of sophisticated irrigation works in **Kalinga (Odisha), Nagarjunakonda (Andhra Pradesh), Bennur (Karnataka), Kolhapur (Maharashtra)**.
- **11th century**: **Bhopal Lake**, one of the largest artificial lakes of its time.
- **14th century**: the tank in **Hauz Khas, Delhi**, was constructed by **Iltutmish** to supply water to the Siri Fort area.

> [!KEY] Dam and multi-purpose project
> A **dam** is a barrier across flowing water that obstructs, directs or retards the flow, often creating a reservoir, lake or impoundment. **Multi-purpose projects** integrate several uses: irrigation, electricity generation, water supply for domestic and industrial use, flood control, recreation, inland navigation and fish breeding. **Jawaharlal Nehru** proudly proclaimed dams as the "**temples of modern India**", because they would integrate development of agriculture and village economy with rapid industrialisation and urban growth.

**Examples:** In the **Sutlej-Beas basin**, the **Bhakra Nangal** project water is used both for **hydel power and irrigation**. The **Hirakud** project in the **Mahanadi** basin integrates **conservation of water with flood control**.

**Why multi-purpose projects are criticised:**
1. **Regulating and damming rivers** affects their natural flow, causing poor sediment flow and excessive sedimentation at the bottom of the reservoir, resulting in rockier stream beds and poorer habitats for aquatic life.
2. Dams **fragment rivers**, making it difficult for aquatic fauna to migrate, especially for spawning.
3. Reservoirs on floodplains **submerge existing vegetation and soil**, leading to decomposition.
4. **Large-scale displacement** of local communities, who often do not get the benefits; landless and poor suffer while big landowners and industrialists benefit.
5. **Irrigation has changed cropping patterns** toward water-intensive commercial crops, causing salinisation of soil.
6. Dams did not always control floods; sedimentation reduced flood-carrying capacity, and **sudden release of water** during heavy rain worsened floods (e.g., Maharashtra and Gujarat in 2006).
7. They may induce **earthquakes**, water-borne diseases, pests and pollution from excessive use of water.

**Movements against dams**: the **Narmada Bachao Andolan** (Save Narmada Movement), a non-governmental organisation that mobilised tribal people, farmers, environmentalists and human rights activists against the **Sardar Sarovar Dam** on the Narmada in Gujarat. It started focusing on environmental issues related to trees that would be submerged, and later shifted to rehabilitation of displaced people. The **Tehri Dam Andolan** is another example.

**Inter-state water disputes** have also arisen, e.g., the **Krishna-Godavari dispute**: objections raised by Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh to the diversion of more water at **Koyna** by the Maharashtra government for a multipurpose project, which would reduce downstream flow in their states.

## Rainwater harvesting
Many thought that given the disadvantages and rising resistance against multi-purpose projects, **water harvesting** was a viable socio-economically and environmentally feasible alternative.

> [!KEY] Traditional methods by region
> - **Hilly and mountainous regions (western Himalayas)**: people built diversion channels like the **'guls' or 'kuls'** for agriculture.
> - **Rajasthan (drinking water)**: **rooftop rainwater harvesting** was commonly practised.
> - **Flood plains of Bengal**: people developed **inundation channels** to irrigate their fields.
> - **Arid and semi-arid regions (Rajasthan)**: agricultural fields were converted into rain-fed storage structures that allowed water to stand and moisten the soil, like the **'khadins' in Jaisalmer** and **'johads'** in other parts.
> - **Bikaner, Phalodi and Barmer (Rajasthan)**: almost all houses traditionally had underground tanks or **'tankas'** for storing drinking water, connected to sloping roofs by pipes. Rain falling on rooftops travelled down the pipes into the tanka. The first spell of rain was usually not collected, to clean the roofs. The rainwater, called **'palar pani'**, is considered the **purest form of natural water**. Tankas also keep rooms cool in summer.
> - **Meghalaya**: a **200-year-old system of bamboo drip irrigation**. Water from streams and springs is diverted through bamboo pipes; **18–20 litres** of water enter the system per minute, and it is reduced to **20–80 drops per minute** at the site of the plant (used for black pepper and betel leaf).

**Modern examples:**
- **Gendathur**, a remote backward village in **Mysuru, Karnataka**: villagers installed household rooftop rainwater harvesting systems (about 200 households). The village earns the rare distinction of being rich in rainwater.
- **Shillong (Meghalaya)**: interestingly, although **Cherrapunji and Mawsynram**, 55 km away, receive the highest rainfall in the world, Shillong faces acute water shortage, so nearly every household has a rooftop rainwater harvesting structure.
- **Tamil Nadu** is the **first state in India** to make **rooftop rainwater harvesting compulsory** for all houses across the state; there are legal provisions to punish defaulters.
- In western Rajasthan, the practice of rooftop harvesting is declining because perennial **Rajasthan Canal (Indira Gandhi Canal)** water is now available, though some houses still keep tankas because they do not like the taste of tap water.

> [!MISTAKE] Bhakra Nangal vs Hirakud
> **Bhakra Nangal** (Sutlej-Beas): hydel power + irrigation. **Hirakud** (Mahanadi): water conservation + flood control. Students often swap these.

=== agriculture ===
## The big idea
India is an agriculturally important country: about **two-thirds** of its population is engaged in agricultural activities. Agriculture produces most of the food we eat and raw materials for industries (cotton, jute, sugarcane). This chapter looks at **types of farming**, **cropping seasons**, **major crops** with the conditions they need and where they grow, and the **reforms** that aim to make farming productive and fair.

## Types of farming
> [!KEY] 1. Primitive subsistence farming
> Practised on small patches of land with primitive tools like the hoe, dao and digging sticks, and family/community labour. Depends on monsoon, natural fertility of soil and suitability of other environmental conditions. It is **'slash and burn' agriculture**: farmers clear a patch of land, produce cereals and other food crops, and when soil fertility decreases, shift to a new patch. Nature replenishes the fertility of the old patch. Productivity is low because no fertilisers or modern inputs are used.

**Names of shifting cultivation:**
| In India | Outside India |
|---|---|
| **Jhumming** in Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland (north-east) | **Milpa** in Mexico and Central America |
| **Bewar** or **Dahiya** in Madhya Pradesh | **Conuco** in Venezuela |
| **Podu** or **Penda** in Andhra Pradesh | **Roca** in Brazil |
| **Pama Dabi**, **Koman** or **Bringa** in Odisha | **Masole** in Central Africa |
| **Kumari** in the Western Ghats | **Ladang** in Indonesia |
| **Valre** or **Waltre** in south-eastern Rajasthan | **Ray** in Vietnam |
| **Khil** in the Himalayan belt; **Kuruwa** in Jharkhand | |

> [!KEY] 2. Intensive subsistence farming
> Practised in areas of **high population pressure on land**. It is **labour-intensive**; high doses of biochemical inputs and irrigation are used for higher production. Due to the 'right of inheritance', land is divided among successive generations, so landholdings become **uneconomical** and small. Farmers continue to take maximum output from limited land because they have no alternative source of livelihood.

> [!KEY] 3. Commercial farming
> Uses **higher doses of modern inputs** like high yielding variety (HYV) seeds, chemical fertilisers, insecticides and pesticides to obtain higher productivity. The degree of commercialisation varies by region: **rice is a commercial crop in Punjab and Haryana**, but a subsistence crop in Odisha.
> **Plantation** is a type of commercial farming in which a **single crop** is grown on a large area. It has an interface of agriculture and industry, uses capital-intensive inputs and migrant labourers, and all produce is used as raw material in industries. Examples: **tea, coffee, rubber, sugarcane, banana**. Tea in **Assam and North Bengal**, coffee in **Karnataka**. Development of plantations depends on a well-developed network of transport and communication connecting plantations, processing industries and markets.

## Cropping seasons
| Season | Sown | Harvested | Main crops |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rabi** | Winter, **October to December** | Summer, **April to June** | **Wheat, barley, peas, gram, mustard** |
| **Kharif** | Onset of **monsoon** (June–July) | **September–October** | **Paddy, maize, jowar, bajra, tur (arhar), moong, urad, cotton, jute, groundnut, soybean** |
| **Zaid** | Short season **between rabi and kharif** (summer months) | | **Watermelon, muskmelon, cucumber, vegetables, fodder crops** |

- **Rabi** crops are grown mainly in the north and north-western states: Punjab, Haryana, Himachal Pradesh, Jammu and Kashmir, Uttarakhand and Uttar Pradesh. **Western temperate cyclones** (western disturbances) bring winter precipitation that helps the success of these crops; the Green Revolution in Punjab, Haryana, western UP and parts of Rajasthan also helped.
- **Kharif**: important rice-growing regions include Assam, West Bengal, coastal Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Tamil Nadu, Kerala and Maharashtra (Konkan), along with UP and Bihar; recently paddy has also become important in Punjab and Haryana. In **Assam, West Bengal and Odisha**, three crops of paddy are grown in a year: **Aus, Aman and Boro**.
- **Sugarcane** takes almost a year to grow.

## Major crops
> [!KEY] Food crops
> **Rice** - staple food of the majority of people. India is the **second largest producer of rice** in the world after **China**. Kharif crop; needs **high temperature (above 25°C)**, **high humidity** and **annual rainfall above 100 cm**; grown in areas with less rainfall with the help of irrigation. Grown in the plains of north and north-east India, coastal areas and deltaic regions. Canal irrigation and tube-wells made it possible in Punjab, Haryana, western UP and parts of Rajasthan.
> **Wheat** - second most important cereal; main food crop in the north and north-west. Rabi crop; needs a **cool growing season and bright sunshine at the time of ripening**; **50 to 75 cm** of annual rainfall evenly distributed over the growing season. Two important wheat-growing zones: the **Ganga-Satluj plains** in the north-west and the **black soil region of the Deccan**. Major producers: Punjab, Haryana, UP, MP, Bihar and Rajasthan.
> **Millets** - **jowar, bajra and ragi**: coarse grains with very high nutritional value. **Ragi** is very rich in **iron, calcium**, other micro-nutrients and roughage.
> - **Jowar**: third most important food crop with respect to area and production; **rain-fed**, grown in moist areas that hardly need irrigation. **Maharashtra** is the largest producer, followed by Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh.
> - **Bajra**: grows well on **sandy soils and shallow black soil**. **Rajasthan** is the largest producer, followed by UP, Maharashtra, Gujarat and Haryana.
> - **Ragi**: a crop of **dry regions**; grows well on red, black, sandy, loamy and shallow black soils. **Karnataka** is the largest producer, followed by Tamil Nadu; also HP, Uttarakhand, Sikkim, Jharkhand and Arunachal Pradesh.
> **Maize** - used both as food and fodder. Kharif crop requiring **21°C to 27°C** and old alluvial soil. In some states like **Bihar**, maize is grown in the **rabi** season too. Major producers: Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, UP, Bihar, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana. Use of modern inputs (HYV seeds, fertilisers, irrigation) increased production.
> **Pulses** - India is the **largest producer as well as consumer of pulses** in the world. They are a major source of **protein** in a vegetarian diet. Major pulses: tur (arhar), urad, moong, masur, peas and gram. Pulses need less moisture and survive even in dry conditions. Being **leguminous** crops, all except arhar help **restore soil fertility by fixing nitrogen** from the air, so they are mostly grown in rotation with other crops. Major producers: Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan, Maharashtra and Karnataka.

> [!KEY] Food crops other than grains
> **Sugarcane** - a tropical as well as subtropical crop. Grows well in hot and humid climate with **21°C to 27°C** and **75–100 cm** annual rainfall; irrigation needed in low-rainfall regions. Grown on a variety of soils; needs manual labour from sowing to harvesting. India is the **second largest producer of sugarcane after Brazil**. It is the main source of sugar, gur (jaggery), khandsari and molasses. Major producers: **Uttar Pradesh**, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Bihar, Punjab and Haryana.
> **Oilseeds** - India is among the largest producers of oilseeds in the world; different oilseeds cover approximately 12 per cent of the total cropped area. Main oilseeds: **groundnut, mustard, coconut, sesamum (til), soybean, castor seeds, cotton seeds, linseed and sunflower**. **Groundnut** is a kharif crop and accounts for about half of the major oilseeds produced; **Gujarat** was the largest producer of groundnut (2019–20 figures in NCERT), followed by Rajasthan and Tamil Nadu. **Linseed and mustard** are rabi crops. **Sesamum** is a kharif crop in the north and a rabi crop in the south. **Castor seed** is grown both as rabi and kharif.
> **Tea** - a beverage crop introduced in India initially by the **British**. Today, most tea plantations are owned by Indians. Grows well in **tropical and subtropical climates** endowed with **deep and fertile well-drained soil, rich in humus and organic matter**. Tea bushes require a **warm and moist frost-free climate** all through the year; frequent showers evenly distributed over the year ensure continuous growth of tender leaves. Tea is **labour-intensive** and requires abundant, cheap and skilled labour. It is processed within the tea garden to retain freshness. Major producers: **Assam**, the hills of **Darjeeling and Jalpaiguri** districts (West Bengal), Tamil Nadu and Kerala; also HP, Uttarakhand, Meghalaya, Andhra Pradesh and Tripura.
> **Coffee** - Indian coffee is known for its good quality. The **Arabica** variety initially brought from **Yemen** is produced in the country; it is in great demand worldwide. Its cultivation was introduced on the **Baba Budan Hills**, and even today it is confined to the **Nilgiris** in **Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu**.
> **Horticulture crops** - India is the **second largest producer of fruits and vegetables** in the world (after China). Mangoes of Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, UP and West Bengal; oranges of Nagpur and Cherrapunjee (Meghalaya); bananas of Kerala, Mizoram, Maharashtra and Tamil Nadu; litchi and guava of UP and Bihar; pineapples of Meghalaya; grapes of Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Maharashtra; apples, pears, apricots and walnuts of Jammu and Kashmir and Himachal Pradesh.

> [!KEY] Non-food crops
> **Rubber** - an **equatorial crop**, but under special conditions also grown in tropical and subtropical areas. Requires **moist and humid climate** with rainfall of **more than 200 cm** and temperature **above 25°C**. An important industrial raw material. Mainly grown in **Kerala**, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, **Andaman and Nicobar Islands** and the **Garo hills of Meghalaya**.
> **Fibre crops** - **cotton, jute, hemp and natural silk**. The first three are derived from crops grown in the soil; silk is obtained from cocoons of silkworms fed on green leaves of mulberry. Rearing of silkworms for silk fibre is called **sericulture**.
> **Cotton** - India is believed to be the original home of the cotton plant. India is the **second largest producer of cotton** in the world (after China). Grows well in **drier parts of the black cotton soil of the Deccan plateau**. Requires **high temperature, light rainfall or irrigation, 210 frost-free days and bright sunshine**. It is a **kharif** crop and requires **6 to 8 months** to mature. Major producers: **Maharashtra, Gujarat**, Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Tamil Nadu, Punjab, Haryana and Uttar Pradesh.
> **Jute** - known as the **golden fibre**. Grows well on **well-drained fertile soils in flood plains where soils are renewed every year**. High temperature is required during growth. Major producers: **West Bengal**, Bihar, Assam, Odisha and Meghalaya. Used for gunny bags, mats, ropes, yarn, carpets and other artefacts. Due to its high cost, it is losing market to synthetic fibres and packing materials, especially nylon.

## Technological and institutional reforms
Sustained uses of land without compatible techno-institutional changes have hindered agricultural development. Despite development of irrigation, farmers in many parts still depend on monsoon and natural fertility.

- **Institutional reforms** after independence: collectivisation, **consolidation of holdings**, cooperation and **abolition of zamindari** were given priority in the First Five Year Plan.
- **Land reforms** were the main focus of the First Five Year Plan; the right of inheritance had led to fragmentation of holdings.
- In the 1960s and 1970s, the Government of India introduced agricultural reforms: the **Green Revolution** (based on HYV seeds, chemical fertilisers and irrigation) and the **White Revolution (Operation Flood)** to improve milk production.
- **Comprehensive land development programme** included both institutional and technical reforms: provision for **crop insurance** against drought, flood, cyclone, fire and disease; establishment of **Grameen banks, cooperative societies and banks** for loan facilities at lower interest rates.
- **Kisan Credit Card (KCC)** and **Personal Accident Insurance Scheme (PAIS)** were introduced.
- Special weather bulletins and agricultural programmes on radio and television.
- The government announces **Minimum Support Price (MSP)**, remunerative and procurement prices for important crops to check exploitation of farmers by speculators and middlemen.

> [!KEY] Bhoodan-Gramdan
> **Vinoba Bhave**, a spiritual heir of Gandhi, undertook padyatra to spread Gandhi's message. At **Pochampalli** (now in Telangana), some landless villagers demanded land. Vinoba Bhave promised to try, and **Shri Ram Chandra Reddy** offered **80 acres** of land to 80 landless villagers. This act was known as **'Bhoodan'**. Some zamindars, owners of many villages, offered to distribute entire villages among the landless; this was known as **'Gramdan'**. This initiative is also known as the **"Blood-less Revolution"**.

**Contribution of agriculture and food security.** Agriculture is the backbone of the Indian economy, but its share in GDP has declined. The government has set up the **Food Corporation of India (FCI)**, which procures food grains from farmers at MSP, and the **Public Distribution System (PDS)** distributes them at subsidised prices through ration shops.

> [!MISTAKE] Second-largest producer mix-ups
> India is **second** (after China) in rice, cotton, fruits and vegetables, and tea (after China); **second** in sugarcane after **Brazil**; but the **largest** producer **and** consumer of **pulses**.

=== minerals-and-energy-resources ===
## The big idea
Almost everything we use, from a pin to a train, is made from **minerals**. And every factory, vehicle and home needs **energy**. Minerals are **non-renewable** and unevenly distributed, and most of our energy comes from **fossil fuels** that will run out. This chapter covers how minerals occur, India's major minerals and their belts, conventional and non-conventional energy, and why conservation matters.

> [!KEY] Mineral
> Geologists define a mineral as a **"homogenous, naturally occurring substance with a definable internal structure."** Minerals are found in varied forms, from the hardest diamond to the softest talc. **Rocks** are combinations of homogenous substances called minerals.

## Mode of occurrence of minerals
Minerals are usually found in **'ores'** (an accumulation of any mineral mixed with other elements). The mineral content of the ore must be in sufficient concentration to make extraction commercially viable.

1. **Igneous and metamorphic rocks**: minerals occur in **cracks, crevices, faults or joints**. Smaller occurrences are called **veins** and larger ones **lodes**. They are formed when minerals in liquid/molten and gaseous forms are forced upward through cavities. Examples: **tin, copper, zinc and lead**.
2. **Sedimentary rocks**: minerals occur in **beds or layers**, formed by deposition, accumulation and concentration in horizontal strata. **Coal** and some forms of **iron ore** are concentrated by long periods under great heat and pressure; others like **gypsum, potash salt and sodium salt** form by evaporation, especially in arid regions.
3. **Decomposition of surface rocks**: soluble constituents are removed, leaving a **residual mass** of weathered material containing ores. **Bauxite** is formed this way.
4. **Alluvial deposits** in the sands of valley floors and the base of hills: called **'placer deposits'**, they generally contain minerals not corroded by water: **gold, silver, tin and platinum**.
5. **Ocean waters**: most minerals in ocean water are too widely diffused to be of economic significance, but **common salt, magnesium and bromine** are largely derived from ocean waters. Ocean beds are rich in **manganese nodules**.

> [!EXAMPLE] Rat-hole mining
> In **Jowai and Cherrapunjee (Meghalaya)**, coal mining is done by family members in the form of a long narrow tunnel known as **'rat hole' mining**. In most of India minerals are a nationalised property, but in tribal north-east India they are owned by individuals or communities.

**Peninsular rocks** contain most reserves of coal, metallic minerals, mica and many non-metallic minerals; **sedimentary rocks** on the western and eastern flanks of the peninsula (Gujarat and Assam) have most petroleum deposits; **Rajasthan** with its rock systems has reserves of many non-ferrous minerals; the vast alluvial plains of north India are almost devoid of economic minerals.

## Ferrous minerals
Ferrous minerals account for about **three-fourths** of the total value of metallic mineral production.

> [!KEY] Iron ore
> The basic mineral and the backbone of industrial development. India is rich in good-quality iron ores.
> - **Magnetite**: the **finest** iron ore with a very high content of iron, **up to 70%**. Has excellent **magnetic** qualities, valuable for the electrical industry.
> - **Hematite**: the **most important industrial iron ore** in terms of quantity used, but slightly lower iron content than magnetite (**50–60%**).
> **Major iron ore belts:**
> 1. **Odisha–Jharkhand belt**: high-grade hematite at **Badampahar mines** in Mayurbhanj and Kendujhar districts of Odisha; in **Gua and Noamundi** in Singhbhum district of Jharkhand.
> 2. **Durg–Bastar–Chandrapur belt** (Chhattisgarh and Maharashtra): very high-grade hematites in the famous **Bailadila range of hills** in Bastar district, Chhattisgarh; the range has 14 deposits of super high-grade hematite with the best physical properties for steel making. Iron ore from these mines is **exported to Japan and South Korea via Visakhapatnam port**.
> 3. **Ballari–Chitradurga–Chikkamagaluru–Tumakuru belt** (Karnataka): large reserves. The **Kudremukh mines** in the Western Ghats of Karnataka are a **100 per cent export unit**; Kudremukh deposits are known to be one of the largest in the world. The ore is transported as **slurry through a pipeline** to a port near **Mangaluru**.
> 4. **Maharashtra–Goa belt**: includes **Goa** and **Ratnagiri** district of Maharashtra. Ores are not of very high quality but are efficiently exploited; exported through **Marmagao port**.

**Manganese** is mainly used in the manufacturing of **steel and ferro-manganese alloy**. Nearly **10 kg of manganese** is required to manufacture **one tonne of steel**. Also used for bleaching powder, insecticides and paints. **Odisha** is the largest producer of manganese ores in India.

## Non-ferrous minerals
India's reserves and production of non-ferrous minerals are not very satisfactory.

- **Copper**: India is critically deficient in copper reserves. Being malleable, ductile and a good conductor, copper is mainly used in electrical cables, electronics and chemical industries. The **Balaghat mines** in Madhya Pradesh, the **Khetri mines** in Rajasthan and the **Singhbhum district** of Jharkhand are leading producers.
- **Bauxite**: a clay-like substance from which **alumina and later aluminium** is obtained. Bauxite deposits form by the decomposition of rocks rich in **aluminium silicates**. Aluminium combines strength of metals like iron with extreme lightness, good conductivity and great malleability. Deposits are mainly found in the **Amarkantak plateau, Maikal hills and the plateau region of Bilaspur-Katni**. **Odisha** was the largest bauxite producing state in India; **Panchpatmali deposits** in **Koraput district** are the most important.

## Non-metallic minerals
- **Mica**: made up of a series of plates or leaves; splits easily into thin sheets. Can be clear, black, green, red, yellow or brown. Due to excellent **di-electric strength**, low power loss factor, insulating properties and resistance to high voltage, it is one of the most indispensable minerals used in **electric and electronic industries**. Deposits are found on the **northern edge of the Chota Nagpur plateau**. The **Koderma–Gaya–Hazaribagh belt of Jharkhand** is the leading producer. In Rajasthan, the major producing area is around **Ajmer**; **Nellore** mica belt of Andhra Pradesh is also important.
- **Limestone** is found in association with rocks composed of calcium carbonates or calcium and magnesium carbonates. It is the basic raw material for the **cement industry** and essential for **smelting iron ore** in the blast furnace.

**Hazards of mining**: dust and noxious fumes inhaled by miners make them vulnerable to **pulmonary diseases**; risk of collapsing mine roofs, inundation and fires; water sources in the region get contaminated; dumping of waste and slurry leads to degradation of land, soil and increased stream and river pollution.

**Conservation of minerals**: geological processes of mineral formation are so slow that the rates of replenishment are infinitely small compared to present consumption. Minerals are **finite and non-renewable**. We should use them in a planned and sustainable manner, use improved technologies to use low-grade ores at low costs, **recycle metals** (using scrap metals), and use **substitutes**.

## Energy resources: conventional sources
Energy can be generated from fuel minerals like coal, petroleum, natural gas, uranium and from electricity. **Conventional sources**: firewood, cattle dung cake, coal, petroleum, natural gas and electricity (hydel and thermal). **Non-conventional**: solar, wind, tidal, geothermal, biogas and atomic energy. In rural India, firewood and cattle dung cake are most common; over 70% of rural household energy comes from these. But continuing them is difficult because forest area is decreasing and dung is better used as manure.

> [!KEY] Coal
> The most abundantly available fossil fuel in India; provides a substantial part of the nation's energy needs. Formed due to the compression of plant material over millions of years. Depending on the degree of compression, depth and time of burial:
> - **Peat**: decaying plants in swamps; low carbon, high moisture, low heating capacity.
> - **Lignite**: low-grade **brown coal**, soft with high moisture content. Principal reserves at **Neyveli (Tamil Nadu)**; used for generating electricity.
> - **Bituminous**: coal buried deep and subjected to increased temperatures; the most popular coal in commercial use. **Metallurgical coal** is high-grade bituminous coal of special value for smelting iron in blast furnaces.
> - **Anthracite**: the **highest quality hard coal**.
> **Two main groups in India:** **Gondwana** coal, a little over **200 million years** old, and **Tertiary** deposits, only about **55 million years** old. Major Gondwana resources are in the **Damodar valley (West Bengal–Jharkhand)**: **Jharia, Raniganj and Bokaro** are important coalfields; the **Godavari, Mahanadi, Son and Wardha** valleys also contain coal. **Tertiary coals** occur in the north-eastern states of **Meghalaya, Assam, Arunachal Pradesh and Nagaland**. Coal is a bulky material that loses weight on use, so heavy industries and thermal power stations are located on or near coalfields.

> [!KEY] Petroleum
> Petroleum (mineral oil) is the next major energy source after coal. It provides fuel for heat and lighting, lubricants for machinery and raw materials for many manufacturing industries. Petroleum refineries act as a **"nodal industry"** for synthetic textile, fertiliser and numerous chemical industries. Most petroleum occurrences in India are associated with **anticlines and fault traps** in rock formations of the tertiary age. In regions of folding, anticlines or domes, oil is trapped in the crest of the upfold; gas, being lighter, usually occurs above the oil.
> - About **63 per cent** of India's petroleum production is from **Mumbai High**, **18 per cent** from **Gujarat** and **16 per cent** from **Assam**.
> - **Ankleshwar** is the most important field of Gujarat.
> - **Assam** is the **oldest** oil-producing state of India. **Digboi, Naharkatiya and Moran-Hugrijan** are important oil fields there.

> [!KEY] Natural gas
> An important clean energy resource found in association with or without petroleum. Used as a source of energy as well as an industrial raw material in the **petrochemical** industry; considered an environment-friendly fuel because of low CO₂ emissions. Large reserves have been discovered in the **Krishna-Godavari basin**; along the west coast reserves are found in the **Mumbai High** and allied fields, supplemented by finds in the **Gulf of Khambhat**; the **Andaman and Nicobar Islands** also have large reserves. The **1,700 km long Hazira–Vijaipur–Jagdishpur (HVJ)** cross-country gas pipeline links Mumbai High and Bassien with fertiliser, power and industrial complexes in western and northern India. **CNG** is used as a liquid fuel substitute for vehicles.

**Electricity** is generated mainly in two ways:
- **Hydro electricity**: by **fast-flowing water**, a renewable resource. Multi-purpose projects like **Bhakra Nangal, Damodar Valley Corporation and the Kopili Hydel Project** produce hydroelectric power.
- **Thermal electricity**: by burning **coal, petroleum and natural gas** to drive turbines. Thermal power stations use non-renewable fossil fuels.

## Non-conventional sources of energy
Growing consumption of energy has made India increasingly dependent on fossil fuels. Rising prices of oil and gas and their potential shortages have raised uncertainties about future energy security, and fossil fuels cause pollution. So there is a pressing need to use renewable sources.

- **Nuclear or atomic energy**: obtained by **altering the structure of atoms**; enormous energy is released as heat and used to generate electricity. **Uranium and thorium**, available in **Jharkhand and the Aravalli ranges of Rajasthan**, are used. The **monazite sands of Kerala** are also rich in **thorium**. Nuclear power stations: **Narora (UP), Rawatbhata (Rajasthan), Kakrapar (Gujarat), Tarapur (Maharashtra), Kaiga (Karnataka), Kalpakkam (Tamil Nadu)**.
- **Solar energy**: India is a tropical country with enormous possibilities of tapping solar energy. **Photovoltaic technology** converts sunlight directly into electricity. The largest solar plant of India at the time was located at **Madhapur, near Bhuj (Gujarat)**, where solar energy is used to **sterilise milk cans**. Solar energy can reduce rural dependence on firewood and dung cakes.
- **Wind power**: India ranks among the "**Wind Super Power**" countries of the world. The largest wind farm cluster is located in **Tamil Nadu from Nagercoil to Madurai**. Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Gujarat, Kerala, Maharashtra and Lakshadweep have important wind farms; **Nagercoil and Jaisalmer** are well known for effective use of wind energy.
- **Biogas**: shrubs, farm waste, animal and human waste are used to produce biogas for domestic consumption in rural areas. Decomposition of organic matter yields gas with higher thermal efficiency than kerosene, dung cake and charcoal. Plants using cattle dung are known as **'Gobar gas plants'**; they provide energy and improved quality manure, and prevent loss of trees and manure due to burning of fuelwood and cow dung cakes.
- **Tidal energy**: oceanic tides can be used to generate electricity. Floodgate dams are built across inlets; during high tide water flows into the inlet and gets trapped when the gate is closed; it then flows back through a pipe that carries it through a power-generating turbine. In India, the **Gulf of Khambhat, the Gulf of Kuchchh** in Gujarat on the western coast and the **Gangetic delta in the Sunderban regions** of West Bengal provide ideal conditions.
- **Geothermal energy**: heat and electricity produced by using the **heat from the interior of the Earth**. Where the geothermal gradient is high, high temperatures are found at shallow depths; groundwater absorbs heat and rises as hot springs. Two experimental projects: one in the **Parvati valley near Manikarn (Himachal Pradesh)** and the other in the **Puga valley, Ladakh**.

## Conservation of energy resources
Energy is a basic requirement for economic development. We need a cautious approach for judicious use of limited energy resources: use **public transport** instead of individual vehicles, switch off electricity when not in use, use **power-saving devices**, and use **non-conventional sources** of energy. "**Energy saved is energy produced.**"

> Magnetite is the **finest** ore (up to 70% iron); hematite is the **most important industrial** ore by quantity (50–60%). Don't call hematite the richest.

## Quick recap
- Minerals occur in veins/lodes, beds/layers, residual mass, placer deposits and ocean water.
- Iron ore: magnetite (finest), hematite (most used); four belts incl. Bailadila and Kudremukh.
- Odisha leads manganese and bauxite (Panchpatmali, Koraput); copper at Balaghat, Khetri, Singhbhum; mica in Koderma–Gaya–Hazaribagh.
- Coal: Gondwana (200 million yrs, Damodar valley) and Tertiary (55 million yrs, north-east); lignite at Neyveli.
- Petroleum: Mumbai High 63%, Gujarat 18%, Assam 16%; HVJ pipeline 1,700 km.
- Non-conventional: nuclear, solar (Madhapur), wind (Nagercoil–Madurai), biogas, tidal (Khambhat), geothermal (Manikarn, Puga).

W[3]: Distinguish between conventional and non-conventional sources of energy.
A: (1) Conventional sources (coal, petroleum, natural gas, firewood, cattle dung cake, hydel and thermal electricity) have been in use for a long time; non-conventional sources (solar, wind, tidal, geothermal, biogas, nuclear) are newer.
(2) Most conventional sources (fossil fuels) are exhaustible/non-renewable; most non-conventional sources are renewable and inexhaustible.
(3) Conventional sources cause pollution and their prices are rising; non-conventional sources are generally clean and eco-friendly, though they need higher initial investment and technology.

W[5]: Why do we need to conserve mineral resources? Suggest ways to do so.
A: Need: (1) The geological processes of mineral formation are very slow, and rates of replenishment are infinitely small compared to present consumption.
(2) Mineral resources are finite and non-renewable; rich ores are being rapidly exhausted.
(3) Continued extraction leads to increasing costs as minerals are taken from greater depths and with lower quality.
Ways: (4) Use minerals in a planned and sustainable manner; improve technologies so that low-grade ores can be used at low costs.
(5) Recycle metals using scrap and use substitutes to reduce consumption.

=== manufacturing-industries ===
## The big idea
Production of goods in large quantities after processing raw materials into more valuable products is called **manufacturing**. Paper from wood, sugar from sugarcane, iron and steel from iron ore, aluminium from bauxite: all are manufacturing. People in the secondary sector are engaged in manufacturing. A country's economic strength is measured by the development of its manufacturing industries.

## Importance of manufacturing
- Helps **modernise agriculture**, the backbone of our economy.
- Reduces heavy dependence of people on agricultural income by providing **jobs in secondary and tertiary sectors**.
- Industrial development is a precondition for **eradicating unemployment and poverty**; this was the main philosophy behind public sector industries and joint ventures in India. It also aimed to bring down **regional disparities** by setting up industries in tribal and backward areas.
- **Export** of manufactured goods expands trade and commerce and brings foreign exchange.
- Countries that transform raw materials into a wide variety of higher-value finished goods are prosperous.

> [!KEY] Agriculture and industry go hand in hand
> Agro-industries in India have given a major boost to agriculture by raising productivity. Industries depend on agriculture for raw materials (cotton, jute, sugarcane), and sell products like **irrigation pumps, fertilisers, insecticides, pesticides, plastic and PVC pipes, machines and tools** to farmers. They are not exclusive of each other.

**Contribution to national economy.** Over the last two decades, the share of manufacturing in GDP has stagnated at about **17 per cent** (out of 27% for industry, including mining, quarrying, electricity and gas). The desired growth rate over the decade is **12 per cent**. Since 2003, manufacturing has grown at 9–10 per cent per annum. The government set up the **National Manufacturing Competitiveness Council (NMCC)** with the objective of improving productivity through appropriate policy interventions.

## Industrial location
Industrial locations are complex: they are influenced by availability of **raw material, labour, capital, power and market**. It is rarely possible to find all these at one place, so manufacturing tends to locate at the most appropriate place where all factors can be made available or arranged at lower cost.

- Many industries tend to come together to make use of advantages offered by urban centres, known as **agglomeration economies**.
- After independence, most industries were concentrated in a few cities like Mumbai, Kolkata and Chennai; pre-independence industries were located from the point of view of overseas trade.

## Agro-based industries
> [!KEY] Textile industry
> The only industry in India that is **self-reliant and complete in the value chain**, from raw material to the highest value-added products. It contributes significantly to industrial production, employment generation (about 35 million directly, as per NCERT) and foreign exchange earnings.
> **Cotton textiles**: in ancient India, cotton textiles were produced by hand spinning and handloom weaving. After the 18th century, power-looms came into use. The **first successful textile mill** was established in **Mumbai in 1854**. Early years: cotton textile mills were concentrated in the cotton-growing belt of **Maharashtra and Gujarat**, due to availability of raw cotton, market, transport including accessible port facilities, labour, moist climate, etc.
> India has world-class production in **spinning**, but **weaving** supplies low-quality fabric because it cannot use much of the high-quality yarn produced in the country. Weaving is highly decentralised (handloom, power-loom, mills). India exports yarn to **Japan**; other importers include the USA, UK, Russia, France, East European countries, Nepal, Sri Lanka and Bangladesh. Problems: erratic power supply, outdated machinery, low output of labour, stiff competition with synthetic fibres.

> [!KEY] Jute textiles
> India is the **largest producer of raw jute and jute goods** and stands at **second place as an exporter after Bangladesh**. Most mills are located in **West Bengal**, mainly along the banks of the **Hugli river**, in a narrow belt. The **first jute mill** was set up near **Kolkata in 1859 at Rishra**. After Partition in 1947, jute mills remained in India but three-fourths of the jute-producing area went to Bangladesh (then East Pakistan).
> **Location factors in the Hugli basin**: proximity of jute-producing areas, inexpensive water transport, good network of railways, roadways and waterways, abundant water for processing raw jute, cheap labour from West Bengal and adjoining states of Bihar, Odisha and UP, and Kolkata as a large urban centre providing banking, insurance and port facilities for export.
> Challenges: stiff competition from synthetic substitutes and from Bangladesh, Brazil, the Philippines, Egypt and Thailand. The **National Jute Policy was formulated in 2005** to increase productivity, improve quality, ensure good prices to farmers and enhance yield per hectare. Growing global concern for environment-friendly, biodegradable materials has opened opportunities for jute products.

> [!KEY] Sugar industry
> India stands **second as a world producer of sugar** but occupies the first place in the production of **gur and khandsari**. The raw material is bulky and its sucrose content reduces in haulage, so mills are located close to sugarcane areas. Over 460 mills are spread over UP, Bihar, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Gujarat, Punjab, Haryana and MP. Sixty per cent of mills are in UP and Bihar. The industry is **seasonal**, ideally suited to the **cooperative sector**. In recent years, mills have shifted and concentrated in **southern and western states, especially Maharashtra**, because cane there has a **higher sucrose content**, the **cooler climate** ensures a longer crushing season, and **cooperatives are more successful** there. Challenges: seasonal nature, old and inefficient methods, transport delays, need to maximise use of bagasse.

## Mineral-based industries
> [!KEY] Iron and steel industry
> The iron and steel industry is the **basic industry**, since all other industries, heavy, medium and light, depend on it for their machinery. It is a **heavy industry** because all raw materials and finished goods are heavy and bulky, entailing heavy transportation costs. Iron ore, coking coal and limestone are required in the ratio of approximately **4:2:1**; some manganese is also needed to harden the steel.
> India is an important iron and steel producing country; it ranks **second among world crude steel producers** (after China; as per NCERT 2019) but is also the largest producer of sponge iron. Despite this, per capita consumption of steel is low. Most public sector steel plants market their steel through the **Steel Authority of India Limited (SAIL)**, while TISCO markets through **Tata Steel**.
> **Chotanagpur plateau region** has the maximum concentration of iron and steel industries, mainly because of the low cost of iron ore, high-grade raw materials in proximity, cheap labour and vast growth potential in the home market.
> Problems: high costs and limited availability of coking coal, lower productivity of labour, irregular supply of energy and poor infrastructure. Liberalisation and FDI have boosted the industry.

> [!KEY] Aluminium smelting
> The **second most important metallurgical industry** in India. Aluminium is light, resistant to corrosion, a good conductor of heat, malleable, and becomes strong when mixed with other metals. Used to manufacture aircraft, utensils and wires; it has gained popularity as a substitute for steel, copper, zinc and lead. Smelters are located in **Odisha, West Bengal, Kerala, Uttar Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Maharashtra and Tamil Nadu**. **Bauxite**, the raw material, is a very bulky, dark reddish coloured rock. Regular supply of electricity and assured sources of raw material at minimum cost are the two prime factors for location.

**Chemical industries**: comprise both **large and small-scale** units; includes **inorganic** (sulphuric acid, nitric acid, alkalies, soda ash, caustic soda) and **organic** chemicals (petrochemicals for synthetic fibres, plastics, dyes, pharmaceuticals). Chemical industry is its own largest consumer.

**Fertiliser industry**: centred around the production of **nitrogenous fertilisers (mainly urea)**, phosphatic fertilisers and ammonium phosphate (DAP) and complex fertilisers. India is the **third largest producer** of nitrogenous fertilisers. **Gujarat, Tamil Nadu, Uttar Pradesh, Punjab and Kerala** contribute half of the fertiliser production. Expansion came after the Green Revolution.

**Cement industry**: essential for construction (houses, factories, bridges, roads, airports, dams). Requires bulky and heavy raw materials like **limestone, silica and gypsum**. The **first cement plant was set up in Chennai in 1904**. After independence the industry expanded; decontrol of price and distribution since 1989 and other policy reforms led to rapid growth. Plants are in Gujarat (with access to markets in the Gulf), etc.

**Automobile industry**: provides vehicles for quick transport of goods, services and passengers. After liberalisation, the coming of new and contemporary models stimulated demand. Located around Delhi, Gurugram, Mumbai, Pune, Chennai, Kolkata, Lucknow, Indore, Hyderabad, Jamshedpur and Bengaluru.

**Information technology and electronics industry**: covers a wide range of products from transistor sets to television, telephones, cellular telecom, computers. **Bengaluru** has emerged as the **electronic capital of India**. A major impact of this industry is **employment generation**, and it has been a major earner of foreign exchange through the fast-growing **Business Process Outsourcing (BPO)** sector. Continuing growth in hardware and software is key to India's success. A large number of women are employed in this sector.

## Industrial pollution and environmental degradation
Industries are responsible for four types of pollution:
1. **Air pollution**: caused by high proportion of undesirable gases like **sulphur dioxide and carbon monoxide**; airborne particulate materials (solids and liquids) like dust, sprays, mist and smoke. Smoke is emitted by chemical and paper factories, brick kilns, refineries and smelting plants, and burning of fossil fuels. Toxic gas leaks can be very hazardous with long-term effects (e.g., **Bhopal gas tragedy, 1984**).
2. **Water pollution**: caused by **organic and inorganic industrial effluents** discharged into rivers. Main culprits: paper, pulp, chemical, textile and dyeing, petroleum refineries, tanneries and electroplating industries that let out dyes, detergents, acids, salts and heavy metals like lead and mercury, pesticides, fertilisers, synthetic chemicals with carbon, plastics and rubber. Fly ash, phospho-gypsum and iron and steel slags are major solid wastes.
3. **Thermal pollution**: occurs when **hot water from factories and thermal plants** is drained into rivers and ponds before cooling. Wastes from nuclear power plants and nuclear and weapon production facilities cause cancers, birth defects and miscarriages.
4. **Noise pollution**: results in irritation and anger, can cause hearing impairment, increased heart rate and blood pressure. Sources: industrial and construction activities, machinery, factory equipment, generators, saws, pneumatic and electric drills.
**Land and soil pollution**: dumping of wastes (glass, harmful chemicals, industrial effluents, packaging, salts and garbage) makes soil useless; rainwater percolating carries pollutants to groundwater.

> [!KEY] Control of environmental degradation
> - Minimise use of water by **reusing and recycling** in two or more successive stages.
> - **Harvest rainwater** to meet water requirements.
> - **Treat hot water and effluents** before releasing into rivers and ponds. Treatment can be primary (mechanical: screening, grinding, flocculation and sedimentation), secondary (biological process) and tertiary (biological, chemical and physical processes, recycling of wastewater).
> - Overdrawing of groundwater by industry must be regulated legally.
> - Particulate matter in air can be reduced by fitting smoke stacks with **electrostatic precipitators, fabric filters, scrubbers and inertial separators**. Smoke can be reduced by using **oil or gas instead of coal**.
> - Machinery can be redesigned for energy efficiency and reduced noise; use of noise-absorbing material and earplugs/earphones.
> **Example: NTPC** has shown the way by preserving the natural environment and resources (e.g., at **Ramagundam**): optimum utilisation of equipment through latest technologies, minimising waste generation by maximising **ash utilisation**, providing **green belts** for nurturing ecological balance, reducing environmental pollution through ash pond management, ash water recycling and liquid waste management, and **ecological monitoring**.

> [!MISTAKE] Public vs joint sector
> **Oil India Ltd** is a **joint sector** industry; **BHEL and SAIL** are **public sector**; **TISCO and Bajaj Auto** are **private sector**. Cooperative examples: **sugar in Maharashtra** and **coir in Kerala**.

=== lifelines-of-national-economy ===
## The big idea
Goods and services do not move on their own. **Transport** carries them from supply locations to demand locations; **communication** carries messages; and **trade** exchanges goods within and between countries. Together, these are the **lifelines of the national economy**. Today the world has become a "large village" because of efficient and fast-moving transport and communication.

> [!KEY] Why transport matters
> The movement of goods and people is the basis of trade. A dense and efficient network of transport and communication is a prerequisite for local, national and global trade. Transport is broadly divided into **land (roads, railways, pipelines)**, **water** and **air**.

## Roadways
India has one of the **largest road networks** in the world, about **62.16 lakh km** (as per NCERT, 2020–21 figure).

**Why roads have an edge over railways:**
- Construction cost of roads is much **lower** than railway lines.
- Roads can traverse comparatively more **dissected and undulating topography**.
- Roads can negotiate **higher gradients** of slopes and can traverse mountains like the Himalayas.
- Road transport is **economical** for transporting **few persons and relatively smaller amounts of goods over short distances**.
- Provides **door-to-door service**, reducing the cost of loading and unloading.
- Road transport is used as a **feeder** to other modes, linking railway stations, air and sea ports.

> [!KEY] Classification of roads (by capacity)
> 1. **Golden Quadrilateral Super Highways**: a major road development project linking **Delhi–Kolkata–Chennai–Mumbai–Delhi** by six-lane super highways. The **North–South corridor** links **Srinagar (J&K) and Kanyakumari (Tamil Nadu)**, and the **East–West corridor** connects **Silchar (Assam) and Porbandar (Gujarat)**. Objective: reduce time and distance between the mega cities. Implemented by the **National Highway Authority of India (NHAI)**.
> 2. **National Highways**: link extreme parts of the country; primary road systems laid and maintained by the **Central Public Works Department (CPWD)**. Many major NHs run in north-south and east-west directions. The historical **Sher-Shah Suri Marg** (called **NH-1**) runs between Delhi and Amritsar.
> 3. **State Highways**: link a state capital with district headquarters; constructed and maintained by the State **Public Works Department (PWD)**.
> 4. **District Roads**: connect district headquarters with other places of the district; maintained by the **Zila Parishad**.
> 5. **Other roads**: rural roads linking rural areas and villages with towns; received special impetus under the **Pradhan Mantri Grameen Sadak Yojana**, under which every village in the country is linked to a major town by an all-season motorable road.
> 6. **Border roads**: the **Border Roads Organisation (BRO)**, established in **1960**, constructs and maintains roads in strategically sensitive border areas, improving accessibility in difficult terrain (e.g., the northern and north-eastern border areas).

Roads can also be classified by construction material: **metalled** (cement, concrete or bitumen; all-weather) and **unmetalled** (go out of use in the rainy season).

**Road density** = length of road per 100 sq km of area. It varies widely across India, being low in hilly, plateau and desert areas.

**Problems of roads**: road network inadequate for the volume of traffic; about half the roads are unmetalled; national highways are inadequate; roads are highly congested in cities; most bridges and culverts are old and narrow.

## Railways
Railways are the **principal mode of transportation for freight and passengers** in India. They make it possible to conduct multifarious activities like business, sightseeing and pilgrimage along with transportation of goods over longer distances. The Indian Railways is the **largest public sector undertaking** in the country.

- The **first train** steamed off from **Bombay to Thane** in **1853**, covering a distance of **34 km**.
- Indian Railways is reorganised into **16 zones** (as per NCERT; zones have been added since).
- Railway gauges: **broad gauge (1.676 m)**, **metre gauge (1.000 m)** and **narrow gauge (0.762 m and 0.610 m)**.

**Factors affecting distribution of railways:**
- The **northern plains** with vast level land, high population density and rich agricultural resources provided the most favourable conditions, though a large number of **rivers** requiring construction of bridges posed obstacles.
- In the hilly terrains of the **peninsular region**, railway tracks are laid through low hills, gaps or tunnels.
- The **Himalayan mountainous regions** are unfavourable due to high relief, sparse population and lack of economic opportunities.
- It was difficult to lay tracks on the **sandy plain of western Rajasthan**, swamps of Gujarat, forested tracts of MP, Chhattisgarh, Odisha and Jharkhand.
- The contiguous stretch of the **Sahyadri** could be crossed only through gaps or passes (Ghats). The **Konkan Railway** along the west coast has facilitated movement of passengers and goods in this important economic region, but it faces problems like sinking of track and landslides.

**Problems**: many passengers travel without tickets; thefts and damage of railway property; people stop trains and pull chains unnecessarily, causing heavy damage.

## Pipelines
Pipelines were earlier used to transport water to cities and industries. Now they transport **crude oil, petroleum products and natural gas** from oil and natural gas fields to refineries, fertiliser factories and big thermal power plants. Solids can also be transported through pipelines when converted into **slurry**. Far-inland refineries like **Barauni, Mathura and Panipat** and gas-based fertiliser plants could be thought of only because of pipelines.

**Advantages**: initial cost of laying is high, but subsequent running costs are minimal; rules out trans-shipment losses or delays.

> [!KEY] Three important pipeline networks
> 1. From **oil fields in upper Assam to Kanpur (Uttar Pradesh)**, via **Guwahati, Barauni and Allahabad (Prayagraj)**. It has branches from Barauni to Haldia via Rajbandh, Rajbandh to Maurigram and Guwahati to Siliguri.
> 2. From **Salaya in Gujarat to Jalandhar in Punjab**, via **Viramgam, Mathura, Delhi and Sonipat**. It has branches to connect Koyali (near Vadodara, Gujarat) and Chakshu and other places.
> 3. **Gas pipeline from Hazira in Gujarat connects Jagdishpur in Uttar Pradesh, via Vijaipur in Madhya Pradesh (HVJ)**. It has branches to Kota in Rajasthan, Shahjahanpur, Babrala and other places in UP.

## Waterways
Waterways are the **cheapest means of transport**. They are most suitable for carrying **heavy and bulky goods**. It is a **fuel-efficient and environment-friendly** mode of transport. India has inland navigation waterways of about **14,500 km** in length; out of these only about 5,685 km are navigable by mechanised vessels.

| National Waterway | Stretch | Length |
|---|---|---|
| **NW-1** | **Ganga** river between **Allahabad (Prayagraj) and Haldia** | **1,620 km** |
| **NW-2** | **Brahmaputra** river between **Sadiya and Dhubri** | **891 km** |
| **NW-3** | **West-Coast Canal in Kerala** (Kottapuram–Kollam, Udyogamandal and Champakkara canals) | **205 km** |
| **NW-4** | Specified stretches of **Godavari and Krishna** rivers along with **Kakinada–Puducherry** stretch of canals | **1,078 km** |
| **NW-5** | Specified stretches of **Brahmani** river along with **Matai** river, delta channels of **Mahanadi and Brahmani** rivers and **East Coast Canal** | **588 km** |

Other inland waterways include the Mandavi, Zuari and Cumberjua, Sunderbans, Barak and Kerala backwaters.

**Major sea ports.** India's coastline is about **7,516.6 km** long, dotted with **12 major** and about **200 notified non-major** (minor/intermediate) ports. These major ports handle about **95 per cent** of the country's foreign trade by volume (NCERT figure).

> [!KEY] Major ports (west to east, a map favourite)
> - **Kandla** (now **Deendayal Port**) in **Kachchh**: the **first port developed soon after independence** to ease the volume of trade on Mumbai port, in the wake of the **loss of Karachi port to Pakistan** after Partition. It is a **tidal port** that caters to the convenient handling of exports and imports of highly productive granary and industrial belts stretching across J&K, HP, Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan and Gujarat.
> - **Mumbai**: the **biggest port** with a spacious **natural and well-sheltered harbour**. The **Jawaharlal Nehru port** was planned with a view to decongest Mumbai port and serve as a hub port for this region.
> - **Marmagao (Goa)**: the premier **iron ore exporting port** of the country, accounting for about **fifty per cent** of India's iron ore export.
> - **New Mangalore (Karnataka)**: caters to the export of **iron ore concentrates from Kudremukh mines**.
> - **Kochchi (Kerala)**: the **extreme south-western port**, located at the entrance of a **lagoon** with a natural harbour.
> - **Tuticorin (Thoothukudi, Tamil Nadu)**: the **extreme south-eastern port**. It has a natural harbour and rich hinterland; it handles a large variety of cargoes, including trade with neighbouring Sri Lanka, Maldives and the coastal regions of India.
> - **Chennai**: one of the **oldest artificial ports** of the country; ranks next to Mumbai in volume of trade and cargo.
> - **Visakhapatnam**: the **deepest land-locked and well-protected port**. Originally conceived as an outlet for iron ore exports.
> - **Paradip (Odisha)**: specialises in the export of **iron ore**.
> - **Kolkata**: an **inland riverine port**. It serves a very large and rich hinterland of the Ganga–Brahmaputra basin. Being a **tidal port**, it requires constant **dredging** of the Hooghly. **Haldia** was developed as a **subsidiary port** to relieve growing pressure on Kolkata.

## Airways
Air travel is the **fastest, most comfortable and prestigious** mode of transport. It can cover very difficult terrains like high mountains, dreary deserts, dense forests and long oceanic stretches with great ease. Air travel has made access easier in the **north-eastern part** of the country, which is marked by big rivers, dissected relief, dense forests and frequent floods.
- Air transport was **nationalised in 1953**.
- **Air India** provides domestic and international air services.
- **Pawan Hans Helicopters Ltd.** provides helicopter services to Oil and Natural Gas Corporation (ONGC) in its offshore operations, to inaccessible areas and difficult terrains like the north-eastern states and interior parts of Jammu and Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand.
- Air travel is not within reach of common people, except in the north-east, where special provisions are made.

## Communication
**Personal communication**: The Indian postal network is the **largest in the world**. It handles parcels as well as personal written communications.
- **First-class mail**: cards and envelopes; **airlifted** between stations covering both land and air.
- **Second-class mail**: book packets, registered newspapers and periodicals; carried by **surface mail**, covering land and water transport.
- To facilitate quick delivery of mail in large towns and cities, **six mail channels** were introduced recently: **Rajdhani Channel, Metro Channel, Green Channel, Business Channel, Bulk Mail Channel and Periodical Channel**.

India has one of the largest **telecom networks** in Asia. **STD** facilities are available in many places; the government has made special provisions to extend 24-hour STD facility to every village. There is a uniform rate of STD across the country, possible by integrating developments in space technology with communication technology.

**Mass communication** provides entertainment and creates awareness among people about national programmes and policies: radio, television, newspapers, magazines, books and films. **All India Radio (Akashwani)** broadcasts a variety of programmes in national, regional and local languages. **Doordarshan**, the national television channel of India, is one of the largest terrestrial networks in the world. India publishes a large number of newspapers and periodicals in about 100 languages and dialects; the largest number of newspapers published in India are in **Hindi**, followed by English and Urdu. India is the **largest producer of feature films** in the world. The **Central Board of Film Certification** is the authority to certify both Indian and foreign films.

## International trade
The exchange of goods among people, states and countries is referred to as **trade**. The market is the place where such exchanges take place. **Trade between two countries is called international trade.** It may take place through sea, air or land routes.

> [!KEY] Balance of trade
> Exports and imports are the components of trade. The **balance of trade** of a country is the **difference between its exports and imports**. When the value of exports exceeds the value of imports, it is called a **favourable balance of trade**; on the contrary, if the value of imports exceeds the value of exports, it is termed an **unfavourable balance of trade**.

Advancement of international trade of a country is an index of its economic prosperity; it is considered the **economic barometer** for a country. India has trade relations with all major trading blocs and geographical regions of the world. India's exports include agriculture and allied products, ores and minerals, gems and jewellery, chemicals and allied products, engineering goods and petroleum products. Imports include petroleum and petroleum products, pearls and precious stones, inorganic chemicals, coal, coke and briquettes, machinery, fertilisers, edible oil, newsprint. India has emerged as a **software giant** at the international level, earning large foreign exchange through IT exports.

**Tourism as a trade**: tourism in India has grown substantially over the last few decades. **More than 15 million people** are directly engaged in the tourism industry. Tourism promotes **national integration**, provides support to **local handicrafts** and cultural pursuits, helps develop international understanding about our culture and heritage, and earns **foreign exchange**. Foreign tourists visit India for heritage tourism, eco-tourism, adventure tourism, cultural tourism, medical tourism and business tourism.

=== power-sharing ===
## The big idea
In a democracy, **power is not concentrated in one person, group or organ of government**. It is shared among different organs, levels, social groups and parties. This chapter uses two stories, **Belgium** and **Sri Lanka**, to show why sharing power keeps a country united, and why refusing to share can tear it apart.

## Belgium: accommodation
Belgium is a small country in Europe, smaller in area than the state of Haryana, with a population of a little over one crore.

| Group | Share of population | Region |
|---|---|---|
| **Dutch-speaking** | **59%** | Flemish region |
| **French-speaking** | **40%** | Wallonia region |
| **German-speaking** | **1%** | |

In the capital, **Brussels**, **80%** speak French and **20%** speak Dutch. The minority French-speaking community was relatively rich and powerful. This was resented by the Dutch-speaking community, who got the benefit of economic development and education much later. This led to tensions in the 1950s and 1960s, more acute in Brussels, where Dutch speakers were a majority in the country but a minority in the capital.

> [!KEY] The Belgian model of accommodation
> Between **1970 and 1993**, Belgian leaders **amended their constitution four times**, working out an innovative arrangement:
> 1. The Constitution prescribes that the number of **Dutch and French-speaking ministers shall be equal** in the central government. Some special laws require support of the majority of members from each linguistic group, so no single community can make decisions unilaterally.
> 2. Many powers of the central government have been given to **state governments** of the two regions. State governments are **not subordinate** to the central government.
> 3. **Brussels has a separate government** in which both communities have **equal representation**. The French-speaking people accepted equal representation in Brussels because the Dutch-speaking community accepted equal representation in the central government.
> 4. Apart from central and state governments, there is a third kind of government, the **'community government'**, elected by people belonging to one language community (Dutch, French or German), no matter where they live. It has power regarding **cultural, educational and language-related issues**.

The arrangement is complicated, but it has worked well: it helped avoid civic strife and a possible division of the country on linguistic lines. When many countries of Europe came together to form the **European Union**, **Brussels was chosen as its headquarters**.

## Sri Lanka: majoritarianism
Sri Lanka, an island nation just off the southern coast of Tamil Nadu, has about 2 crore people.

- **Sinhala**-speakers: **74%**; **Tamil**-speakers: **18%**. Among Tamils, **Sri Lankan Tamils** (natives of the country) are **13%**, and **Indian Tamils** (whose forefathers came from India as plantation workers during colonial rule) are **5%**.
- Most Sinhala-speakers are **Buddhists**; most Tamils are **Hindus or Muslims**. About **7%** are Christians, both Tamil and Sinhala.
- Sri Lankan Tamils are concentrated in the **north and east** of the country.

> [!KEY] Majoritarian measures after independence (1948)
> Sri Lanka became independent in **1948**. Leaders of the Sinhala community sought to secure dominance by virtue of their majority. They adopted **majoritarian** measures:
> - In **1956**, an Act was passed to recognise **Sinhala as the only official language**, disregarding Tamil.
> - Governments followed preferential policies that **favoured Sinhala applicants for university positions and government jobs**.
> - The new constitution stipulated that the state shall **protect and foster Buddhism**.

These measures gradually increased the feeling of alienation among Sri Lankan Tamils. They felt that none of the major political parties led by Buddhist Sinhala leaders was sensitive to their language and culture, and that the constitution and government policies denied them equal political rights, discriminated against them in getting jobs and other opportunities, and ignored their interests.

By the 1980s, several political organisations were formed demanding an **independent Tamil Eelam** (state) in northern and eastern Sri Lanka. The distrust turned into widespread conflict and soon a **civil war**. Thousands of people of both communities were killed; many families were forced to leave as refugees, and many more lost their livelihoods. The civil war ended in **2009** (with the defeat of the LTTE), but it caused a terrible setback to the social, cultural and economic life of the country.

> [!KEY] Majoritarianism
> A belief that the **majority community should be able to rule a country in whichever way it wants**, by disregarding the wishes and needs of the minority.

## Why power-sharing is desirable
> [!KEY] Two sets of reasons
> **Prudential reasons** (based on careful calculation of gains and losses): power-sharing is good because it helps to **reduce the possibility of conflict** between social groups. Since social conflict often leads to violence and political instability, power-sharing is a good way to **ensure the stability of political order**. Imposing the will of the majority community on others may look like an attractive option in the short run, but in the long run it undermines the unity of the nation (Sri Lanka shows this).
> **Moral reasons** (based on the intrinsic worth of power-sharing): power-sharing is **the very spirit of democracy**. A democratic rule involves sharing power with those affected by its exercise and who have to live with its effects. People have a right to be consulted on how they are to be governed. A **legitimate government** is one where citizens, through participation, acquire a stake in the system.

## Forms of power-sharing
1. **Horizontal distribution (among organs of government)**: power is shared among the **legislature, executive and judiciary**. Each organ is placed at the same level and **checks** the others. Ministers and government officials exercise power but are responsible to Parliament or State Assemblies; judges are appointed by the executive but can check the functioning of the executive or laws made by legislatures. This arrangement is called a system of **checks and balances**.
2. **Vertical distribution (among governments at different levels)**: a general government for the entire country (the **Union or Central Government**), governments at the provincial or regional level (**State Governments** in India), and further at the local level (**municipalities and panchayats**). Such a division of powers between higher and lower levels of government is called **vertical division of power**. When the Constitution clearly lays this down, it is **federal** government.
3. **Among social groups**: power may be shared among different social groups, such as religious and linguistic groups. The **'community government'** in Belgium is a good example. In some countries there are constitutional and legal arrangements whereby socially weaker sections and women are represented in legislatures and administration, like **reserved constituencies** in India.
4. **Among political parties, pressure groups and movements**: democracy ensures that power does not remain in the hands of one; competition among parties ensures power keeps shifting. In the long run, power is shared among different parties representing different ideologies and social groups. Sometimes two or more parties form an **alliance** to contest elections and, if elected, form a **coalition government**. Interest groups such as traders, businessmen, industrialists, farmers and industrial workers also share governmental power, either through participation in government committees or by influencing decision-making.

> [!MISTAKE] "Power-sharing weakens a country"
> Many people think concentrated power gives quick decisions and unity. Belgium shows the opposite: sharing power **kept the country united**. Sri Lanka shows that refusing to share power **led to civil war**.

=== federalism ===
## The big idea
Power-sharing between different levels of government is the heart of **federalism**. India has a central government, state governments and local governments, each with its own powers given by the **Constitution**. This chapter explains what makes a country federal, how India practises federalism, and how the **1992 amendment** created a third tier of local government.

> [!KEY] Federalism
> A system of government in which the power is **divided between a central authority and various constituent units** of the country. Usually there are two levels: one for the entire country (common national interest) and another at the level of provinces or states (day-to-day administering). Both levels enjoy their power **independent of the other**. In a **unitary** system, there is either only one level of government, or sub-units are subordinate to the central government (e.g., Sri Lanka).

## Key features of federalism
1. There are **two or more levels (tiers)** of government.
2. Different tiers govern the **same citizens**, but each tier has its own **jurisdiction** in specific matters of legislation, taxation and administration.
3. The jurisdictions are specified in the **Constitution**, so the existence and authority of each tier is **constitutionally guaranteed**.
4. Fundamental provisions of the Constitution **cannot be unilaterally changed** by one level of government; changes require the consent of both levels.
5. **Courts** have the power to interpret the Constitution and the powers of different levels; the highest court acts as an **umpire** if disputes arise.
6. **Sources of revenue** for each level are clearly specified to ensure its financial autonomy.
7. Dual objectives: to **safeguard and promote unity** of the country, while **accommodating regional diversity**. Mutual trust and agreement to live together are essential.

> [!KEY] Two routes to federation
> - **Coming together federations**: independent states come together on their own to form a bigger unit, pooling sovereignty and retaining identity. All constituent states usually have **equal power** and are strong vis-à-vis the federal government. Examples: **USA, Switzerland, Australia**.
> - **Holding together federations**: a large country decides to divide its power between constituent states and the national government. The central government tends to be **more powerful**; constituent units may have **unequal powers** (some get special powers). Examples: **India, Spain, Belgium**.

## What makes India a federal country
The Constitution declared India a **Union of States**. Although it did not use the word "federation", the Indian Union is based on federal principles. It provides a **three-fold distribution** of legislative powers through three lists:

| List | Who makes laws | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Union List** | **Union Government** alone | Subjects of national importance: **defence, foreign affairs, banking, communications and currency** |
| **State List** | **State Governments** alone | Subjects of state and local importance: **police, trade, commerce, agriculture and irrigation** |
| **Concurrent List** | **Both** Union and State; if laws conflict, the **Union law prevails** | Common interest: **education, forest, trade unions, marriage, adoption and succession** |

**Residuary subjects** (not in any list, e.g., **computer software**, which came up after the Constitution was made) are with the **Union Government**.

- **Not all states have identical powers**: some states enjoy a special status (e.g., under Article 371, some states in the north-east have special provisions). (Jammu and Kashmir had its own constitution under Article 370 until 2019, when it was reorganised into two Union Territories.)
- **Union Territories** (e.g., **Chandigarh, Lakshadweep**, and the capital **Delhi**) are areas too small to be independent states or could not be merged; they do not have the powers of a state. The Central Government has special powers in running them.
- Changing this power-sharing arrangement is not easy. The Parliament cannot change it on its own. Any change has to be passed by **both Houses of Parliament with at least a two-thirds majority**, and then **ratified by the legislatures of at least half of the total states**.
- The **judiciary** plays an important role in overseeing implementation of constitutional provisions; in disputes about division of powers, the **High Courts and the Supreme Court** decide.
- Both levels can **raise resources by levying taxes**.

## How is federalism practised? Linguistic states and language policy
**Linguistic states.** The creation of linguistic states was the **first and a major test** for democratic politics in India. Many old states vanished and new states were created in **1947** onward; in **1956**, boundaries of several old states were changed to ensure that people who spoke the same language lived in the same state. Some states were created on the basis of culture, ethnicity or geography (Nagaland, Uttarakhand, Jharkhand). Many national leaders feared it would lead to disintegration, but experience shows that it actually made the country **more united** and made administration easier.

**Language policy.** The Constitution did **not give the status of national language to any one language**. **Hindi** was identified as the **official language**, but Hindi is the mother tongue of only about **40 per cent** of Indians. Besides Hindi, there are **22 other languages recognised as Scheduled Languages** (in the Eighth Schedule). A candidate in an examination for central government positions may opt to take the exam in any of these languages. States also have their own official languages. According to the Constitution, the use of **English** for official purposes was to stop in **1965**. However, many non-Hindi speaking states (e.g., Tamil Nadu) demanded continuation; the Central Government responded by agreeing to **continue English along with Hindi** for official purposes. Promotion of Hindi continues to be the official policy, but this does not mean imposing Hindi on states where people speak a different language. This flexibility helped India avoid the situation Sri Lanka faced.

**Centre-state relations.** The way power-sharing works depends on how ruling parties and leaders follow these arrangements. For a long time, the same party ruled both at the centre and in most states, so state governments did not exercise their rights as autonomous federal units. The Central Government often **misused the Constitution to dismiss state governments** controlled by rival parties. After **1990**, there was the rise of **regional political parties** and the beginning of the era of **coalition governments** at the Centre. Since no single party got a clear majority in the Lok Sabha, major national parties had to enter into alliances with regional parties. This led to a new culture of **power-sharing and respect for the autonomy of state governments**. A **Supreme Court** judgment also made it difficult for the Central Government to dismiss state governments arbitrarily.

## Decentralisation in India
> [!KEY] Decentralisation
> When power is taken away from Central and State governments and given to **local government**, it is called decentralisation. The basic idea: there are a large number of problems and issues best settled at the **local level**, as people have better knowledge of local problems, and local government is the best way to realise the principle of **democratic participation**.

The need was recognised in the Constitution, and there were several attempts (gram panchayats in villages and municipalities in cities), but these were directly under state governments, elections were not held regularly, and they had no powers or resources of their own.

> [!KEY] The 1992 amendment (73rd and 74th Amendments): steps towards real decentralisation
> 1. It is now **constitutionally mandatory** to hold **regular elections** to local government bodies.
> 2. **Seats are reserved** in the elected bodies and the executive heads for **Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Other Backward Classes**.
> 3. At least **one-third of all positions are reserved for women**.
> 4. An independent institution called the **State Election Commission** has been created in each state to conduct panchayat and municipal elections.
> 5. **State governments are required to share some powers and revenue** with local government bodies. The nature of sharing varies from state to state.

**Structure of rural local government (Panchayati Raj):**
- **Gram Panchayat**: each village or group of villages has a gram panchayat, a council of several ward members, often called **panch**, and a president or **sarpanch**. They are directly elected by all adult population living in that ward or village. It is the decision-making body for the entire village.
- **Gram Sabha**: the panchayat works under the overall supervision of the gram sabha. **All voters in the village are its members.** It has to meet at least twice or thrice a year to approve the annual budget of the gram panchayat and review its performance.
- **Panchayat Samiti** (or **Block** or **Mandal**): a few gram panchayats grouped together. Members are elected by all panchayat members in that area.
- **Zila Parishad**: all panchayat samitis or mandals in a district together constitute the zila (district) parishad. Most members are elected; members of the Lok Sabha and MLAs of that district and some officials of other district-level bodies are also members. The **zila parishad chairperson** is the political head.

**Urban local government**: **municipalities** in towns and **municipal corporations** in big cities, controlled by elected bodies of people's representatives. The **municipal chairperson** is the political head of a municipality; in a municipal corporation, this officer is called the **mayor**.

This new system of local government is the **largest experiment in democracy** conducted anywhere in the world: there are now about 36 lakh elected representatives in panchayats and municipalities. It has deepened democracy and increased women's representation. Difficulties remain: **gram sabhas are not held regularly**, most state governments have **not transferred significant powers** to local governments, nor adequate resources.

> [!MISTAKE] Gram Sabha vs Gram Panchayat
> The **Gram Sabha** is the body of **all voters** of the village; the **Gram Panchayat** is the **elected council** (panch + sarpanch). The panchayat works under the supervision of the gram sabha.

=== gender-religion-and-caste ===
## The big idea
Every society has differences: between women and men, among religions, among castes. Democracy does not try to erase these differences; it asks how they should be **expressed in politics**. Sometimes political expression of social differences helps disadvantaged groups win justice; sometimes it divides people and leads to violence. This chapter looks at three such differences: **gender, religion and caste**.

## Gender and politics
Gender division is a form of hierarchical social division seen everywhere, but it is rarely recognised in the study of politics. It is often understood as natural and unchangeable. However, it is based not on biology but on **social expectations and stereotypes**.

> [!KEY] Sexual division of labour
> A system in which **all work inside the home is either done by the women of the family, or organised by them through domestic helpers**. Boys and girls are brought up to believe that the main responsibility of women is housework and bringing up children. Women do work outside (fetching water, collecting fuel, working in fields) but their work is often **not valued** and does not get recognition. An average woman works **one hour more** than an average man every day (Time Use Survey), yet much of her work is unpaid. Men are willing to do the same work outside the home if paid (most tailors and cooks in hotels are men).

As a result, women's role in public life, especially politics, has been minimal in most societies. Women's political agitations demanded equal rights for women: voting rights, enhancement of political and legal status, and improvement of educational and career opportunities. Radical women's movements aimed at equality in personal and family life as well; these movements are called **feminist movements**.

> [!KEY] Feminist
> A woman or a man who **believes in equal rights and opportunities for women and men**.

In **Scandinavian countries** such as **Sweden, Norway and Finland**, the participation of women in public life is very high.

**Women's disadvantage in India**: India is a **patriarchal** society (literally, rule by father; a system that values men more and gives them power over women).
- **Literacy**: literacy rate among women is only **65.46%** compared with **82.14%** among men (Census 2011). A smaller proportion of girl students go for higher studies; parents prefer to spend resources on boys' education.
- **Jobs and wages**: the proportion of women among highly paid and valued jobs is still very small. The **Equal Remuneration Act, 1976** provides that equal wages should be paid for equal work. However, in almost all areas of work, from sports and cinema to factories and fields, women are paid less than men, even when both do exactly the same work.
- **Son preference**: in many parts of India, parents prefer sons and find ways to have the girl child aborted (sex-selective abortion). This has led to a decline in the **child sex ratio** (number of girl children per thousand boys) in the country to merely **919** (Census 2011); in some states it is below 850 or even 800.
- **Violence and safety**: there are reports of harassment, exploitation and violence against women. Urban areas have become particularly unsafe for women; even at home, women face beatings and domestic violence.

**Women's political representation.** In India, the proportion of women in legislatures has been very low. For example, the percentage of elected women members in the **Lok Sabha** touched **14.36 per cent** of its total strength for the first time in **2019**. Their share in state assemblies is less than 5 per cent. In this respect, India is among the bottom group of nations in the world. One way to solve this problem is to make it legally binding to have a fair proportion of women in elected bodies. This is what the **Panchayati Raj** has done: **one-third of seats in local government bodies** are reserved for women. There are now more than **10 lakh** elected women representatives in rural and urban local bodies. Women's organisations and activists long demanded a similar reservation of at least one-third of seats in Lok Sabha and State Assemblies for women; such a law, the **Constitution (106th Amendment) Act / Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam**, was passed in **2023** (to come into effect after the next delimitation).

> [!REMEMBER] Gender as a political issue works
> Gender division is one example where some form of social division needs to be expressed in politics. Disadvantaged groups benefit when social divisions become a political issue. Political expression of gender issues helped improve women's role in public life.

## Religion, communalism and politics
Gandhiji used to say that **religion can never be separated from politics**. By religion, he meant not any particular religion like Hinduism or Islam but the **moral values that inform all religions**. He believed politics must be guided by ethics drawn from religion.

Human rights groups argue that most victims of communal riots in India are people from **religious minorities**, and demand special steps to protect them. The women's movement argues that **family laws** of all religions discriminate against women, so government should change them to make them more equitable.

These instances involve a relationship between religion and politics, but they are not wrong: people should be able to express their needs, interests and demands in politics as members of a religious community, and the state should ensure that no religion is discriminated against.

> [!KEY] Communalism
> The problem begins when **religion is seen as the basis of the nation**, when beliefs of one religion are presented as superior to those of other religions, when demands of one religious group are formed in opposition to another, and when **state power is used to establish domination of one religious group** over the rest. This manner of using religion in politics is **communal politics**. It is based on the idea that religion is the principal basis of social community, that followers of a particular religion must belong to one community, and that their fundamental interests are the same and different from (or opposed to) those of followers of other religions.

**Forms of communalism in politics:**
1. **In everyday beliefs**: religious prejudices, stereotypes of religious communities and belief in the superiority of one's religion over others.
2. **Desire for political dominance**: a communal mind often leads to a quest for political dominance of one's own religious community. For those belonging to the majority community, this takes the form of **majoritarian dominance**; for those of the minority community, it can take the form of a desire to form a **separate political unit**.
3. **Political mobilisation on religious lines**: using sacred symbols, religious leaders, emotional appeal and plain fear to bring followers of one religion together in politics. In electoral politics, this involves special appeal to the interests or emotions of voters of one religion in preference to others.
4. **Communal violence, riots and massacres**: India and Pakistan suffered some of the worst communal riots at the time of **Partition**. The post-independence period has also seen large-scale communal violence.

> [!KEY] Secular state
> Communalism was and continues to be one of the major challenges to democracy in our country. The makers of our Constitution chose the model of a **secular state**:
> - There is **no official religion** for the Indian state. Unlike Buddhism in Sri Lanka, Islam in Pakistan and Christianity in England, our Constitution does not give a special status to any religion.
> - The Constitution provides to all individuals and communities **freedom to profess, practise and propagate any religion**, or not to follow any.
> - The Constitution **prohibits discrimination on grounds of religion**.
> - At the same time, the Constitution **allows the state to intervene in the matters of religion** in order to ensure equality within religious communities. For example, it **bans untouchability**.

Secularism is not just an ideology of some parties or persons; it is one of the **foundations of our country**. Communalism must be combated in everyday life, and religion-based mobilisation must be countered in politics.

## Caste and politics
**Caste inequalities.** Gender and religious divisions exist all over the world, but **caste division is special to India**. Caste system was based on **exclusion of and discrimination against the 'outcaste'** groups, subjected to inhuman practices of untouchability. Hereditary occupational division was sanctioned by rituals. Members of the same caste group were supposed to form a social community that practised the same or similar occupation, married within the caste group and did not eat with members of other caste groups.

Political leaders and social reformers like **Jotiba Phule, Gandhiji, B.R. Ambedkar and Periyar Ramaswami Naicker** advocated and worked to establish a society in which caste inequalities are absent. Partly due to their efforts and partly due to socio-economic changes (**urbanisation, growth of literacy and education, occupational mobility, and weakening of the position of landlords in villages**), caste hierarchy is breaking down. The Constitution prohibited any caste-based discrimination and laid foundations for policies to reverse injustices.

But caste has not disappeared: most people still marry within their own caste; untouchability persists despite constitutional prohibition; and effects of centuries of advantages and disadvantages continue: the caste groups that had access to education under the old system have done very well in acquiring modern education too. There is a large presence of "upper caste" among the urban middle classes, and caste continues to be closely linked to economic status.

> [!KEY] Caste in politics
> - When parties choose candidates in elections, they keep in mind the **caste composition of the electorate** and nominate candidates from different castes so as to muster necessary support to win elections.
> - Political parties and candidates make **appeals to caste sentiment** to muster support. Some parties are known to favour some castes.
> - **Universal adult franchise** and the principle of **one-person-one-vote** compelled political leaders to gear up to mobilise and secure political support. It also brought new consciousness among people of castes that were hitherto treated as inferior and low.

**But elections are not only about caste:**
- **No parliamentary constituency in the country has a clear majority of one single caste.** So every candidate and party needs to win the confidence of more than one caste and community.
- **No party wins the votes of all the voters of a caste** or community. When people say a caste is a "vote bank" of a party, it usually means a large proportion of voters from that caste vote for that party.
- Many political parties may put up candidates from the same caste. Some voters have more than one candidate from their caste, while many voters have no candidate from their caste.
- The ruling party and the sitting MP or MLA frequently **lose elections** in our country. That could not have happened if all castes and communities were frozen in their political preferences.
- Voters have strong attachments to political parties, often stronger than to their caste or community. People within the same caste or community have different interests depending on their economic condition. Rich and poor or men and women from the same caste often vote very differently. People's assessment of the government's performance and the popularity rating of leaders matter and are often decisive.

> [!KEY] Politics in caste
> Politics too influences the caste system and caste identities by bringing them into the political arena. So it is not politics that gets caste-ridden; it is the **caste that gets politicised**:
> - Each caste group tries to become bigger by **incorporating within it neighbouring castes or sub-castes** which were earlier excluded from it.
> - Various caste groups are required to enter into **coalitions** with other castes or communities and thus enter into dialogue and negotiation.
> - **New kinds of caste groups** have come up in the political arena, like **'backward' and 'forward'** caste groups.

Caste in politics has positive aspects: in some situations, expression of caste differences in politics gives many disadvantaged communities the space to demand their share of power; **Dalits and OBCs** have gained better access to decision-making. But exclusive attention to caste can produce negative results: it can divert attention from pressing issues like poverty, development and corruption, and in some cases leads to tensions, conflict and even violence.

> [!MISTAKE] "Secular means anti-religion"
> A secular state is **not** against religion. It has no official religion, gives everyone religious freedom, prohibits discrimination, and may intervene to ensure equality (e.g., banning untouchability).

=== political-parties ===
## The big idea
Political parties are the most visible institution in a democracy. For most ordinary citizens, democracy is equal to political parties. Yet parties are also among the least trusted institutions. This chapter explains **what parties are, what they do, why we need them, the kinds of party systems, India's national and state parties, and the challenges and reforms** of party politics.

> [!KEY] Political party
> A group of people who come together to **contest elections and hold power in the government**. They agree on some policies and programmes for society with a view to promote the collective good. A party is known by **which part it stands for, which policies it supports and whose interests it upholds**.
> A political party has **three components**: (1) the **leaders**, (2) the **active members**, (3) the **followers**.

## Functions of political parties
1. **Contest elections**: in most democracies, elections are fought mainly among candidates put up by parties. In some countries like the **USA**, members and supporters of a party choose its candidates; in India, **top party leaders** choose candidates.
2. **Put forward different policies and programmes**: voters choose from them. A party reduces a vast variety of opinions into a few basic positions that it supports. A government is expected to base its policies on the line taken by the ruling party.
3. **Make laws**: formally, laws are debated and passed in the legislature, but since most members belong to a party, they go by the direction of the party leadership.
4. **Form and run governments**: parties recruit leaders, train them and make them ministers to run the government as they want.
5. **Play the role of opposition**: parties that lose elections voice different views, criticise the government for its failures or wrong policies, and mobilise opposition to the government.
6. **Shape public opinion**: they raise and highlight issues. Parties have lakhs of members and activists spread all over the country; many of their pressure groups are extensions of parties among different sections of society. They often launch movements for resolution of problems faced by people.
7. **Provide access to government machinery and welfare schemes**: for an ordinary citizen, it is easy to approach a local party leader than a government officer. Party leaders feel close to people even when people do not fully trust them.

## Why do we need parties?
Without parties, every candidate in elections would be **independent**. No one would be able to make any promises to the people about major policy changes; the government may be formed, but its utility would remain uncertain. Elected representatives would be accountable to their constituency for what they do in the locality, but **no one would be responsible for how the country is run**. The rise of political parties is directly linked to the emergence of **representative democracies**. Large-scale societies need representative democracy; parties gather different views on various issues and present them to the government. They bring various representatives together so that a responsible government can be formed, and they support or restrain the government.

> [!EXAMPLE] Non-party elections
> In many states, **panchayat elections** are not fought on party lines. But villages often split into factions that put up a "panel" of candidates, which is exactly what a party does.

## Party systems
| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **One-party system** | Only one party is allowed to control and run the government | **China** (Communist Party of China). Not a good option for democracy because voters have no free choice. |
| **Two-party system** | Power usually changes between two main parties; several other parties may exist and contest, but only the two main parties have a serious chance of winning a majority | **USA, United Kingdom** |
| **Multi-party system** | Several parties compete for power, and more than two parties have a reasonable chance of coming to power either on their own strength or in alliance with others | **India** |

When several parties in a multi-party system join hands to contest elections and win power, it is called an **alliance or a front**. In India, there were three such major alliances in the 2004 parliamentary elections: the **National Democratic Alliance (NDA)**, the **United Progressive Alliance (UPA)** and the **Left Front**. Multi-party systems often appear messy and lead to political instability, but they allow a **variety of interests and opinions** to enjoy political representation.

**Why India has a multi-party system**: no system is ideal for all countries; party systems evolve over a long time depending on the nature of society, its social and regional divisions, its history of politics and its system of elections. India is a large country with **social and geographical diversity**, which is not easily absorbed by two or even three parties.

> [!REMEMBER] Partisanship
> **Partisan** means a person who is strongly committed to a party, group or faction. Partisanship is marked by a tendency to take a side and an inability to take a balanced view on an issue. Participation in party activities in India has been increasing over the decades; the proportion of people who say they are members of some party has gone up.

## National and state parties
Every party in the country has to **register** with the **Election Commission**. While the Commission treats all parties equally, it offers some special facilities to large and established parties: they are given a **unique symbol**, which only official candidates of that party can use. Parties that get this privilege are **"recognised" parties**.

> [!KEY] Recognition criteria
> - **State party**: a party that secures at least **six per cent of the total votes in an election to the Legislative Assembly of a State** and wins at least **two seats**.
> - **National party**: a party that secures at least **six per cent of the total votes in Lok Sabha elections or Assembly elections in four States** and wins at least **four seats in the Lok Sabha**.

**State parties** (commonly called regional parties) are not necessarily regional in ideology; some are all-India parties that happen to succeed only in some states (e.g., Samajwadi Party, Rashtriya Janata Dal). Others like the **Biju Janata Dal, Sikkim Democratic Front, Mizo National Front and Telangana Rashtra Samithi** are conscious about their state identity. Over the last three decades, the number and strength of state parties have expanded, making the Parliament more diverse; no national party is able to secure a majority on its own in some elections, so national parties are compelled to form alliances with state parties. This has contributed to strengthening **federalism and democracy**.

## Challenges to political parties
1. **Lack of internal democracy**: all over the world, there is a tendency for power to concentrate in one or a few top leaders. Parties do not keep membership registers, do not hold organisational meetings or conduct internal elections regularly. Ordinary members do not get sufficient information about what happens inside the party; those who disagree with leadership find it difficult to continue.
2. **Dynastic succession**: since most parties do not practise open and transparent procedures, there are very few ways for an ordinary worker to rise to the top. Those who happen to be the leaders are in a position of unfair advantage to favour people close to them or family members. In many parties, top positions are always controlled by members of one family.
3. **Growing role of money and muscle power**: since parties are focused only on winning elections, they tend to use short-cuts. They nominate candidates who have or can raise lots of money. Rich people and companies who give funds tend to influence policies and decisions. In some cases, parties support **criminals** who can win elections.
4. **Lack of meaningful choice**: in recent years there has been a decline in ideological differences among parties in most parts of the world. Those who want really different policies have no option available; sometimes people cannot even elect very different leaders, because the same set of leaders keep shifting from one party to another.

## How can parties be reformed?
**Recent efforts:**
- The Constitution was amended to prevent elected MLAs and MPs from changing parties (**anti-defection law**). If any MLA or MP changes parties, he or she will **lose the seat** in the legislature. This has helped bring defection down, but it has made dissent even more difficult: MPs and MLAs have to accept whatever the party leaders decide.
- The **Supreme Court** passed an order to reduce the influence of money and criminals: now it is mandatory for every candidate who contests elections to file an **affidavit giving details of his property and criminal cases** pending against him. The new system has made a lot of information available to the public, though there is no system to check if the information given is true.
- The **Election Commission** passed an order making it necessary for political parties to **hold their organisational elections and file their income tax returns**. Parties have started doing so, but it is sometimes a mere formality.

**Suggestions often made:**
- A **law to regulate the internal affairs** of political parties: maintain a register of members, follow its own constitution, have an independent authority to act as judge in case of party disputes, hold open elections to the highest posts.
- Make it mandatory for parties to give a minimum number of tickets, about **one-third, to women candidates**; similarly a quota for women in decision-making bodies of the party.
- **State funding of elections**: the government should give parties money to support their election expenses, in kind (petrol, paper, telephone) or cash, on the basis of votes secured in the last election.

Two other ways: **people can put pressure** on parties through petitions, publicity and agitations (pressure groups, media); and **political parties can improve if those who want this join them**. It is difficult to change democracy if ordinary citizens do not take part and simply criticise from outside.

> State party: **6% votes in a state assembly election + 2 seats**. National party: **6% votes in Lok Sabha or assembly elections in four states + 4 Lok Sabha seats**. Students often swap 2 and 4.

=== outcomes-of-democracy ===
## The big idea
Most countries in the world today claim to be democracies. But how do we judge whether democracy actually **delivers**? This chapter evaluates democracy on what we can reasonably expect from it: an **accountable, responsive and legitimate government**, **economic growth and development**, **reduction of inequality and poverty**, **accommodation of social diversity**, and **dignity and freedom of citizens**. The answer is balanced: democracy is not a magic solution, but it is better than any alternative we know.

> [!KEY] Why democracy is preferred
> Democracy (1) promotes **equality among citizens**, (2) enhances the **dignity of the individual**, (3) **improves the quality of decision-making**, (4) provides a method to **resolve conflicts**, and (5) allows room to **correct mistakes**.

## How do we assess democracy's outcomes?
We must not expect democracy to solve every problem. Democracy is only a **form of government**; it can create conditions for achieving something, and citizens must take advantage of those conditions. The fact that people complain about democracy is itself a testimony to its success: it shows that people have developed awareness and the ability to look critically at power holders.

## Accountable, responsive and legitimate government
**Accountable.** Democracy ensures that decision-making is based on **norms and procedures**. A citizen who wants to know if a decision was taken through the correct procedures can find out. This is called **transparency**: the right and means to examine the process of decisions. (In India, the **Right to Information** Act gives citizens such a means.) Democratic governments may be **slower** than dictatorships because they follow procedures, deliberate and negotiate, but their decisions are more acceptable and more effective. So the **cost of time** is worth paying.

**Responsive.** Democracy should produce a government that attends to the needs and expectations of people. Here the record is mixed: democracies often frustrate the needs of people and ignore the demands of a majority of the population. But they are better than non-democratic regimes at holding regular, free and fair elections and allowing open public debate on major policies.

> [!KEY] Legitimate government
> Democratic government is a **legitimate government**. It may be slow, less efficient, not always very responsive or clean, but it is **people's own government**. That is why there is overwhelming support for the idea of democracy all over the world, and people wish to be ruled by representatives elected by them.

## Economic growth and development
If we consider all democracies and all dictatorships for the fifty years between **1950 and 2000**, **dictatorships have a slightly higher rate of economic growth**.

| Regime type (1950–2000) | Average rate of growth (%) |
|---|---|
| All democratic regimes | **3.95** |
| All dictatorial regimes | **4.42** |
| Poor countries under dictatorship | 4.34 |
| Poor countries under democracy | 4.28 |

Economic development depends on several factors: the country's **population size, global situation, cooperation from other countries, economic priorities adopted** by the country, etc. The difference in rates of growth between less developed countries under dictatorship and democracy is **negligible**. Overall, we cannot say democracy is a guarantee of economic development, but we can expect it not to lag behind dictatorships. Given its other positive outcomes, democracy is a better choice even though it may not deliver faster growth.

## Reduction of inequality and poverty
Democracies are based on **political equality**: all individuals have equal weight in electing representatives (one person, one vote). But alongside the process of bringing individuals into the political arena on an equal footing, we find **growing economic inequalities**. A small number of ultra-rich enjoy a highly disproportionate share of wealth and incomes. Those at the bottom of society have very little to depend upon; their incomes have been declining. Sometimes they find it difficult to meet their basic needs of life: food, clothing, house, education and health.

In actual life, democracies do not appear to be very successful in reducing economic inequalities. The poor constitute a large proportion of our voters, and no party will like to lose their votes, yet democratically elected governments do not appear to be as keen to address the question of poverty as you would expect them to. The situation is much worse in some other countries; for example, in **Bangladesh**, more than half its population lived in poverty (NCERT example).

> [!MISTAKE] "Democracy has removed inequality"
> No. Democracy gives **political equality**, but **economic inequality** has often grown in democracies. Write this honestly in answers; examiners reward balanced points.

## Accommodation of social diversity
Democracies usually develop a procedure to conduct their competition. This **reduces the possibility of tensions** becoming explosive or violent. No society can fully and permanently resolve conflicts among different groups, but we can learn to respect these differences and evolve mechanisms to negotiate them. Democracy is best suited to produce this outcome. **Belgium** has successfully negotiated differences among ethnic populations; **Sri Lanka**, by contrast, shows what happens when the majority refuses to accommodate.

> [!KEY] Two conditions for accommodation
> 1. **Democracy is not simply rule by majority opinion.** The majority always needs to work with the minority so that governments function to represent the general view. Majority and minority opinions are **not permanent**.
> 2. **Rule by majority does not become rule by majority community** in terms of religion, race or linguistic groups. Democracy remains democracy only as long as **every citizen has a chance of being in the majority at some point of time**. If someone is barred from being in the majority on the basis of birth, the democratic rule ceases to be accommodative for that person or group.

## Dignity and freedom of the citizens
Democracy stands much superior to any other form of government in promoting **dignity and freedom of the individual**. Every individual wants to receive respect from fellow beings. Conflicts often arise among individuals because some feel they are not treated with due respect.

- **Dignity of women**: most societies across the world were historically **male-dominated**. Long struggles by women have created some sensitivity today that respect to and equal treatment of women are necessary ingredients of a democratic society. In non-democratic set-ups, such claims would not have legal basis. Once the principle of individual freedom and dignity has been accepted in democracy, it is easier for women to wage a struggle against what is now unacceptable legally and morally.
- **Caste inequalities**: democracy in India has **strengthened the claims of the disadvantaged and discriminated castes** for equal status and equal opportunity. There are still instances of caste-based inequalities and atrocities, but these lack the moral and legal foundations.
- **Expectations and complaints**: democracy is a continuous process. As people get some benefits of democracy, they ask for more. **Expectations from democracy** keep rising, and people complain when they are not met. This is a sign of success: it shows people have become aware and critical; they have become **citizens** from subjects.

## Quick recap
- Democracy promotes equality, dignity, better decisions, conflict resolution and correction of mistakes.
- It gives accountable (transparent), responsive and legitimate government, though it may be slower.
- 1950–2000: dictatorships grew slightly faster (4.42%) than democracies (3.95%); difference among poor countries negligible.
- Political equality has not removed economic inequality and poverty.
- Accommodation needs majority working with minority and no permanent majority based on birth.
- Democracy promotes dignity of women and disadvantaged castes; rising expectations show its success.

=== development ===
## The big idea
What does "development" mean? For a landless rural labourer it may mean more days of work and better wages; for a rich farmer, higher support prices; for a girl, as much freedom as her brother. **Different people have different, sometimes conflicting, developmental goals.** This chapter explores how we measure development, using **income** and other **goals** like health, education and security, how we compare countries and states, and why development must be **sustainable**.

## Different people, different goals
| Category of person | Possible developmental goals |
|---|---|
| Landless rural labourers | More days of work and better wages; local school for children; no social discrimination |
| Prosperous farmers from Punjab | Higher support prices for crops; assured high family income; settling children abroad |
| Farmers dependent only on rain | Irrigation, better rainfall, crop insurance |
| A rural woman from a land-owning family | Regular job and freedom; same opportunities as her brother |
| Urban unemployed youth | Jobs |
| A boy from a rich urban family | Better higher education, travel |
| A girl from a rich urban family | As much freedom as her brother; to decide what she wants to do in life |
| An adivasi from Narmada valley | Livelihood and land; no displacement |

> [!KEY] Conflicting goals
> What may be development for one may not be development for another, and may even be destructive. Example: to get more electricity, industrialists may want more dams. But this may submerge land and disrupt the lives of people who are displaced, such as **tribals**. So people's notion of development is **different and conflicting**.

## Income and other goals
Common goal: **more income**, regular work, better wages and decent prices for crops. But people also seek **non-material things**: equal treatment, freedom, security, respect of others. They resent discrimination. In some cases, these may be more important than more income or more consumption, because material things are not all that is needed to live.

> [!EXAMPLE] Why non-income goals matter
> If women are engaged in paid work, their dignity in the household and society increases. However, it is also the case that if there is respect for women, there would be more sharing of housework and greater acceptance of women working outside. A **safe and secure environment** may allow more women to take up a variety of jobs or run a business. So, for development, people look at a **mix of goals**.

## Comparing countries and states
**National income** is the total income of all residents of a country. It is not a useful measure for comparison between countries since populations differ. So we compare **average income**, which is the total income of the country divided by its total population. Average income is also called **per capita income**.

> [!FORMULA] Per capita (average) income
> Per capita income = Total income of the country ÷ Total population

**World Bank's classification.** The **World Bank** uses per capita income as the criterion in its **World Development Report** to classify countries. Countries with a per capita income of about **US$ 12,000 and above per annum** (2017 figures) are called **rich or high-income countries**, and those with about **US$ 1,000 or less** are called **low-income countries**. **India** comes in the category of **low middle-income countries**; its per capita income in 2017 was just about **US$ 1,820** per annum. The rich countries, excluding countries of the Middle East and certain other small countries, are generally called **developed countries**.

**Limitation of average income**: while it is useful for comparison, it **hides disparities**. Two countries may have the same average income, but in one, almost everyone may be equally well-off, while in the other, a few are extremely rich and the rest poor.

> [!EXAMPLE] Average income can mislead
> Country A: five citizens each earn ₹10,000 per month (average ₹10,000). Country B: four earn ₹500 and one earns ₹48,000 (average also ₹10,000). Most people would prefer to live in Country A, which has a more **equitable** distribution.

**Income and other criteria: Haryana, Kerala and Bihar.** Of the three states, **Haryana** has the **highest per capita income** and **Bihar** the **lowest**. Yet **Kerala** has a much lower **Infant Mortality Rate** (about 7 per 1,000 live births, versus about 30 in Haryana and 32 in Bihar), a higher **literacy rate** (about 94%) and a higher **Net Attendance Ratio** than Haryana. Why? Kerala has **adequate provision of basic health and educational facilities**. Also, in some states, the **Public Distribution System (PDS)** functions well; health and nutritional status of people there is likely to be better.

> [!KEY] Key terms
> - **Infant Mortality Rate (IMR)**: the number of children that die before the age of one year as a proportion of 1,000 live children born in that particular year.
> - **Literacy Rate**: the proportion of literate population in the 7 years and above age group.
> - **Net Attendance Ratio**: the total number of children of age group 14 and 15 years attending school as a percentage of the total number of children in the same age group.
> - **Body Mass Index (BMI)**: weight in kg divided by the square of height in metres; used to check under-nutrition among adults. If BMI is less than **18.5**, the person is undernourished; if more than **25**, overweight.

**Public facilities.** Money in your pocket cannot buy all the goods and services you need to live well. Income by itself is not a completely adequate indicator of material goods and services that citizens are able to use. Normally, money cannot buy a **pollution-free environment** or ensure **unadulterated medicines**, unless you can afford to shift to a community that already has these. Money may not protect you from infectious diseases unless the whole community takes preventive steps. It is cheaper to provide collective facilities (schools, hospitals, security) than for individuals to buy them.

**Human Development Index (HDI).** Once we realise income is not adequate, we think of other criteria. The **Human Development Report** published by the **UNDP (United Nations Development Programme)** compares countries based on the **educational levels of people, their health status and per capita income**. On this index, **Sri Lanka** ranks much higher than India, and many small neighbouring countries do better than India in some indicators despite being much smaller economies. In recent reports India ranks around 130th out of about 190 countries.

## Sustainability of development
Recent evidence suggests that the **groundwater** in many parts of India is under serious threat of overuse (e.g., in **Punjab, western UP**, hard rock plateau areas of central and south India, some coastal regions and Rajasthan). One-third of the country is overusing its groundwater reserves. **Non-renewable resources** like crude oil will get exhausted after years of use; India has limited crude oil reserves (estimates suggest they may last only a few decades at current rates), so it imports a large share.

> [!KEY] Sustainable development
> Development that meets the needs of the present **without compromising the ability of future generations** to meet their own needs. Environmental degradation does not respect state or national boundaries; it is a **global issue**. Our future is linked together. **Sustainability** of development is a new area of knowledge, in which scientists, economists, philosophers and other social scientists are working together.

> [!MISTAKE] "Higher income means higher development"
> Not always. Kerala has a lower per capita income than Haryana, yet has lower IMR and higher literacy. Income alone cannot measure development.

=== sectors-of-the-indian-economy ===
## The big idea
Millions of Indians do millions of different jobs: farming, weaving, teaching, driving, coding. Economists group these activities into **sectors** so that we can study how the economy is changing and where jobs are. This chapter classifies economic activities in three ways: **primary, secondary and tertiary**; **organised and unorganised**; and **public and private**. It also looks at **underemployment** and how to create more jobs.

## Primary, secondary and tertiary sectors
> [!KEY] The three sectors
> - **Primary sector**: activities undertaken by **directly using natural resources**: agriculture, dairy, fishing, forestry, mining. Since most natural products we get are from agriculture, dairy, fishing and forestry, this sector is also called **agriculture and related sector**.
> - **Secondary sector**: activities in which natural products are changed into other forms through ways of **manufacturing**. It is the next step after primary. The product is not produced by nature but has to be made, so some process of manufacturing is essential, in a factory, workshop or at home. Example: using cotton fibre to spin yarn and weave cloth; using sugarcane to make sugar or gur. Also called the **industrial sector**.
> - **Tertiary sector**: activities that **help in the development of the primary and secondary sectors**. These activities do not produce a good but are an aid or support for the production process: transport, storage, communication, banking, trade. Since these generate services rather than goods, it is also called the **service sector**. It also includes services like teachers, doctors, washermen, barbers, cobblers, lawyers, and new IT-based services like internet cafés, ATM booths, call centres and software companies.

**Interdependence**: the sectors depend on each other. If farmers refuse to sell sugarcane to a particular sugar mill, the mill will shut down (secondary depends on primary). If a company decides not to buy cotton from India, farmers will be affected. Transport and storage (tertiary) connect them all.

## Comparing the three sectors: GDP
> [!KEY] Final goods, intermediate goods and GDP
> - **Final goods**: goods that reach the consumer (e.g., biscuits).
> - **Intermediate goods**: goods used up in producing final goods (e.g., wheat and flour used in biscuits). Their value is already included in the final good's value.
> - To avoid **double counting**, only the value of final goods and services is counted.
> - **Gross Domestic Product (GDP)**: the **value of all final goods and services produced within a country during a particular year**. It shows how big the economy is. In India, the task of measuring GDP is undertaken by a central government ministry (the **Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation**, through its National Statistical Office, earlier the Central Statistical Organisation), with help from state and union territory departments.

**Historical change in sectors.** In the early stages of development of most countries, the **primary sector** was the most important. As methods of farming changed and agriculture prospered, more food was produced; people took up other activities, and the **secondary sector** gradually became more important (industrialisation). In the past 100 years, there has been a further shift from secondary to **tertiary** in developed countries; the service sector has become the most important in terms of total production and employment.

**India**: over the forty years between 1973–74 and 2013–14, production increased in all three sectors, but it increased the most in the tertiary sector. As a result, in **2013–14**, the **tertiary sector emerged as the largest producing sector** in India, replacing the primary sector.

> [!KEY] Why is the tertiary sector growing so fast?
> 1. Basic services such as **hospitals, educational institutions, post and telegraph, police stations, courts, village administrative offices, municipal corporations, defence, transport, banks and insurance** are required in any country; the government takes responsibility for providing them.
> 2. **Development of agriculture and industry** leads to development of services like transport, trade and storage.
> 3. As income levels rise, people demand more services: **eating out, tourism, shopping, private hospitals, private schools, professional training**.
> 4. Over the past decade, new services based on **information and communication technology** have become important and essential.
> But not all of the service sector is growing equally: it includes highly skilled, well-paid workers as well as a very large number of workers in small shops, repair shops and transport, who barely manage to earn a living.

**Where are most people employed?** Even though the share of the primary sector in GDP has fallen sharply, it remains the **largest employer**. In 2017–18, nearly **44%** of workers were in the primary sector, while it produced only about a sixth of GDP. The secondary and tertiary sectors produce most of the output but employ fewer people. Not enough jobs were created in the secondary and tertiary sectors.

## Underemployment (disguised unemployment)
> [!KEY] Disguised unemployment
> A situation where **more people are working than are needed**, so that if some workers are removed, total output does not fall. Each person seems to be working, but no one is fully employed; their labour effort is divided. Example: a small farm needs only three people, but five family members work on it; removing two would not reduce production. This is **hidden**, unlike someone clearly without a job. It is common in **agriculture**, and also in the service sector in urban areas (e.g., painters, plumbers, repair persons, street vendors who search for work daily).

**How to create more employment:**
- Provide **cheap credit** and build **wells, check dams and canals** for irrigation in villages; more land can be cultivated, and dams create jobs.
- Invest in **transport and storage**: roads and rural cold storage help farmers sell produce to markets and create jobs in transport and trade.
- Set up **agro-processing industries** (e.g., dal mills, cold storage, honey collection centres, vegetable and fruit processing) in semi-rural areas.
- Promote **tourism, regional crafts, IT** and other services; every state can develop these.
- Expand **education and health** services, which need many teachers and health workers.

> [!KEY] MGNREGA 2005
> The central government made a law implementing the **Right to Work** in about **625 districts** of India (NCERT figure): the **Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act, 2005**. Under it, all those who are able to and need work in rural areas are **guaranteed 100 days of employment in a year** by the government. If the government fails to provide employment, it will give **unemployment allowance** to the people. The types of work that would in future help increase production from land are given preference. **One-third of the proposed jobs are reserved for women**.

## Organised and unorganised sectors
| Organised sector | Unorganised sector |
|---|---|
| Enterprises or places of work where the **terms of employment are regular**, so people have **assured work** | Small and scattered units **largely outside the control of the government** |
| **Registered** by the government and have to follow its rules and regulations (Factories Act, Minimum Wages Act, Payment of Gratuity Act, Shops and Establishments Act) | Rules and regulations exist but are **not followed** |
| Workers enjoy **job security**, fixed working hours, **overtime pay**, **paid leave**, payment during holidays, provident fund, gratuity, medical benefits, safe working environment and pension after retirement | Jobs are **low-paid and often not regular**; no provision for overtime, paid leave, holidays or leave due to sickness; **no job security**; workers can be asked to leave without any reason |
| Example: government employees, workers in registered factories and large companies | Example: daily wage labourers, small shopkeepers, street vendors, domestic workers, workers in small workshops |

**Protecting workers in the unorganised sector.** Since the 1990s, many workers have lost jobs in the organised sector, so they take up jobs in the unorganised sector with low earnings. Besides the need for more work, workers in the unorganised sector need **protection and support**:
- In **rural areas**, the unorganised sector mostly comprises **landless agricultural labourers, small and marginal farmers, sharecroppers and artisans** (weavers, blacksmiths, carpenters, goldsmiths). They need support through timely delivery of seeds, agricultural inputs, credit, storage facilities and marketing outlets.
- In **urban areas**, it comprises workers in small-scale industry, casual workers in construction, trade and transport, street vendors, head-load workers, garment makers, rag pickers. Small-scale industry needs government support for procuring raw material and marketing output.
- Casual workers in both rural and urban areas need protection.
- A majority of workers from **Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and backward communities** find themselves in the unorganised sector, facing social discrimination too. Protection and support are needed for both economic and social development.

## Public and private sectors
| Public sector | Private sector |
|---|---|
| The **government owns most of the assets** and provides all the services | **Ownership of assets and delivery of services** is in the hands of **private individuals or companies** |
| Motive is **not just to earn profits** but **public welfare** | Activities are guided by the **motive to earn profits** |
| Examples: **Indian Railways, post office**, BHEL, SAIL | Examples: **Tata Iron and Steel Company Ltd (TISCO), Reliance Industries Ltd (RIL)** |

**Why the government must provide some services:**
- There are several things society needs that the **private sector will not provide at a reasonable cost** because of the huge spending needed: construction of **roads, bridges, railways, harbours, generating electricity, providing irrigation through dams**. The government raises money through taxes and undertakes such heavy spending, ensuring facilities are available for everyone.
- Some activities need government support even if private industries can do them: e.g., selling **electricity at low rates** helps small-scale industries. The government buys **wheat and rice from farmers at a 'fair price'**, stores them in godowns and sells them at a lower price to consumers through **ration shops**, bearing part of the cost.
- Providing **health and education facilities** for all; running proper schools and providing quality education, particularly elementary education. India's large number of children dropping out and the need for quality health facilities in rural areas make this essential. The government also pays attention to **human development** aspects such as availability of safe drinking water, housing for the poor, food and nutrition.

> [!MISTAKE] Classifying by ownership vs by nature of activity
> Primary/secondary/tertiary is based on the **nature of activity**. Organised/unorganised is based on **conditions of employment**. Public/private is based on **ownership of assets**. Don't mix up the criteria in classification questions.

## Quick recap

- GDP: value of final goods and services produced within a country in a year; avoid double counting.

=== money-and-credit ===
## The big idea
Money is something we use every day, but have you wondered **why** people accept a piece of paper in exchange for goods? This chapter explains **money as a medium of exchange**, **modern forms of money** (currency and bank deposits), how **banks** create **credit**, and why access to cheap and fair **credit** is crucial for development, especially for the poor, through **formal sources** and **Self-Help Groups**.

## Money as a medium of exchange
In a **barter system**, goods are directly exchanged without the use of money. A shoe manufacturer who wants wheat must find a wheat farmer who not only sells wheat but also wants to buy shoes. Both parties must agree to sell and buy each other's commodities.

> [!KEY] Double coincidence of wants
> What a person desires to sell is **exactly what the other wishes to buy**. In a barter system, this is essential and very hard to find. Money **eliminates the need for double coincidence of wants** by acting as an intermediate in the exchange process. That is why money is called a **medium of exchange**.

## Modern forms of money
Before coins, a variety of objects were used as money: **grains and cattle**, then **metallic coins** of gold, silver and copper. Modern forms of money include **currency (paper notes and coins)** and **deposits with banks**.

> [!KEY] Why is currency accepted?
> Modern currency is not made of precious metal and has no use of its own. It is accepted as a medium of exchange because it is **authorised by the government** of the country. In India, the **Reserve Bank of India (RBI)** issues currency notes on behalf of the **central government**. As per Indian law, no other individual or organisation is allowed to issue currency. The law **legalises the use of rupee** as a medium of payment that **cannot be refused** in settling transactions in India. No individual in India can legally refuse a payment made in rupees.

**Deposits with banks.** People deposit extra cash with banks by opening an account in their name. Banks accept deposits and also **pay an amount as interest** on them. Since deposits in bank accounts can be withdrawn on demand, they are called **demand deposits**. Demand deposits share the essential features of money: they can be used for payments through **cheques**.

> [!KEY] Cheque
> A **paper instructing the bank to pay a specific amount from the person's account to the person in whose name the cheque has been issued**. The facility of cheques against demand deposits makes it possible to directly settle payments without the use of cash. Today, digital modes (debit cards, UPI, net banking) also transfer demand deposits.

## Loan activities of banks
Banks keep only a small proportion of their deposits as **cash** with themselves; in India, banks hold about **15 per cent** of their deposits as cash. This is kept as provision to pay depositors who might come to withdraw money on any given day. Since only some depositors come to withdraw cash at a time, banks can manage with this cash. Banks use the **major portion of deposits to extend loans**. There is a huge demand for loans for various economic activities.

**Banks mediate between those who have surplus funds (depositors) and those who need funds (borrowers).** Banks charge a **higher interest rate on loans** than what they offer on deposits. The difference between the two is the **main source of income for banks**.

## Two different credit situations
> [!KEY] Credit (loan)
> An **agreement in which the lender supplies the borrower with money, goods or services in return for the promise of future payment**.

> [!EXAMPLE] Credit as a support: Salim
> **Salim**, a shoe manufacturer, gets an order from a large trader in town for 3,000 pairs of shoes to be delivered in a month. He obtains supplies of leather on credit and promises to pay later, and gets an advance payment from the trader. He completes the order, makes a good profit and repays. Here, credit plays a **vital and positive role**: it helps him meet ongoing expenses of production, complete production on time and increase earnings.

> [!EXAMPLE] Debt trap: Swapna
> **Swapna**, a small farmer, takes a loan from a moneylender for her groundnut crop. The crop is hit by pests and fails. She cannot repay, the debt grows with high interest, and she has to sell part of her land to repay. Here, credit pushes her into a **debt trap**: a situation in which the borrower is left in a worse condition than before.

Whether credit is useful or not depends on the **risks in the situation** and whether there is some support in case of loss.

## Terms of credit
Every loan agreement specifies:
- **Interest rate**: the rate at which the borrower must pay the lender along with repayment of the principal.
- **Collateral**: an **asset that the borrower owns** (land, building, vehicle, livestock, deposits with banks) and uses as a **guarantee to a lender until the loan is repaid**. If the borrower fails to repay, the lender has the right to sell the asset to obtain payment.
- **Documentation** required.
- **Mode of repayment**.

Terms of credit vary substantially from one credit arrangement to another, depending on the nature of the lender and the borrower.

## Formal and informal sources of credit
| Formal sources | Informal sources |
|---|---|
| **Banks and cooperatives** | **Moneylenders, traders, employers, relatives and friends** |
| **Supervised by the RBI**, which monitors that banks maintain the minimum cash balance and give loans not just to profit-making businesses but also to small cultivators, small-scale industries, small borrowers | **No organisation supervises** their credit activities |
| Lower interest rates | They can lend at **whatever interest rate they choose**; no one stops them from using **unfair means** to get money back |
| Need collateral and documentation | Often no collateral needed, but higher cost |

> [!KEY] Why the formal sector should expand
> - Higher cost of informal borrowing means a **larger part of earnings goes to repaying loans**, leaving less income for the borrower; in some cases, the interest exceeds the borrower's income, leading to a **debt trap**.
> - Cheap and affordable credit is crucial for the country's development: it allows people to grow crops, do business, set up small-scale industries and invest in new industries.
> - Banks and cooperatives need to lend more, particularly in rural areas, so that dependence on informal sources reduces.
> - Formal credit is currently distributed unequally: **rich households** get most of their credit from **formal sources**, while **poor households** depend largely on **informal sources**. So it is important that formal credit is **equally distributed** and reaches the poor.

**Why don't poor households get bank loans?** Banks are not present everywhere in rural India; getting a bank loan is difficult because it requires proper documents and **collateral**. Absence of collateral is one of the major reasons why banks are not willing to lend to the poor. Moneylenders know borrowers personally and are often willing to give loans without collateral; the borrower can approach them even without repaying earlier loans, but at very high rates of interest.

## Self-Help Groups for the poor
> [!KEY] Self-Help Groups (SHGs)
> - A typical SHG has **15–20 members**, usually belonging to one neighbourhood, who meet and **save regularly**. Savings per member vary from ₹25 to ₹100 or more, depending on ability.
> - Members can take **small loans from the group itself** to meet their needs. The group charges interest on these loans, still less than what the moneylender charges.
> - **After a year or two**, if the group is regular in savings, it becomes **eligible for availing loan from the bank**. The loan is sanctioned in the **name of the group** and is meant to create self-employment opportunities for members (e.g., small loans to release mortgaged land, for working capital, housing, buying assets like sewing machines, handlooms, cattle).
> - **Decisions** regarding savings and loan activities are taken by the **group members**. The group decides the loans to be granted, purpose, amount, interest, repayment schedule. The group is responsible for repayment; any case of non-repayment is followed up by other members.
> **Benefits**: helps borrowers **overcome the problem of lack of collateral**; gives timely loans for a variety of purposes at a reasonable interest rate; organises rural poor, especially **women**; makes women financially self-reliant; regular meetings provide a platform to discuss and act on social issues like health, nutrition, domestic violence.

> [!EXAMPLE] Grameen Bank of Bangladesh
> Started in the 1970s as a small project, the **Grameen Bank** of Bangladesh lends to the poor, mostly **women**, at reasonable rates without collateral. Its founder, **Professor Muhammad Yunus**, received the **Nobel Peace Prize in 2006**. It shows that the poor are creditworthy: they can be trusted to repay loans.

> [!MISTAKE] Collateral is not the interest
> Collateral is an **asset pledged as a guarantee**; interest is the **extra amount paid** on the loan. Don't confuse the two in "terms of credit" answers.

## Quick recap
- Money solves the double coincidence of wants; it is a medium of exchange.
- Modern money: currency (issued by RBI on behalf of the government; legal tender) and demand deposits (cheques).
- Banks keep about 15% of deposits as cash and lend the rest; interest difference is their income.
- Credit can help (Salim) or create a debt trap (Swapna).
- Terms of credit: interest, collateral, documentation, mode of repayment.
- Formal (banks, cooperatives, RBI-supervised) vs informal (moneylenders, traders, relatives).
- SHGs (15–20 members) and Grameen Bank (Muhammad Yunus, Nobel 2006) extend credit to the poor without collateral.

=== globalisation-and-the-indian-economy ===
## The big idea
Your phone may be designed in the USA, assembled in China and sold in India; your shoes may be made in Vietnam for a European brand. Markets and production across countries are becoming **interconnected**. This process is **globalisation**. This chapter explains the role of **multinational corporations (MNCs)**, **foreign trade**, the **factors** that enabled globalisation, the role of the **WTO**, and the effects of globalisation on India, along with the need for **fair globalisation**.

## Production across countries and MNCs
Until the middle of the twentieth century, production was largely organised within countries; what crossed boundaries were raw materials, food and finished products. Colonies exported raw materials and imported finished goods. Trade was the main channel connecting distant countries.

> [!KEY] Multinational Corporation (MNC)
> A company that **owns or controls production in more than one nation**. MNCs set up offices and factories for production in regions where they can get **cheap labour and other resources**, so that the cost of production is low and they earn greater profits.

**Example of spreading production**: a large MNC producing industrial equipment designs its products in research centres in the **USA**, has components manufactured in **China**, ships them to **Mexico and Eastern Europe** where products are assembled, and sells them all over the world; its customer care is carried out through call centres in **India**.

**Where MNCs set up production**: close to **markets**; where there is **skilled and unskilled labour at low cost**; and where availability of other factors of production is assured. They also look for **government policies** that look after their interests.

## Interlinking production across countries
> [!KEY] Investment and foreign investment
> - **Investment**: money spent to buy assets such as land, building, machines and other equipment.
> - **Foreign investment**: investment made by MNCs.

MNCs interlink production in several ways:
1. **Joint production with local companies**: the MNC provides money for additional investment (e.g., faster machines) and brings the latest technology. Example: **Ford Motors**, an American company, came to India in **1995** and spent ₹1,700 crore to set up a large plant near **Chennai** in collaboration with **Mahindra and Mahindra**, a major Indian manufacturer of jeeps and trucks.
2. **Buying up local companies**: the most common route to quick expansion. Example: **Cargill Foods**, a very large American MNC, bought over the smaller Indian company **Parakh Foods**, which had built a large marketing network and a well-respected brand. Cargill became the largest producer of edible oil in India.
3. **Placing orders for production with small producers**: e.g., garments, footwear and sports items are produced by small producers around the world, and the products are supplied to MNCs, which sell them under their own **brand names** to customers. These large MNCs have tremendous power to determine **price, quality, delivery and labour conditions** for these distant producers.

## Foreign trade and integration of markets
**Foreign trade** creates an opportunity for producers to reach **beyond the domestic markets**. Producers can sell their produce not only in markets located within the country but also compete in markets in other countries. Similarly, for buyers, imports of goods from another country expand choice beyond domestically produced goods.

> [!EXAMPLE] Chinese toys in India
> Chinese manufacturers learnt of an opportunity to export toys to India, where toys were sold at a high price. Plastic toys from China, being cheaper and of new designs, became popular in Indian markets. Within a year, 70 to 80 per cent of the toy shops replaced Indian toys with Chinese toys. **Buyers** got more choice at lower prices; **Chinese toy makers** expanded business; but **Indian toy makers** faced losses as their toys sold much less.

**Effect of foreign trade**: as a result, **prices of similar goods in two markets tend to become equal**, and producers in the two countries now closely compete against each other even though they are separated by thousands of miles. **Foreign trade thus results in connecting the markets** or integration of markets in different countries.

> [!KEY] Globalisation
> The process of **rapid integration or interconnection between countries**. MNCs play a major role. More and more goods and services, investments and technology are moving between countries; there is also some (but restricted) movement of people between countries in search of better income, jobs or education.

## Factors that have enabled globalisation
1. **Technology**: rapid improvement in **transportation technology** has made much faster delivery of goods across long distances possible at lower costs (e.g., containers for transport of goods). Even more remarkable are developments in **information and communication technology**: telecommunications (telegraph, telephone, mobile phones, fax), computers and the **internet** make it easy to contact one another around the world, access information instantly and communicate from remote areas. For example, a news magazine in London can be designed and printed in Delhi, with text sent over the internet.
2. **Liberalisation of foreign trade and foreign investment policy**: a **trade barrier** is a restriction set up by the government, e.g., a **tax on imports**. Governments use trade barriers to increase or decrease (regulate) foreign trade and to decide what kinds of goods and how much of each should come into the country. After independence, the Indian government put barriers to foreign trade and investment to **protect producers** within the country from foreign competition; industries were just coming up in the 1950s and 1960s. Starting around **1991**, some far-reaching changes in policy were made in India: the government decided that the time had come for Indian producers to compete with producers around the globe. Barriers on foreign trade and investment were removed to a large extent. **Removing barriers or restrictions set by the government is called liberalisation.**
3. **World Trade Organisation (WTO)**: an organisation whose aim is to **liberalise international trade**. Started at the initiative of the developed countries, the WTO establishes **rules regarding international trade** and sees that these rules are obeyed. About **164 countries** of the world are currently members of the WTO (as of 2016). Although the WTO is supposed to allow free trade for all, in practice the **developed countries have unfairly retained trade barriers**. For example, developing countries are forced to remove barriers, while the **US government gives massive subsidies to its farmers** (e.g., cotton farmers), so they can sell at low prices, harming farmers in developing countries. Developing countries are therefore demanding fair trade rules.

## Impact of globalisation in India
**Positive effects:**
- Greater **choice for consumers**, who now enjoy improved quality and lower prices for several products, especially the well-off sections in urban areas.
- **MNCs have increased their investments** in India over the past 20 years: industries such as **cellphones, automobiles, electronics, soft drinks, fast food** and services such as **banking** in urban areas. New jobs have been created; local companies supplying raw materials to these industries have prospered.
- Several top Indian companies benefited from increased competition: they invested in newer technology and production methods and raised production standards; some gained from successful collaborations with foreign companies.
- Some large Indian companies have emerged as **multinationals** themselves: **Tata Motors** (automobiles), **Infosys** (IT), **Ranbaxy** (medicines), **Asian Paints** (paints), **Sundaram Fasteners** (nuts and bolts) are spreading operations worldwide.
- New opportunities for companies providing **services**, especially those involving IT: e.g., an Indian company producing a magazine for a London company, and call centres.

**Special Economic Zones (SEZs)**: industrial zones set up by the government to attract foreign companies to invest in India. They have world-class facilities: electricity, water, roads, transport, storage, recreational and educational facilities. Companies that set up production units in SEZs do not have to pay taxes for an initial period of five years. The government has also allowed **flexibility in labour laws** to attract foreign investment.

**Negative effects / challenges:**
- Globalisation and the pressure of competition have substantially changed the lives of workers. Faced with growing competition, most employers now prefer to employ workers **flexibly**: workers' jobs are no longer secure.
- **Small producers** have been hit hard by rising competition. Several units shut down, rendering many workers jobless. Industries such as **batteries, capacitors, plastics, toys, tyres, dairy products and vegetable oil** are examples where small manufacturers were badly hit.
- Benefits have **not been shared equally**: well-off, educated, skilled and wealthy producers gained, while many small producers and workers suffered.

> [!EXAMPLE] Garment workers
> A garment exporter facing competition from MNC buyers cuts labour costs: workers are employed on a **temporary basis**, work long hours (including night shifts during peak season), get **low wages**, and have no job security, while the MNC earns large profits.

## The struggle for fair globalisation
> [!KEY] Fair globalisation
> Globalisation that creates **opportunities for all** and ensures that its **benefits are better shared**. The government can play a major role:
> - Ensure that **labour laws are properly implemented** and workers get their rights.
> - **Support small producers** to improve their performance until they become strong enough to compete.
> - If necessary, use **trade and investment barriers**.
> - **Negotiate at the WTO for 'fairer rules'** and align with other developing countries to fight against the domination of developed countries in the WTO.
> People's organisations and campaigns have also influenced important decisions related to trade and investment at the WTO.

> [!MISTAKE] Liberalisation vs globalisation
> **Liberalisation** is the removal of government restrictions on trade and investment (a policy, from 1991 in India). **Globalisation** is the wider process of integration between countries; liberalisation is one of the **factors** that enabled it.

## Quick recap
- MNC: owns or controls production in more than one nation; seeks cheap labour, markets and supportive policies.
- MNCs interlink production via joint ventures (Ford with Mahindra, 1995), buying local firms (Cargill bought Parakh), and orders to small producers.
- Foreign trade integrates markets; prices tend to become equal across countries.
- Factors: technology (transport, IT), liberalisation (India from 1991), WTO (about 164 members).
- Impact: more choice, new jobs, Indian MNCs (Tata Motors, Infosys); but small producers and workers hit, insecure jobs.
