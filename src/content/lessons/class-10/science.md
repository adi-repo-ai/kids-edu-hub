=== chemical-reactions-and-equations ===
## The big idea
In a **chemical reaction**, substances (reactants) change into new substances (products) with new properties. You can tell a reaction has happened from clues: a **change in state, colour or temperature**, **evolution of a gas**, or **formation of a precipitate**.

> [!EXAMPLE] Everyday reactions
> Milk turning sour, iron rusting, food being digested, magnesium ribbon burning with a dazzling white flame to form white magnesium oxide powder.

## Writing and balancing equations
A **chemical equation** shows a reaction with symbols and formulae: Mg + O₂ → MgO. This is a **skeletal** equation; it is not balanced.

> [!KEY] Law of conservation of mass
> Mass can neither be created nor destroyed in a reaction, so the number of atoms of **each element** must be the same on both sides. Balancing makes this true: **2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO**.

How to balance (hit-and-trial): balance the compound with the most atoms first, then the remaining elements one at a time, never changing the formulae, only the **coefficients** in front. Add state symbols: (s), (l), (g), (aq).

> [!EXAMPLE] Balance Fe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂
> Balance O: 4H₂O. Then H: 4H₂. Then Fe: 3Fe.
> **3Fe(s) + 4H₂O(g) → Fe₃O₄(s) + 4H₂(g)**

## Types of reactions
| Type | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Combination | A + B → AB | CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ (slaked lime; very exothermic) |
| Decomposition | AB → A + B | 2FeSO₄ → Fe₂O₃ + SO₂ + SO₃ (heat); 2AgCl → 2Ag + Cl₂ (sunlight) |
| Displacement | A + BC → AC + B | Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu (blue solution turns green) |
| Double displacement | AB + CD → AD + CB | Na₂SO₄ + BaCl₂ → BaSO₄↓ + 2NaCl (white precipitate) |

Decomposition needs energy: **heat** (thermal), **light** (photolytic, used in black-and-white photography) or **electricity** (electrolysis of water gives H₂ and O₂ in a 2 : 1 volume ratio).

**Exothermic** reactions release heat (respiration, burning). **Endothermic** reactions absorb heat (decomposition of calcium carbonate).

## Oxidation and reduction
> [!KEY] Redox
> **Oxidation**: gain of oxygen or loss of hydrogen. **Reduction**: loss of oxygen or gain of hydrogen.
> In CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O, CuO is **reduced** to Cu and H₂ is **oxidised** to H₂O. The substance that gets oxidised is the reducing agent.

## Oxidation in daily life
- **Corrosion**: metals attacked by moisture, air and acids. Rusting of iron (reddish-brown), black coating on silver, green coating on copper.
- **Rancidity**: oxidation of fats and oils gives food a bad smell and taste. Prevented by adding antioxidants, flushing chip packets with **nitrogen**, keeping food airtight or refrigerated.

> [!MISTAKE] Changing subscripts while balancing
> Writing H₂O₂ instead of 2H₂O changes the substance. Only change the numbers **in front**.

## Quick recap
- Balanced equations obey conservation of mass.
- Combination, decomposition, displacement, double displacement.
- Oxidation = gain O / lose H; reduction = lose O / gain H.
- Corrosion and rancidity are everyday oxidation.

=== acids-bases-and-salts ===
## The big idea
**Acids** taste sour and turn blue litmus red; **bases** taste bitter, feel soapy and turn red litmus blue. When they react, they cancel each other in **neutralisation**, forming a **salt and water**.

## Indicators
| Indicator | In acid | In base |
|---|---|---|
| Blue litmus | Red | Blue |
| Red litmus | Red | Blue |
| Phenolphthalein | Colourless | Pink |
| Methyl orange | Red | Yellow |
| Turmeric | Yellow | Reddish-brown |

**Olfactory indicators** change smell: onion and vanilla lose their smell in a base.

## How acids and bases react
> [!KEY] Key reactions
> Acid + metal → salt + **hydrogen** (the gas burns with a pop sound)
> Acid + metal carbonate/hydrogencarbonate → salt + water + **carbon dioxide** (turns lime water milky)
> Acid + base → salt + water (**neutralisation**)
> Acid + metal oxide → salt + water (metal oxides are basic)
> Base (NaOH) + zinc → sodium zincate + hydrogen

Acids produce **H⁺ (H₃O⁺) ions** in water; bases (alkalis, if soluble) produce **OH⁻ ions**. That is why both conduct electricity in water but dry HCl gas doesn't change dry litmus.

> [!MISTAKE] Diluting an acid
> Always add **acid to water** slowly, with stirring. Adding water to concentrated acid releases so much heat that the mixture can splash out.

## The pH scale
pH runs from **0 to 14**. pH 7 is neutral, below 7 acidic, above 7 basic. The lower the pH, the stronger the acid; the higher the pH, the stronger the base. A **universal indicator** shows different colours across the scale.

> [!EXAMPLE] pH in everyday life
> Our body works in a narrow range of 7.0–7.8. Tooth decay starts below pH 5.5, so toothpaste (basic) neutralises mouth acid. Antacids (milk of magnesia) neutralise excess stomach acid. Bee stings (methanoic acid) are treated with baking soda. Plants need soil of a suitable pH.

## Important salts
| Salt | Formula | How it's made / used |
|---|---|---|
| Common salt | NaCl | From sea water; raw material for many chemicals |
| Sodium hydroxide | NaOH | Chlor-alkali process: electrolysis of brine gives NaOH, Cl₂, H₂ |
| Bleaching powder | CaOCl₂ | Cl₂ + dry slaked lime; bleaching, disinfecting water |
| Baking soda | NaHCO₃ | Baking (releases CO₂), antacid, soda-acid fire extinguishers |
| Washing soda | Na₂CO₃·10H₂O | Recrystallised sodium carbonate; removes hardness of water, cleaning |
| Plaster of Paris | CaSO₄·½H₂O | Heating gypsum at 373 K; plasters for fractures, toys, decoration |

**Water of crystallisation**: fixed water molecules in a salt crystal. Blue copper sulphate (CuSO₄·5H₂O) turns white when heated as it loses this water.

## Quick recap
- Acids give H⁺, bases give OH⁻; acid + base → salt + water.
- pH < 7 acidic, 7 neutral, > 7 basic.
- Know the formula, preparation and uses of the six key salts.

=== metals-and-non-metals ===
## The big idea
Elements can be sorted as **metals** (iron, copper, sodium…) and **non-metals** (carbon, sulphur, oxygen…). Their properties, and how readily they react, decide where we use them and how we extract them from the earth.

## Physical properties
| Property | Metals | Non-metals |
|---|---|---|
| Lustre | Shiny | Dull (except iodine) |
| Malleable / ductile | Yes (gold, silver most) | No, brittle solids |
| Conduct heat and electricity | Yes (silver and copper best) | No (except graphite) |
| State | Solids (except mercury) | Solids, liquid (bromine) or gases |
| Sonorous | Yes | No |

Exceptions to remember: sodium and potassium are soft (cut with a knife); gallium and caesium melt in your palm; diamond (carbon) is the hardest natural substance.

## Chemical properties
> [!KEY] How metals react
> Metal + oxygen → metal oxide (basic). **Amphoteric** oxides like Al₂O₃ and ZnO react with both acids and bases.
> Metal + water → metal hydroxide (or oxide) + hydrogen. Na and K react violently with cold water; Mg with hot water; Al, Fe, Zn only with steam; Cu, Ag, Au not at all.
> Metal + dilute acid → salt + hydrogen (not Cu, Ag, Au).
> A more reactive metal **displaces** a less reactive one from its salt solution.

## The reactivity series
**K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > [H] > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au**

Metals above hydrogen displace it from dilute acids. Non-metal oxides are **acidic** (SO₂, CO₂).

## Ionic compounds
Metals lose electrons and non-metals gain them, forming ions that attract strongly. Example: Na (2,8,1) gives one electron to Cl (2,8,7) to form Na⁺Cl⁻.

> [!KEY] Properties of ionic compounds
> Hard solids with **high melting and boiling points**, usually soluble in water but not in kerosene or petrol, and they **conduct electricity when molten or dissolved** (ions are free to move), not as solids.

## Extraction of metals (metallurgy)
1. **Concentration of ore**: remove gangue (sand, rock).
2. **Low reactivity** metals (Hg, Cu): heat the sulphide ore in air.
3. **Medium reactivity** (Zn, Fe, Pb): sulphide → oxide by **roasting**; carbonate → oxide by **calcination**; then reduce with carbon: ZnO + C → Zn + CO. Highly reactive Al can reduce oxides in the **thermite reaction**: Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → 2Fe + Al₂O₃ (used to weld railway tracks).
4. **High reactivity** (Na, Mg, Ca, Al): **electrolysis** of the molten chloride or oxide.
5. **Refining**: electrolytic refining, with impure metal as anode and pure metal as cathode.

## Corrosion and its prevention
Rusting needs **both air and water**. Prevent it by painting, oiling, greasing, **galvanising** (coating with zinc) or making **alloys**: stainless steel (iron + nickel + chromium), brass (copper + zinc), bronze (copper + tin), solder (lead + tin, low melting point).

## Quick recap
- Metals: lustrous, malleable, ductile, good conductors, form basic oxides.
- Reactivity series decides displacement and extraction method.
- Ionic compounds: high melting point, conduct when molten or in solution.
- Prevent corrosion by coating, galvanising and alloying.

=== carbon-and-its-compounds ===
## The big idea
Carbon forms millions of compounds: fuels, plastics, medicines and the molecules of life. It does this because of **covalent bonding**, **catenation** and **tetravalency**.

## Covalent bonds
Carbon (atomic number 6, configuration 2, 4) can neither gain 4 nor lose 4 electrons easily, so it **shares** electrons. A **covalent bond** is a shared pair of electrons.

Examples: H₂ (single bond), O₂ (double bond), N₂ (triple bond), CH₄ (four single bonds). Covalent compounds have **low melting and boiling points** and are **poor conductors** of electricity.

## Why carbon is special
> [!KEY] Versatility of carbon
> **Catenation**: carbon atoms link with other carbon atoms to form long chains, branched chains and rings.
> **Tetravalency**: a valency of 4 lets carbon bond with four other atoms (C, H, O, N, S, Cl…).
> Carbon–carbon bonds are very strong, making these compounds stable.

**Allotropes** of carbon: diamond (each C bonded to 4 others; hardest), graphite (layers of hexagons; conducts electricity, slippery), fullerene C₆₀ (football shape).

## Saturated and unsaturated compounds
- **Saturated** (single bonds only): alkanes, CₙH₂ₙ₊₂, e.g. ethane C₂H₆.
- **Unsaturated** (double or triple bonds): alkenes CₙH₂ₙ (ethene C₂H₄), alkynes CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ (ethyne C₂H₂).

**Homologous series**: compounds with the same functional group whose successive members differ by **–CH₂–** (14 u). They have similar chemical properties and gradually changing physical properties.

| Functional group | Suffix/prefix | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Halogen (–Cl, –Br) | chloro-, bromo- | chloropropane |
| Alcohol (–OH) | -ol | propanol |
| Aldehyde (–CHO) | -al | propanal |
| Ketone (>C=O) | -one | propanone |
| Carboxylic acid (–COOH) | -oic acid | propanoic acid |

## Chemical properties
- **Combustion**: carbon compounds burn in oxygen giving CO₂, H₂O, heat and light. Saturated compounds burn with a clean blue flame; unsaturated ones with a sooty yellow flame.
- **Oxidation**: alcohols → carboxylic acids using alkaline KMnO₄ or acidified K₂Cr₂O₇.
- **Addition**: unsaturated + H₂ (Ni/Pd catalyst) → saturated. Used in **hydrogenation of vegetable oils**.
- **Substitution**: saturated hydrocarbons + Cl₂ in sunlight; H atoms are replaced by Cl.

## Ethanol and ethanoic acid
- **Ethanol** (C₂H₅OH): the active ingredient of alcoholic drinks and a common solvent. Reacts with sodium to give hydrogen; with hot concentrated H₂SO₄ it dehydrates to ethene.
- **Ethanoic acid** (CH₃COOH): 5–8% solution is **vinegar**; pure acid freezes in winter (glacial acetic acid). Turns blue litmus red.

> [!KEY] Esterification
> Ethanoic acid + ethanol → (acid catalyst) **ester** (sweet-smelling, used in perfumes) + water.
> **Saponification**: ester + NaOH → alcohol + sodium salt of the acid (soap).

## Soaps and detergents
A soap molecule has an **ionic (hydrophilic) end** that dissolves in water and a **long hydrocarbon (hydrophobic) tail** that dissolves in oil. In water, soap molecules form **micelles**, trapping oily dirt in the centre so it can be rinsed away.

Soap forms **scum** with the calcium and magnesium salts of hard water; **detergents** don't, so they work in hard water.

## Quick recap
- Covalent bonding, catenation and tetravalency make carbon versatile.
- Homologous series differ by –CH₂–; functional groups decide properties.
- Combustion, oxidation, addition, substitution.
- Micelles explain how soap cleans.

=== life-processes ===
## The big idea
Living things keep themselves alive by carrying out **life processes**: nutrition, respiration, transportation and excretion. Even while you sleep, these processes keep repairing and maintaining your body.

## Nutrition
- **Autotrophic**: organisms make their own food. Green plants use **photosynthesis**:
  6CO₂ + 12H₂O → (sunlight, chlorophyll) → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ + 6H₂O
  Steps: chlorophyll absorbs light; light energy splits water into hydrogen and oxygen; CO₂ is reduced to carbohydrate. CO₂ enters through **stomata**, which open and close using **guard cells**.
- **Heterotrophic**: organisms depend on others for food. Amoeba engulfs food with **pseudopodia**; fungi break food down outside the body (saprophytic); some are parasites (Cuscuta, ticks).

> [!KEY] Human digestion
> **Mouth**: saliva has **salivary amylase**, which breaks starch into sugar.
> **Stomach**: HCl (acidic medium, kills germs), **pepsin** (digests protein), mucus (protects the lining).
> **Small intestine**: bile from the liver **emulsifies fats** and makes the medium alkaline; pancreatic juice has trypsin (proteins) and lipase (fats). **Villi** increase the surface area for absorption.
> **Large intestine**: absorbs water; the anus removes waste.

## Respiration
Glucose is broken down to release energy, stored as **ATP**.
- **Aerobic** (with oxygen, in mitochondria): glucose → CO₂ + water + lots of energy.
- **Anaerobic** in yeast: glucose → ethanol + CO₂ + energy (fermentation).
- **Lack of oxygen in muscles**: glucose → lactic acid + energy, which causes cramps.

The first step, glucose → pyruvate, happens in the **cytoplasm** in all cases.

Human respiratory path: nostrils → pharynx → larynx → trachea (rings of cartilage stop it collapsing) → bronchi → bronchioles → **alveoli**, where oxygen diffuses into blood. **Haemoglobin** in red blood cells carries oxygen.

## Transportation
> [!KEY] Double circulation
> Blood passes through the heart **twice** in each cycle: right side → lungs → left side → body. The four chambers keep oxygenated and deoxygenated blood separate, giving efficient oxygen supply for warm-blooded animals.

- **Arteries** carry blood away from the heart (thick, elastic walls). **Veins** bring blood back (valves stop backflow). **Capillaries** are one cell thick for exchange.
- **Platelets** help blood clot. **Lymph** carries absorbed fat and drains extra fluid.
- In plants, **xylem** carries water and minerals up (pulled by **transpiration**); **phloem** carries food (sugar) both ways (**translocation**, uses ATP).

## Excretion
The **kidneys** filter blood. Each kidney has about a million **nephrons**. In the nephron, the **glomerulus** (in Bowman's capsule) filters blood; useful substances (glucose, amino acids, salts, water) are **reabsorbed** in the tubule; the rest becomes urine, collected in the bladder.

When kidneys fail, **dialysis** (artificial kidney) filters the blood. Plants excrete by removing oxygen and water through stomata, storing waste in old leaves and bark, and resins and gums.

## Quick recap
- Nutrition: photosynthesis (plants), digestion with enzymes (humans).
- Respiration releases energy as ATP; aerobic or anaerobic.
- Heart: four chambers, double circulation; xylem and phloem in plants.
- Nephrons filter blood to form urine.

=== control-and-coordination ===
## The big idea
To survive, an organism must respond to changes around it. In animals, the **nervous system** and **hormones** coordinate these responses. Plants have no nerves, so they use **hormones** and growth movements.

## The nervous system
A **neuron** has dendrites (receive information), a cell body and an axon (carries the impulse away). Neurons meet at a **synapse**, where chemicals carry the signal across the gap.

> [!KEY] Reflex action
> A quick, automatic response to a stimulus, like pulling your hand off a hot plate. The path is the **reflex arc**: receptor → sensory neuron → **spinal cord** → motor neuron → effector (muscle). The spinal cord responds before the brain even knows.

## The human brain
| Part | Role |
|---|---|
| Forebrain (cerebrum) | Thinking, memory, voluntary actions, senses (sight, hearing, smell) |
| Midbrain | Involuntary actions like reflex movements of the head and eyes |
| Hindbrain: cerebellum | Balance, posture and precise voluntary movements (riding a cycle) |
| Hindbrain: medulla | Involuntary actions: blood pressure, salivation, vomiting |
| Hindbrain: pons | Regulates breathing |

The brain is protected by the skull and fluid; the spinal cord by the vertebral column.

## Coordination in plants
- **Immediate response**: touch-me-not (Mimosa) leaves fold when touched, by changing the water content of cells.
- **Growth movements (tropisms)**: phototropism (shoots grow towards light), geotropism (roots grow down), hydrotropism (towards water), chemotropism (pollen tube towards the ovule). Tendrils of climbers coil around support (thigmotropism).

> [!KEY] Plant hormones
> **Auxin**: made at the shoot tip; on the shaded side it makes cells grow longer, so the shoot bends towards light.
> **Gibberellins**: stem growth. **Cytokinins**: cell division (in fruits and seeds).
> **Abscisic acid**: inhibits growth; causes wilting of leaves.

## Hormones in animals
| Gland | Hormone | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Pituitary | Growth hormone | Body growth (too little → dwarfism, too much → gigantism) |
| Thyroid | Thyroxin | Metabolism; needs **iodine** (lack → goitre) |
| Pancreas | Insulin | Lowers blood sugar (lack → diabetes) |
| Adrenal | Adrenaline | "Fight or flight": faster heartbeat and breathing |
| Testes / ovaries | Testosterone / oestrogen | Changes at puberty |

**Feedback mechanism**: the timing and amount of hormones are controlled. For example, when blood sugar rises, the pancreas releases more insulin; when it falls, insulin secretion drops.

## Quick recap
- Nerve impulses travel neuron to neuron across synapses.
- Reflex arcs pass through the spinal cord for speed.
- Plant movement uses hormones such as auxin.
- Endocrine glands release hormones controlled by feedback.

=== how-do-organisms-reproduce ===
## The big idea
Reproduction makes more individuals of a species. The key event is **DNA copying**: the copies are almost, but not exactly, the same. These small **variations** help a population survive changes in the environment.

## Asexual reproduction (one parent)
| Mode | Example |
|---|---|
| Fission | Binary fission in Amoeba; multiple fission in Plasmodium |
| Fragmentation | Spirogyra breaks into pieces that grow |
| Regeneration | Planaria and Hydra regrow from pieces |
| Budding | Hydra, yeast |
| Vegetative propagation | Potato (stem), Bryophyllum (leaf buds), sugarcane, rose; by cutting, layering, grafting |
| Spore formation | Rhizopus (bread mould) spores in sporangia |

Vegetative propagation gives plants that bear fruit sooner and keeps desired traits exactly.

## Sexual reproduction in flowering plants
The flower is the reproductive part. **Stamens** (male) make pollen; the **pistil** (female) has stigma, style and ovary with ovules.

> [!KEY] From pollen to seed
> **Pollination**: transfer of pollen to the stigma (self or cross, by wind, water, insects).
> **Fertilisation**: a pollen tube grows down the style; the male germ cell fuses with the female germ cell in the ovule to form a **zygote**.
> Afterwards the ovule becomes the **seed** and the ovary becomes the **fruit**. The seed has an embryo that grows into a new plant (germination).

## Human reproduction
At **puberty** the body changes: in boys, voice deepens and facial hair grows; in girls, breasts develop and menstruation begins. These are controlled by hormones (testosterone, oestrogen).

- **Male**: testes make sperm and testosterone; sperm travel through the vas deferens, mixed with fluids from the seminal vesicles and prostate.
- **Female**: ovaries release one egg a month; it travels down the **oviduct** (where fertilisation happens) to the **uterus**. The embryo implants in the uterus wall and gets nourishment through the **placenta**. If there's no fertilisation, the uterus lining breaks down: **menstruation**.

## Reproductive health
Sexually transmitted diseases include gonorrhoea, syphilis (bacterial) and HIV-AIDS (viral). Contraceptive methods are mechanical (condoms), chemical (oral pills), surgical (vasectomy, tubectomy) and copper-T. Female foeticide (sex-selective abortion) is illegal in India, and maintaining a healthy sex ratio is important.

## Quick recap
- DNA copying creates variation, which helps species survive.
- Asexual: fission, fragmentation, regeneration, budding, vegetative propagation, spores.
- Plants: pollination → fertilisation → seed and fruit.
- Humans: fertilisation in the oviduct; embryo nourished via the placenta.

=== heredity ===
## The big idea
Children resemble their parents but are never exact copies. **Heredity** is the passing of traits from parents to offspring; **variation** is the difference between individuals. Gregor Mendel worked out the rules using garden peas.

## Mendel's experiments
Mendel chose pea plants with clear contrasting traits: tall/short, round/wrinkled seeds, violet/white flowers.

> [!EXAMPLE] Monohybrid cross: tall × short
> Parents: TT (tall) × tt (short). **F₁**: all Tt, all **tall**. Tall is **dominant**, short is **recessive**.
> Self-cross F₁ (Tt × Tt): **F₂** genotypes TT : Tt : tt = **1 : 2 : 1**; phenotypes tall : short = **3 : 1**.
> The short trait reappears in F₂, so it was hidden, not lost.

> [!EXAMPLE] Dihybrid cross: round yellow × wrinkled green
> F₁: all round yellow. F₂ phenotype ratio **9 : 3 : 3 : 1** (round yellow : round green : wrinkled yellow : wrinkled green).
> New combinations appear, so the traits are **inherited independently**.

> [!KEY] Key words
> **Gene**: a section of DNA that codes for a protein, which controls a trait.
> Each trait is controlled by **two copies** of a gene, one from each parent. A **dominant** trait shows even if only one copy is present.
> **Genotype**: the genes (TT, Tt). **Phenotype**: what you see (tall).

How genes work: a gene for a growth hormone produces the enzyme. An efficient version means more hormone and a taller plant.

## Sex determination
In humans, women have two X chromosomes (**XX**) and men have one X and one Y (**XY**). Every egg carries an X. A sperm carries either X or Y. If an X sperm fertilises the egg, the child is a girl (XX); if a Y sperm does, a boy (XY). So **the father's sperm decides the sex of the child**.

In some animals the environment decides: in some reptiles, the incubation temperature of eggs determines the sex of the offspring.

> [!MISTAKE] Ratios are probabilities
> A 3 : 1 ratio means the chance of a tall plant is 3/4 for each offspring. It doesn't mean that exactly 3 out of every 4 plants will be tall in a small sample.

## Quick recap
- Traits come in pairs of genes, one from each parent.
- Monohybrid F₂: 3 : 1 (phenotype), 1 : 2 : 1 (genotype). Dihybrid F₂: 9 : 3 : 3 : 1.
- XX = female, XY = male; the father's sperm decides.

=== our-environment ===
## The big idea
An **ecosystem** is all the living things (**biotic**) in an area plus the non-living surroundings (**abiotic**: air, water, soil, temperature). Energy flows through it in one direction, and our actions can harm it.

## Food chains and webs
A **food chain** shows who eats whom: grass → deer → lion. Each step is a **trophic level**.

- **Producers** (plants) make food using sunlight.
- **Consumers**: herbivores (primary), carnivores (secondary, tertiary), omnivores.
- **Decomposers** (bacteria, fungi) break down dead matter and return nutrients to the soil.

Real ecosystems have **food webs**: many interlinked chains.

> [!KEY] The 10% law
> Only about **10%** of the energy at one trophic level passes on to the next; the rest is used for life processes or lost as heat. That's why food chains rarely have more than **3–4 levels**, and why there are fewer animals at higher levels.

> [!EXAMPLE] Energy through a chain
> If plants trap 10,000 J, herbivores get about 1,000 J, small carnivores 100 J and large carnivores only 10 J.

## Biological magnification
Some harmful chemicals (like pesticides) are **not biodegradable**. They enter the food chain and **accumulate**, becoming more concentrated at each trophic level. Humans, at the top of many chains, carry the highest concentration.

## The ozone layer
Ozone (O₃) high in the atmosphere **shields Earth from the Sun's ultraviolet (UV) radiation**, which can cause skin cancer. **Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs)** from refrigerants and fire extinguishers damage it. In 1987, an international agreement (under UNEP) aimed to freeze CFC production at 1986 levels.

## Managing waste
- **Biodegradable** waste (vegetable peels, paper, cotton) is broken down by microbes.
- **Non-biodegradable** waste (plastics, DDT, glass) persists and harms the environment.

Use the three Rs: **reduce, reuse, recycle**. Segregate waste, use cloth bags, compost kitchen waste.

## Quick recap
- Ecosystem = biotic + abiotic components.
- Energy flows one way; only about 10% passes to the next level.
- Non-biodegradable chemicals magnify up the food chain.
- CFCs deplete the ozone layer, which protects us from UV.

=== light-reflection-and-refraction ===
## The big idea
We see objects because light from them reaches our eyes. Light travels in straight lines, **bounces off** surfaces (reflection) and **bends** when it passes from one medium to another (refraction). Mirrors and lenses use these two behaviours to form images.

## Reflection and spherical mirrors
> [!KEY] Laws of reflection
> 1. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.
> 2. The incident ray, the reflected ray and the normal all lie in the same plane.

A **concave** mirror curves inwards (like a spoon's inside); a **convex** mirror bulges outwards.

Key terms: **pole P** (centre of the mirror's surface), **centre of curvature C**, **radius of curvature R**, **principal axis**, **principal focus F**. For small apertures, **f = R/2**.

| Object position (concave mirror) | Image |
|---|---|
| At infinity | At F; highly diminished, real, inverted |
| Beyond C | Between F and C; diminished, real, inverted |
| At C | At C; same size, real, inverted |
| Between C and F | Beyond C; enlarged, real, inverted |
| At F | At infinity; highly enlarged |
| Between P and F | Behind the mirror; enlarged, **virtual, erect** |

A **convex** mirror always gives a **virtual, erect, diminished** image, so it gives a wide field of view: rear-view mirrors in vehicles. Concave mirrors are used in torches and headlights (to make parallel beams), by dentists and in shaving mirrors (enlarged erect image), and in solar furnaces.

> [!FORMULA] Mirror formula and magnification
> 1/v + 1/u = 1/f
> m = h′/h = −v/u

**Sign convention** (New Cartesian): measure all distances from the pole; distances in the direction of the incident light are positive, against it negative. Heights above the axis are positive. The object is usually placed on the left, so **u is negative**. For a concave mirror f is negative; for a convex mirror f is positive.

> [!EXAMPLE] An object is 30 cm in front of a concave mirror of focal length 15 cm. Find the image.
> u = −30, f = −15. 1/v = 1/f − 1/u = −1/15 + 1/30 = −1/30 ⇒ **v = −30 cm** (in front, real).
> m = −v/u = −(−30)/(−30) = −1: **same size, inverted**. The object is at C.

## Refraction
Light bends because it travels at different **speeds** in different media. Going from rarer to denser (air → glass), it bends **towards** the normal; denser to rarer, it bends **away**.

> [!KEY] Laws of refraction
> 1. The incident ray, refracted ray and normal lie in one plane.
> 2. **Snell's law**: sin i / sin r = constant = refractive index of medium 2 with respect to medium 1.

> [!FORMULA] Refractive index
> n = speed of light in vacuum / speed of light in the medium = c/v
> n of glass ≈ 1.5, water ≈ 1.33, diamond ≈ 2.42

Through a rectangular glass slab, the emergent ray is **parallel** to the incident ray but **laterally displaced**. A pencil in water looks bent and a pool looks shallower because of refraction.

## Lenses
A **convex (converging)** lens is thicker in the middle; a **concave (diverging)** lens is thinner in the middle. A convex lens forms images much like a concave mirror (real for distant objects, virtual and enlarged when the object is between F and the optical centre: a magnifying glass). A concave lens always forms a **virtual, erect, diminished** image.

> [!FORMULA] Lens formula, magnification and power
> 1/v − 1/u = 1/f
> m = h′/h = v/u
> P = 1/f (f in metres); unit **dioptre (D)**. Convex lens: positive power. Concave lens: negative.

::sim[lens]

> [!EXAMPLE] A concave lens has focal length 15 cm. Where should the object be placed so that the image is 10 cm from the lens?
> f = −15, v = −10 (virtual, same side). 1/u = 1/v − 1/f = −1/10 + 1/15 = −1/30 ⇒ **u = −30 cm**.

> [!MISTAKE] Mixing the two formulas
> Mirror: 1/v **+** 1/u = 1/f, m = −v/u. Lens: 1/v **−** 1/u = 1/f, m = +v/u.

## Quick recap
- Reflection: angle i = angle r. Concave mirror image depends on object position; convex always virtual and small.
- Refraction: n = c/v; light bends towards the normal entering a denser medium.
- Use the sign convention carefully with the mirror and lens formulas.
- Power P = 1/f (metres), in dioptres.

=== the-human-eye-and-the-colourful-world ===
## The big idea
The eye is a natural camera with a lens that changes its focal length. In this chapter we see how the eye works, why some people need spectacles, and why the sky is blue and rainbows form.

## Structure of the eye
| Part | Role |
|---|---|
| Cornea | Transparent front; most refraction happens here |
| Iris | Coloured muscle that controls pupil size |
| Pupil | Opening that lets light in |
| Eye lens | Fine-tunes focus to form an image on the retina |
| Ciliary muscles | Change the lens's curvature |
| Retina | Light-sensitive screen; cells send signals via the optic nerve |

> [!KEY] Power of accommodation
> The ciliary muscles make the lens **thinner** for distant objects and **thicker** for near ones. The **near point** of a normal eye is about **25 cm**; the far point is **infinity**.

## Defects of vision
| Defect | Can't see clearly | Cause | Correction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Myopia (short-sightedness) | Distant objects | Image forms **in front of** retina (eyeball too long or lens too curved) | **Concave** lens |
| Hypermetropia (long-sightedness) | Nearby objects | Image forms **behind** retina (eyeball too short or focal length too long) | **Convex** lens |
| Presbyopia | Near objects, in old age | Ciliary muscles weaken | Often **bifocal** lenses |

**Cataract** (cloudy lens) is treated by surgery.

## Refraction through a prism
A glass prism bends light twice, so the emergent ray is deviated from its original direction by the **angle of deviation**.

> [!KEY] Dispersion
> White light splits into its seven colours, **VIBGYOR** (violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange, red), because each colour bends by a different amount. **Violet bends the most, red the least.** Isaac Newton showed that a second, inverted prism recombines them into white light.

A **rainbow** forms when sunlight is dispersed, refracted and internally reflected by tiny raindrops. You see it with the Sun **behind** you.

**Atmospheric refraction**: hot air above a fire makes objects behind it seem to flicker; stars **twinkle** because starlight is refracted by layers of the atmosphere; we see the Sun about **2 minutes before** actual sunrise and after actual sunset. Planets don't twinkle because they are closer and act as extended sources.

## Scattering of light
Tiny particles scatter light. The **Tyndall effect** is the scattering of light by particles in a colloid, which makes the path of a beam visible.

> [!EXAMPLE] Why is the sky blue?
> Air molecules are much smaller than the wavelength of visible light and scatter **shorter wavelengths (blue)** much more than red. This scattered blue light reaches us from all directions.
> At **sunrise and sunset**, sunlight travels through more air, most blue is scattered away, and mostly **red** reaches us. Danger signals are red because red scatters least and travels farthest in fog.

## Quick recap
- The eye lens changes shape (accommodation); near point 25 cm.
- Myopia → concave lens; hypermetropia → convex lens.
- Prisms disperse white light into VIBGYOR; violet bends most.
- Scattering explains the blue sky and the red sunset.

=== electricity ===
## The big idea
Electricity powers almost everything around us. This chapter explains what makes charge flow, how resistance controls that flow, and how electrical energy turns into heat.

## Current and potential difference
> [!FORMULA] Electric current
> I = Q / t (unit ampere, A; 1 A = 1 C/s)

Charge flows only in a **closed circuit**. Conventional current flows from + to −, opposite to the flow of electrons. The charge of one electron is 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. An **ammeter** measures current and is connected in **series**.

> [!FORMULA] Potential difference
> V = W / Q (unit volt, V; 1 V = 1 J/C)

A cell maintains a potential difference across the circuit. A **voltmeter** is connected in **parallel**.

## Ohm's law and resistance
> [!KEY] Ohm's law
> At constant temperature, the current through a conductor is proportional to the potential difference across it: **V = IR**. R is the **resistance**, measured in ohms (Ω). The V–I graph is a straight line through the origin.

::sim[ohm]

Resistance depends on the conductor:

> [!FORMULA] Resistivity
> R = ρ l / A
> R increases with length l and decreases with area of cross-section A. ρ (resistivity, Ω m) depends on the material and temperature.

Metals have low ρ (copper, aluminium: used for wires). Alloys like **nichrome** have high ρ and don't oxidise easily (used in heater coils). Glass and rubber are insulators.

## Combining resistors
> [!FORMULA] Series and parallel
> Series: R = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ (same current through each; voltages add)
> Parallel: 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ (same voltage across each; currents add)

> [!EXAMPLE] Resistors of 2 Ω, 3 Ω and 6 Ω are connected in parallel to a 12 V battery
> 1/R = 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/6 = 1 ⇒ **R = 1 Ω**. Current from the battery I = 12/1 = **12 A**.

Household appliances are connected in **parallel** so each gets the full voltage and can be switched on or off independently. The parallel resistance is **less** than the smallest individual resistance.

## Heating effect and power
> [!FORMULA] Joule's law of heating and power
> H = I²Rt
> P = VI = I²R = V²/R (unit watt, W)
> Electrical energy (commercial unit) = 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J

The heating effect is used in electric irons, toasters, kettles, bulbs (tungsten filament, filled with nitrogen or argon) and **fuses** (a thin wire with a low melting point that melts and breaks the circuit when too much current flows).

> [!EXAMPLE] A 100 W bulb runs 5 hours a day. How many units does it use in 30 days?
> Energy = 0.1 kW × 5 h × 30 = **15 kWh (units)**.

> [!MISTAKE] Ammeter and voltmeter positions
> Ammeter in **series** (it must carry the current). Voltmeter in **parallel** (it compares two points).

## Quick recap
- I = Q/t, V = W/Q, V = IR.
- R = ρl/A; series resistances add, parallel reciprocals add.
- H = I²Rt; P = VI; 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ.

=== magnetic-effects-of-electric-current ===
## The big idea
An electric current produces a **magnetic field**. Hans Christian Oersted noticed that a compass needle deflects near a current-carrying wire. This link between electricity and magnetism runs motors, loudspeakers and MRI machines.

## Magnetic field and field lines
A **magnetic field** exists around a magnet. **Field lines** show it: they leave the **north pole** and enter the **south pole** outside the magnet, and go from south to north inside it. They are **closed curves**, **never cross** (the field can't point two ways at once), and are **closer together where the field is stronger**.

## Field due to a current
| Conductor | Shape of field | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Straight wire | Concentric circles around the wire | Stronger near the wire and for larger current |
| Circular loop | Lines nearly straight at the centre | Field increases with the number of turns |
| Solenoid (long coil) | Like a bar magnet; **uniform inside** | Used to make electromagnets with a soft iron core |

> [!KEY] Right-hand thumb rule
> Hold the wire in your right hand with the thumb pointing along the current. Your **curled fingers** show the direction of the magnetic field lines.

## Force on a current-carrying conductor
A conductor carrying current in a magnetic field experiences a **force**. It is maximum when the current is **perpendicular** to the field.

> [!KEY] Fleming's left-hand rule
> Stretch the thumb, forefinger and middle finger of your left hand mutually perpendicular. **F**orefinger = **F**ield, **c**entre (middle) finger = **c**urrent, **th**umb = **th**rust (force/motion).

This is the principle of the **electric motor** and loudspeakers. In medicine, **MRI** uses magnetic fields produced inside the body to form images. Even nerve impulses create weak magnetic fields.

## Domestic electric circuits
In India, the supply is **220 V AC at 50 Hz**.

> [!KEY] Three wires
> **Live** (red insulation), **neutral** (black), **earth** (green). Earth wire connects metal bodies of appliances to the ground so that leaked current flows to earth instead of giving you a shock.

- Separate circuits: **15 A** for high-power appliances (geysers, ACs), **5 A** for bulbs and fans.
- Appliances are in **parallel**.
- **Short circuit**: live and neutral touch, current rises sharply.
- **Overloading**: too many appliances on one socket, or a voltage surge.
- The **fuse** (or MCB) protects against both.

## Quick recap
- Current creates a magnetic field; right-hand thumb rule gives its direction.
- A solenoid's field is uniform inside, like a bar magnet.
- Fleming's left-hand rule gives the force on a conductor.
- Household wiring: live, neutral, earth; fuses protect against short circuits and overloading.
