=== matter-in-our-surroundings ===
## The big idea
Everything that has **mass** and occupies **space** is matter: air, water, your desk, even the smell of food. Matter is made of **tiny particles**, and how those particles behave explains the three states and how they change.

## Characteristics of particles
> [!KEY] Particles of matter
> 1. They are **extremely small**.
> 2. They have **space between them** (sugar dissolves in water without raising the level much).
> 3. They are **continuously moving** (kinetic energy increases with temperature).
> 4. They **attract each other** (it's harder to break iron than chalk).

**Diffusion** is the intermixing of particles of two substances on their own. It is fastest in gases, slower in liquids, very slow in solids, and faster at higher temperature (a drop of ink spreads faster in hot water).

## States of matter
| Property | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shape and volume | Fixed shape and volume | Fixed volume, takes container's shape | No fixed shape or volume |
| Compressibility | Negligible | Very little | Highly compressible (LPG, CNG cylinders) |
| Interparticle space | Least | More | Most |
| Force of attraction | Strongest | Weaker | Weakest |
| Rigidity / fluidity | Rigid | Flows | Flows |

## Change of state
- **Melting point**: temperature at which a solid melts at atmospheric pressure. Ice: **0 °C (273 K)**.
- **Boiling point**: water boils at **100 °C (373 K)**.
- **Latent heat**: heat supplied during a change of state **doesn't raise the temperature**; it is used to overcome the attraction between particles. Latent heat of fusion (melting), of vaporisation (boiling).
- **Sublimation**: solid directly to gas without melting: camphor, naphthalene, ammonium chloride, dry ice (solid CO₂). Gas directly to solid is **deposition**.
- **Pressure** also changes state: increasing pressure and lowering temperature can liquefy gases.

> [!FORMULA] Kelvin and Celsius
> T(K) = T(°C) + 273

## Evaporation
**Evaporation** is a surface phenomenon: particles at the surface escape as vapour **below** the boiling point. It increases with **surface area**, **temperature** and **wind speed**, and decreases with **humidity**.

> [!KEY] Evaporation causes cooling
> Escaping particles take energy with them, so the remaining liquid cools. That's why sweating cools us, an earthen pot (matka) keeps water cool, and cotton clothes are best in summer (they absorb sweat and let it evaporate).

## Quick recap
- Matter is particles with space, motion and attraction.
- Solid, liquid, gas differ in spacing and attraction.
- Latent heat is absorbed at constant temperature during a change of state.
- Evaporation is a surface effect that causes cooling.

=== is-matter-around-us-pure ===
## The big idea
In everyday language "pure" means unadulterated. To a scientist, a **pure substance** has particles that are all the same chemical: an **element** or a **compound**. Most things around us are **mixtures**.

## Mixtures
- **Homogeneous** (uniform composition): salt in water, air, alloys. These are **solutions**.
- **Heterogeneous** (non-uniform): sand in water, oil and water, soil.

| Type | Particle size | Visible? | Settles? | Tyndall effect? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Solution | Smallest (< 1 nm) | No | No | No | Sugar in water |
| Colloid | Intermediate | No (to the eye) | No | **Yes** | Milk, fog, smoke, jelly |
| Suspension | Large | Yes | Yes | Yes | Chalk in water, muddy water |

**Concentration** of a solution = amount of solute / amount of solution (mass by mass % or mass by volume %). A **saturated** solution can't dissolve any more solute at that temperature; **solubility** is the amount dissolved in 100 g of solvent.

## Separating mixtures
| Method | Separates | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Evaporation | Dissolved solid from liquid | Salt from sea water |
| Centrifugation | Fine suspended particles by spinning | Cream from milk |
| Separating funnel | Immiscible liquids | Oil and water |
| Sublimation | Sublimable solid from others | Ammonium chloride from salt |
| Chromatography | Coloured components | Dyes in ink |
| Distillation | Miscible liquids with very different boiling points | Acetone and water |
| Fractional distillation | Liquids with close boiling points | Gases of air, petroleum |
| Crystallisation | Pure solid from solution | Pure copper sulphate, salt |

## Physical and chemical changes
- **Physical change**: no new substance forms; often reversible (melting ice, cutting paper).
- **Chemical change**: a new substance forms (burning, rusting, cooking).

## Elements and compounds
- **Element**: cannot be broken into simpler substances by chemical reactions: metals, non-metals, metalloids (boron, silicon).
- **Compound**: two or more elements **chemically combined** in a **fixed ratio**. Its properties differ from its elements (iron + sulphur heated → iron sulphide, which isn't magnetic).

> [!KEY] Mixture vs compound
> Mixture: variable composition, components keep their properties, separated by physical methods.
> Compound: fixed composition, new properties, separated only by chemical methods.

## Quick recap
- Pure substances: elements and compounds. Mixtures: homogeneous or heterogeneous.
- Solution, colloid and suspension differ in particle size; colloids show the Tyndall effect.
- Choose the separation method by the property that differs.

=== atoms-and-molecules ===
## The big idea
More than 2,000 years ago, Indian philosopher **Maharishi Kanad** suggested that matter is made of tiny indivisible particles (paramanu). In 1808, **John Dalton** turned this into a scientific theory supported by the laws of chemical combination.

## Laws of chemical combination
> [!KEY] Two laws
> **Law of conservation of mass** (Lavoisier): mass is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.
> **Law of constant proportions** (Proust): in a chemical compound, elements are always present in a **definite proportion by mass**. Water always has hydrogen and oxygen in the mass ratio 1 : 8.

Dalton's atomic theory explained these laws: matter is made of atoms; atoms of an element are identical in mass and properties; atoms combine in small whole-number ratios; atoms are neither created nor destroyed in reactions.

## Atoms and atomic mass
Atoms are incredibly small (radius about 10⁻¹⁰ m). Symbols come from English or Latin names: H, O, C, Na (natrium), Fe (ferrum), K (kalium).

**Atomic mass unit (u)**: 1/12 of the mass of one carbon-12 atom. Atomic masses: H = 1 u, C = 12 u, N = 14 u, O = 16 u, Na = 23 u, Cl = 35.5 u.

## Molecules and ions
A **molecule** is the smallest particle of a substance that can exist independently. **Atomicity** = number of atoms in a molecule: monatomic (He, Ar), diatomic (O₂, H₂, Cl₂), triatomic (O₃), polyatomic (P₄, S₈).

**Ions** are charged particles: cations (+) and anions (−). **Polyatomic ions** act as a unit: NH₄⁺, OH⁻, NO₃⁻, CO₃²⁻, SO₄²⁻, PO₄³⁻.

## Writing chemical formulae
> [!TIP] Criss-cross the valencies
> Write the symbols, put the valencies below, cross them over and simplify.
> Aluminium oxide: Al (3), O (2) → **Al₂O₃**. Calcium hydroxide: Ca (2), OH (1) → **Ca(OH)₂**.

## Molecular mass and the mole
**Molecular mass** = sum of atomic masses. H₂O = 2(1) + 16 = **18 u**.

> [!KEY] The mole
> One **mole** of any substance contains **6.022 × 10²³** particles (Avogadro's number, N₀).
> The mass of one mole in grams equals its atomic/molecular mass in u: 1 mole of water = 18 g.

> [!FORMULA] Mole calculations
> number of moles n = given mass m / molar mass M = number of particles N / N₀

> [!EXAMPLE] How many moles are in 52 g of helium (atomic mass 4 u)?
> n = 52/4 = **13 mol**.

## Quick recap
- Conservation of mass and constant proportions.
- Atomic mass in u; molecular mass = sum of atomic masses.
- Criss-cross valencies to write formulae.
- 1 mole = 6.022 × 10²³ particles = molar mass in grams.

=== structure-of-the-atom ===
## The big idea
Dalton thought atoms were indivisible. Experiments around 1900 showed atoms contain even smaller particles: **electrons**, **protons** and **neutrons**. How these are arranged explains why elements behave the way they do.

## Subatomic particles
| Particle | Discovered by | Charge | Mass | Location |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Electron (e⁻) | J. J. Thomson | −1 | About 1/2000 u | Outside nucleus, in shells |
| Proton (p⁺) | E. Goldstein (canal rays) | +1 | 1 u | Nucleus |
| Neutron (n) | J. Chadwick (1932) | 0 | 1 u | Nucleus |

## Models of the atom
- **Thomson**: atom is a positive sphere with electrons embedded, like seeds in a watermelon; overall neutral.
- **Rutherford's gold-foil experiment**: fast alpha particles fired at thin gold foil. Most passed straight through, some deflected, very few (1 in 12,000) bounced back. Conclusions: most of the atom is **empty space**; positive charge and nearly all the mass are in a tiny **nucleus**; electrons revolve around it. Problem: revolving electrons should lose energy and fall into the nucleus.
- **Bohr**: electrons move only in certain **discrete orbits (shells)**, K, L, M, N, without radiating energy.

## Distribution of electrons
> [!KEY] Bohr–Bury rules
> Maximum electrons in a shell = **2n²**: K (n = 1) holds 2, L holds 8, M holds 18.
> The outermost shell can hold at most 8 electrons.
> Shells fill step by step: inner shells first.

Example configurations: Na (11) = 2, 8, 1; Cl (17) = 2, 8, 7; Ar (18) = 2, 8, 8.

## Valency
Electrons in the outermost shell are **valence electrons**. Atoms tend to reach a full outer shell (an **octet**). **Valency** is the combining capacity: equal to the number of valence electrons (if 1–4), or 8 minus that number (if 5–7). Na has valency 1; Cl has valency 8 − 7 = 1; noble gases have 0.

## Atomic number, mass number and isotopes
> [!FORMULA] Two numbers
> Atomic number Z = number of protons
> Mass number A = protons + neutrons; so neutrons = A − Z

- **Isotopes**: same Z, different A (¹H, ²H, ³H; ¹²C, ¹⁴C). Same chemical properties. Uses: uranium isotope as nuclear fuel, cobalt isotope for cancer treatment, iodine isotope for goitre.
- **Isobars**: same A, different Z (⁴⁰Ca and ⁴⁰Ar).

> [!EXAMPLE] Chlorine is ³⁵Cl (75%) and ³⁷Cl (25%). Find its average atomic mass.
> 35 × 0.75 + 37 × 0.25 = 26.25 + 9.25 = **35.5 u**.

## Quick recap
- Electrons (−), protons (+) and neutrons (0); nucleus holds protons and neutrons.
- Rutherford: tiny dense nucleus; Bohr: fixed energy shells.
- 2n² rule; valency from valence electrons.
- Isotopes share Z; isobars share A.

=== the-fundamental-unit-of-life ===
## The big idea
Every living thing, from a bacterium to a banyan tree, is made of **cells**. A cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life: it takes in food, releases energy, removes waste and reproduces.

## Discovery of the cell
- **Robert Hooke (1665)** saw tiny boxes in cork and named them cells.
- **Leeuwenhoek (1674)** first saw free-living cells in pond water.
- **Robert Brown (1831)** discovered the nucleus.
- **Schleiden and Schwann (1838–39)**: all plants and animals are made of cells (**cell theory**). **Virchow (1855)**: all cells arise from pre-existing cells.

Organisms can be **unicellular** (Amoeba, Paramecium, bacteria) or **multicellular** (humans, plants). Cells vary in shape and size; nerve cells are long, red blood cells are disc-shaped.

## Plasma membrane and cell wall
> [!KEY] Plasma membrane
> The outer covering of every cell, made of lipids and proteins. It is **selectively permeable**: it lets some substances in and out but not others.
> **Diffusion**: movement from high to low concentration (O₂, CO₂).
> **Osmosis**: movement of **water** through a selectively permeable membrane from a dilute to a concentrated solution.

In a **hypotonic** solution a cell swells (water enters); in a **hypertonic** solution it shrinks. A plant cell losing water shrinks away from its wall: **plasmolysis**.

Plant cells (and fungi) also have a **cell wall** made of **cellulose**, which gives strength and lets them survive in dilute surroundings without bursting.

## Nucleus
Controls the cell's activities. It contains **chromosomes** made of **DNA** (genetic information) and proteins. Bacteria have no well-defined nucleus; their genetic material lies in a region called the **nucleoid**. These are **prokaryotes**; cells with a membrane-bound nucleus are **eukaryotes**.

## Cell organelles
| Organelle | Function |
|---|---|
| Endoplasmic reticulum (rough, with ribosomes / smooth) | Transport channels; RER makes proteins, SER makes lipids and detoxifies |
| Golgi apparatus | Packages and dispatches materials; forms lysosomes |
| Lysosomes | Digest waste and worn-out parts: the cell's **"suicide bags"** |
| Mitochondria | Release energy as ATP: the **powerhouse of the cell**; have their own DNA and ribosomes |
| Plastids (chloroplasts) | Photosynthesis (in plant cells) |
| Vacuoles | Storage; large central vacuole in plant cells keeps them turgid |
| Ribosomes | Protein synthesis |

> [!KEY] Plant vs animal cells
> Plant cells: cell wall, chloroplasts, large central vacuole.
> Animal cells: no cell wall, no chloroplasts, small vacuoles, centrioles.

## Cell division
- **Mitosis**: for growth and repair; one cell makes two identical daughter cells with the same number of chromosomes.
- **Meiosis**: in reproductive organs to form gametes; four cells with **half** the number of chromosomes.

## Quick recap
- The cell is the unit of life; cell theory by Schleiden, Schwann and Virchow.
- Plasma membrane is selectively permeable; osmosis moves water.
- Know each organelle's role.
- Mitosis for growth, meiosis for gametes.

=== tissues ===
## The big idea
In multicellular organisms, cells that are similar in structure and work together for a particular function form a **tissue**. Plants and animals have different tissues because of their different lifestyles: plants are fixed, animals move.

## Plant tissues
**Meristematic tissue** (dividing cells; thin walls, dense cytoplasm, prominent nucleus, no vacuoles):
- **Apical** at root and shoot tips (length), **lateral** (cambium; girth), **intercalary** at the base of leaves and internodes.

**Permanent tissues** (cells that have lost the ability to divide):

| Tissue | Features | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Parenchyma | Loosely packed living cells, thin walls | Storage; **chlorenchyma** (photosynthesis), **aerenchyma** (buoyancy in water plants) |
| Collenchyma | Walls thickened at corners | Flexibility (bending of leaves and stems) |
| Sclerenchyma | Dead cells with **lignin** | Hardness (husk of coconut) |
| Epidermis | Outer layer, waxy cutin; **stomata** | Protection, gas exchange, transpiration |
| Cork | Dead cells with **suberin** | Protection in older stems |

**Complex permanent tissues** (more than one type of cell):
- **Xylem**: tracheids, vessels, xylem parenchyma, xylem fibres; carries **water and minerals** upward.
- **Phloem**: sieve tubes, companion cells, phloem parenchyma and fibres; carries **food** in both directions.

## Animal tissues
| Tissue | Types and roles |
|---|---|
| **Epithelial** | Covers and lines organs: squamous (skin, lining of mouth), cuboidal (kidney tubules), columnar (intestine), ciliated (respiratory tract), glandular |
| **Connective** | Blood (plasma + RBC, WBC, platelets), bone (hard matrix of calcium and phosphorus), cartilage, ligament (bone to bone), **tendon** (muscle to bone), areolar, adipose (fat storage) |
| **Muscular** | **Striated** (voluntary, skeletal), **smooth** (involuntary, in the gut), **cardiac** (involuntary, heart; never tires) |
| **Nervous** | **Neurons**: cell body, dendrites, long **axon**; carry impulses quickly |

> [!MISTAKE] Ligament vs tendon
> **L**igament joins bone to bone (think "link"). **T**endon joins muscle to bone (think "tug").

## Quick recap
- Plants: meristematic (dividing) and permanent tissues; xylem and phloem conduct.
- Animals: epithelial, connective, muscular, nervous.
- Know where each tissue is found and what it does.

=== improvement-in-food-resources ===
## The big idea
India's growing population needs more food without destroying the environment. Scientists improve **crops** and **animals** so that we produce more, better-quality food from the same land: this is the idea behind the Green Revolution (crops) and White Revolution (milk).

## Crop variety improvement
Crops are bred by **hybridisation** (crossing varieties) or by introducing a gene (**genetically modified** crops) to get:
- higher yield,
- better quality (protein in pulses, oil in oilseeds),
- resistance to diseases and pests,
- tolerance to heat, drought, salinity,
- wider adaptability and suitable agronomic traits (shorter, dwarf plants for less lodging).

**Seasons**: **kharif** crops grow in the rainy season, June–October (paddy, soyabean, maize, cotton). **Rabi** crops grow in winter, November–April (wheat, gram, peas, mustard).

## Crop production management
- **Nutrients**: plants need 16 nutrients. Macronutrients (N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S) are needed in large amounts; micronutrients (Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, Mo, Cl) in small amounts.
- **Manure** (organic, improves soil structure) vs **fertilisers** (chemical, quick but can harm soil and water if overused). **Organic farming** uses minimal chemicals.
- **Irrigation**: wells, canals, river-lift systems, tanks, rainwater harvesting.
- **Cropping patterns**: **mixed cropping** (two crops together in the same field), **intercropping** (in a set row pattern), **crop rotation** (different crops in sequence, e.g. a legume to restore nitrogen).

## Crop protection
Weeds, insect pests and diseases reduce yield. Control them with pesticides used carefully, resistant varieties, timely sowing, summer ploughing and proper storage. Grain is protected from moisture, insects, rodents and fungi by cleaning, drying and fumigation.

## Animal husbandry
- **Cattle farming**: milk-producing animals (milch) and draught animals. Exotic breeds (Jersey, Brown Swiss) give long lactation; local breeds (Red Sindhi, Sahiwal) resist disease; crossbreeding combines both. Proper shelter, cleanliness, balanced feed (roughage + concentrates) and vaccination keep them healthy.
- **Poultry**: layers (eggs) and broilers (meat); crossbreeding of Indian (Aseel) and foreign (Leghorn) breeds.
- **Fish production**: capture fishing (marine, inland) and **culture** fishery (aquaculture). **Composite fish culture** puts 5–6 species that feed at different levels in one pond (catla at the surface, rohu in the middle, mrigal at the bottom).
- **Bee-keeping (apiculture)**: honey and wax; Italian bee *Apis mellifera* gives high yield. Pasturage (flowers available) decides honey quality.

## Quick recap
- Improve crops by hybridisation and GM for yield, quality and resistance.
- Kharif (rainy season) and rabi (winter) crops.
- Manage nutrients, irrigation and cropping patterns; protect crops and stored grain.
- Animal husbandry: cattle, poultry, fish and bee-keeping.

=== motion ===
## The big idea
A body is in **motion** if its position changes with time **relative to a reference point**. To describe motion precisely we need distance, displacement, speed, velocity and acceleration, and graphs and equations that connect them.

## Distance and displacement
- **Distance**: total path length; a **scalar** (magnitude only).
- **Displacement**: shortest distance from the initial to the final position, **with direction**; a **vector**. It can be zero even when distance isn't (a full lap of a circular track).

## Speed, velocity and acceleration
> [!FORMULA] Rates of motion
> Average speed = total distance / total time
> Velocity = displacement / time (speed in a given direction)
> For uniform acceleration, average velocity = (u + v)/2
> Acceleration a = (v − u)/t (unit m/s²)

**Uniform motion**: equal distances in equal intervals. **Non-uniform**: unequal. A negative acceleration (slowing down) is called **retardation** or deceleration.

## Graphs of motion
- **Distance–time graph**: slope = **speed**. A straight sloping line = uniform speed; a curve = changing speed; a horizontal line = at rest.
- **Velocity–time graph**: slope = **acceleration**; **area under the graph = displacement**.

## Equations of motion (uniform acceleration)
> [!FORMULA] The three equations
> v = u + at
> s = ut + ½at²
> v² − u² = 2as

> [!EXAMPLE] A car accelerates uniformly from 18 km/h to 36 km/h in 5 s. Find a and the distance covered.
> Convert: u = 5 m/s, v = 10 m/s. a = (10 − 5)/5 = **1 m/s²**.
> s = ut + ½at² = 25 + 12.5 = **37.5 m**.

> [!TIP] Unit conversion
> To change km/h to m/s, multiply by 5/18. 72 km/h = 20 m/s.

## Uniform circular motion
A body moving in a circle at **constant speed** is still **accelerating**, because its **direction** keeps changing. Speed v = 2πr / t. Examples: the Moon around Earth, an athlete on a circular track, a stone whirled on a string.

> [!MISTAKE] Constant speed ≠ constant velocity
> In circular motion the speed is constant but the velocity is not, because velocity includes direction.

## Quick recap
- Distance is scalar; displacement is vector.
- Slope of x–t = speed; slope of v–t = acceleration; area under v–t = displacement.
- v = u + at, s = ut + ½at², v² − u² = 2as.
- Uniform circular motion is accelerated motion.

=== force-and-laws-of-motion ===
## The big idea
Why does a ball keep rolling, and why do you lurch forward when a bus brakes? **Newton's three laws** explain how **forces** change motion.

## Balanced and unbalanced forces
A **force** is a push or pull. Forces that cancel out are **balanced**: no change in motion. An **unbalanced** force changes the speed or direction of an object.

## Newton's first law (law of inertia)
> [!KEY] First law
> An object remains at rest or in uniform motion in a straight line unless an **unbalanced external force** acts on it.

**Inertia** is the tendency to resist a change in the state of motion. **Mass is the measure of inertia**: a loaded truck is harder to start or stop than a bicycle.

Examples: passengers fall forward when a bus stops suddenly; dust leaves a carpet when it is beaten; a coin on a card drops into a glass when the card is flicked away.

## Newton's second law
**Momentum** p = mv (unit kg m/s). It has the direction of velocity.

> [!KEY] Second law
> The rate of change of momentum is proportional to the applied unbalanced force, in the direction of the force: **F = ma**. The unit of force is the **newton**: 1 N = 1 kg m/s².

> [!EXAMPLE] A 1000 kg car slows from 20 m/s to rest in 4 s. Find the force.
> a = (0 − 20)/4 = −5 m/s². F = 1000 × (−5) = **−5000 N** (a 5000 N force opposing motion).

This law explains why a cricketer **pulls his hands back** when catching: more time to stop the ball means less force on the hands. Seat belts and airbags work the same way.

## Newton's third law
> [!KEY] Third law
> To every action there is an **equal and opposite reaction**. The two forces act on **different bodies**, so they don't cancel.

Examples: a gun recoils when fired; you push the ground back to walk forward; a boat moves back when a sailor jumps forward; a rocket pushes gases down and is pushed up.

## Conservation of momentum
> [!KEY] Law of conservation of momentum
> When no external force acts, the **total momentum** of a system stays constant: m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂.

> [!EXAMPLE] A 4 kg gun fires a 20 g bullet at 400 m/s. Find the recoil velocity.
> Total momentum before = 0. So 4v + 0.02 × 400 = 0 ⇒ v = **−2 m/s** (backwards).

## Quick recap
- First law: inertia; mass measures inertia.
- Second law: F = ma; force = rate of change of momentum.
- Third law: equal and opposite forces on different bodies.
- Momentum is conserved without external forces.

=== gravitation ===
## The big idea
The force that makes an apple fall also keeps the Moon in orbit. Isaac Newton realised this is one **universal** force: **gravitation**.

## Universal law of gravitation
> [!FORMULA] Newton's law of gravitation
> F = G m₁m₂ / d²
> G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻² (universal gravitational constant)

Every object attracts every other object with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. Doubling the distance makes the force **one-fourth**. It explains tides, the Moon's orbit and planets around the Sun.

## Free fall and g
Objects falling only under gravity are in **free fall** with acceleration **g ≈ 9.8 m/s²** near Earth's surface, independent of their mass (ignoring air resistance; a feather and a coin fall together in a vacuum).

> [!FORMULA] Acceleration due to gravity
> g = GM / R², where M is Earth's mass and R its radius

g is slightly greater at the poles than at the equator, because Earth's radius is smaller at the poles. The equations of motion work with a = g.

## Mass and weight
> [!KEY] Mass vs weight
> **Mass** is the amount of matter; it is the same everywhere (kg).
> **Weight** is the force of gravity on an object: **W = mg** (N). It changes with g.
> The Moon's gravity is about **1/6** of Earth's, so you would weigh 1/6 as much there, with the same mass.

## Thrust and pressure
**Thrust**: force acting perpendicular to a surface. **Pressure** = thrust / area (unit pascal, Pa = N/m²). Sharp knives and nails have small areas (large pressure); buildings have wide foundations and school bags have broad straps (small pressure).

Fluids exert pressure in all directions.

## Buoyancy and Archimedes' principle
When an object is immersed in a fluid, the fluid pushes it up with a **buoyant force (upthrust)**.

> [!KEY] Archimedes' principle
> A body wholly or partially immersed in a fluid experiences an upward force equal to the **weight of the fluid displaced**.
> An object **floats** if its density is less than the fluid's, and **sinks** if it is greater.

**Relative density** = density of substance / density of water (no units). Uses: designing ships and submarines, lactometers (milk purity), hydrometers.

## Quick recap
- F = Gm₁m₂/d²; inverse-square law.
- g ≈ 9.8 m/s²; W = mg; Moon's g ≈ g/6.
- Pressure = thrust/area.
- Buoyant force = weight of fluid displaced.

=== work-and-energy ===
## The big idea
In science, **work** is done only when a force **moves** an object. Energy is the **capacity to do work**, and it is never lost, only changed from one form to another.

## Work
> [!FORMULA] Work done by a constant force
> W = F × s (in the direction of the force); unit joule (J): 1 J = 1 N × 1 m

Work is **positive** when force and displacement are in the same direction (pushing a cart forward), **negative** when opposite (friction slowing a ball), and **zero** when they are perpendicular or there is no displacement (a porter carrying a load horizontally does no work against gravity; pushing a wall does no work).

## Kinetic and potential energy
> [!FORMULA] Two forms of mechanical energy
> Kinetic energy (energy of motion): KE = ½mv²
> Potential energy (energy of position, height h): PE = mgh

> [!EXAMPLE] A 2 kg ball moves at 5 m/s. Find its kinetic energy.
> KE = ½ × 2 × 25 = **25 J**.

Doubling the speed makes KE **four times** larger. Potential energy depends on height, not the path taken to get there.

## Conservation of energy
> [!KEY] Law of conservation of energy
> Energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be transformed from one form to another. The **total energy stays constant**.

> [!EXAMPLE] A freely falling ball
> At the top, all its energy is PE. As it falls, PE turns into KE. Just before hitting the ground, it's all KE. At every point, **PE + KE = constant** (ignoring air resistance).

Other transformations: chemical energy in food → muscles; electrical → light and heat in a bulb; solar → chemical in photosynthesis.

## Power
> [!FORMULA] Power
> P = W / t (rate of doing work); unit watt (W): 1 W = 1 J/s
> Commercial unit of energy: 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J (one "unit" on the electricity bill)

> [!EXAMPLE] A 60 W bulb runs for 10 hours. Energy used?
> 60 × 10 = 600 Wh = **0.6 kWh**.

## Quick recap
- W = Fs; it can be positive, negative or zero.
- KE = ½mv², PE = mgh.
- Total energy is conserved.
- Power = work/time; 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ.

=== sound ===
## The big idea
Sound is a form of energy produced by **vibrating** objects. It needs a **medium** (solid, liquid or gas) to travel, which is why there is no sound in space.

## How sound travels
A vibrating object pushes and pulls the particles of the medium, creating regions of high pressure (**compressions**) and low pressure (**rarefactions**). The particles move back and forth **along** the direction of travel, so sound is a **longitudinal wave**. (In transverse waves, like ripples on water, particles move perpendicular to the wave.)

## Characteristics of a sound wave
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Wavelength (λ) | Distance between two consecutive compressions (unit m) |
| Frequency (ν) | Number of oscillations per second (hertz, Hz) |
| Time period (T) | Time for one oscillation; T = 1/ν |
| Amplitude | Maximum disturbance; decides **loudness** |
| Pitch | How high or low; decided by **frequency** |
| Quality (timbre) | Distinguishes sounds of the same pitch and loudness |

> [!FORMULA] Wave speed
> v = λ × ν

> [!EXAMPLE] A sound wave has frequency 2 kHz and wavelength 35 cm. Find its speed.
> v = 0.35 × 2000 = **700 m/s**.

The speed of sound depends on the medium and temperature: about **346 m/s in air at 25 °C**, faster in water and fastest in solids (≈5000 m/s in steel). Light is much faster, which is why we see lightning before hearing thunder.

## Reflection of sound
Sound reflects like light. An **echo** is a reflected sound heard separately. Because our ear keeps a sound for about **0.1 s**, the reflecting surface must be at least about **17.2 m** away (in air at 22 °C).

**Reverberation** is the persistence of sound from repeated reflections; auditoriums reduce it with sound-absorbing panels, curtains and seat fabric. Megaphones, stethoscopes and curved ceilings use repeated reflection.

## Range of hearing
Humans hear **20 Hz to 20,000 Hz**. Below 20 Hz is **infrasound** (elephants, whales, earthquakes); above 20 kHz is **ultrasound** (dogs, bats, dolphins).

> [!KEY] Uses of ultrasound
> Cleaning hard-to-reach parts, detecting cracks in metal, **echocardiography** (heart), **ultrasonography** (internal organs, foetus), breaking kidney stones, and **SONAR** (finding ocean depth and underwater objects).

> [!EXAMPLE] SONAR
> A ship's ultrasound pulse returns from the sea-bed after 2 s. Speed in water is 1500 m/s.
> Total distance = 1500 × 2 = 3000 m, so depth = **1500 m** (half, because the sound goes down and back).

## Quick recap
- Sound is a longitudinal wave of compressions and rarefactions; needs a medium.
- v = λν; frequency → pitch, amplitude → loudness.
- Echo needs at least 17.2 m; ultrasound has many uses including SONAR.
