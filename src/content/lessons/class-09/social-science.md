=== the-french-revolution ===
## The big idea
In 1789 the people of France rose against a king who ruled with absolute power and a society that made the poor pay for the luxuries of the rich. The French Revolution gave the world the ideas of **liberty, equality and fraternity**, and the belief that power belongs to the people, not to a king chosen by birth or "by God".

> [!KEY] Key dates at a glance
> **1774** Louis XVI becomes king · **5 May 1789** Estates General meets · **20 June 1789** Tennis Court Oath · **14 July 1789** Storming of the Bastille · **1791** Constitution makes France a constitutional monarchy · **1792** France becomes a republic · **1793** Louis XVI executed; Reign of Terror (1793–94) · **1795** Directory · **1799** Napoleon seizes power · **1848** slavery finally abolished in French colonies · **1946** women in France get the vote.

## French society in the late 18th century
When Louis XVI (of the **Bourbon** family) became king at the age of 20, the treasury was empty. Long wars, the upkeep of the grand palace at **Versailles**, and helping the thirteen American colonies win independence from Britain had pushed France into a debt of over **2 billion livres**. To meet costs, the state raised taxes.

French society was divided into three **Estates** (the "Old Regime", or *ancien régime*):

| Estate | Who | Tax position |
|---|---|---|
| First | Clergy | Exempt from taxes to the state |
| Second | Nobility | Exempt; enjoyed feudal privileges |
| Third | Merchants, lawyers, officials, peasants, artisans, servants (about 90% of people) | Paid all the taxes |

Peasants paid **taille** (a direct tax to the state), indirect taxes on salt and tobacco, **tithes** (one-tenth of produce to the Church) and feudal dues to nobles.

**Subsistence crisis:** population rose from about 23 million (1715) to 28 million (1789). Grain production could not keep up, so the price of bread (the staple food) shot up while wages did not. A drought or hailstorm could leave the poor without food. This is called a subsistence crisis.

**The middle class** (merchants, lawyers, administrators) were educated and wealthy, yet had no privileges. They were inspired by philosophers:
- **John Locke** (Two Treatises of Government) rejected the divine and absolute right of the king.
- **Rousseau** (The Social Contract) proposed government based on a contract between people and their representatives.
- **Montesquieu** (The Spirit of the Laws) proposed **separation of powers** among the legislative, executive and judiciary.

## Outbreak of the revolution
On **5 May 1789** Louis XVI called the **Estates General** (last called in 1614) to approve new taxes. Each Estate traditionally had one vote. The Third Estate demanded **one vote per member**. When the king refused, its members walked out and on **20 June** gathered at an indoor tennis court, declaring themselves the **National Assembly** and swearing not to disperse until they had drafted a constitution (the **Tennis Court Oath**). They were led by **Mirabeau** (a noble) and **Abbé Sieyès** (a priest, author of the pamphlet "What is the Third Estate?").

On **14 July 1789** an angry crowd stormed the **Bastille**, a fortress-prison hated as a symbol of the king's despotic power. In the countryside, rumours spread that lords had hired bands to destroy crops; in this **Great Fear** peasants burnt manor records. The king gave in and on the night of **4 August 1789** the Assembly abolished feudal obligations and tithes; Church lands were confiscated.

The **Constitution of 1791** made France a **constitutional monarchy**: the king's powers were divided among the legislature, executive and judiciary. But only **active citizens** (men over 25 who paid taxes equal to at least 3 days' wages) could vote. Women, children and poor men were **passive citizens**. The Constitution began with the **Declaration of the Rights of Man and Citizen** (right to life, freedom of speech, freedom of opinion, equality before law).

## Abolition of monarchy and the Reign of Terror
The **Jacobin Club**, led by **Maximilien Robespierre**, drew poorer sections: shopkeepers, artisans, servants and daily wagers. They wore long striped trousers and were called **sans-culottes** ("those without knee breeches"). In August 1792 they stormed the Tuileries palace. Elections were held in which all men over 21 could vote, and the new assembly, the **Convention**, abolished the monarchy on **21 September 1792**. France became a **republic**. Louis XVI was executed for treason in **January 1793**, and Queen Marie Antoinette soon after.

**Reign of Terror (1793–1794):** Robespierre punished all those he saw as enemies of the republic with the **guillotine**. He also ordered rationing of bread (the "bread of equality"), fixed maximum wages and prices, and closed churches. In **July 1794** he himself was guillotined.

After this a **Directory** of five members ran the government, but political instability let **Napoleon Bonaparte** seize power in 1799. He crowned himself emperor in 1804.

> [!MISTAKE] Republic vs constitutional monarchy
> In 1791 France still had a king, with limited powers. It became a **republic** only in **1792**. Do not mix the two.

## Did women have a revolution?
Women of the Third Estate worked as seamstresses, laundresses, flower and vegetable sellers, or domestic servants. They had little access to education or job training. They started around **60 women's clubs**; the most famous was the **Society of Revolutionary and Republican Women**. They demanded the right to vote, to be elected and to hold office.

Early gains: state schools and compulsory schooling for girls; marriage became a contract entered freely and registered under civil law; divorce was made legal. But during the Terror women's clubs were closed. **Olympe de Gouges** wrote a declaration of the rights of women and was executed in 1793. French women won the vote only in **1946**.

**Slavery:** France ran a triangular slave trade between Europe, Africa and the Americas (ports Bordeaux and Nantes). The Convention freed slaves in French colonies in **1794**, Napoleon reintroduced slavery, and it was finally abolished in **1848**.

## Legacy
- Ideas of **liberty and democratic rights** spread across Europe in the 19th century; feudal systems were abolished.
- Colonised peoples (for example **Tipu Sultan** and **Raja Rammohan Roy** in India) were inspired by the ideas of the Revolution.
- Censorship ended; newspapers, pamphlets, plays and songs spread ideas to people who could not read.

> [!REMEMBER] Symbols of the Revolution
> Broken chain (freedom), bundle of rods or fasces (strength lies in unity), eye within a triangle radiating light (knowledge), sceptre (royal power), snake biting its tail (eternity), red Phrygian cap (freedom), blue-white-red (national colours), winged woman (the law), law tablet (equality before law).

> [!TIP] Board favourite
> "Explain the causes of the French Revolution" is asked almost every year. Write: social inequality of estates, empty treasury and taxes, subsistence crisis, rise of the middle class, and philosophers' ideas.

## Quick recap
- Three Estates: only the Third Estate paid taxes.
- 1789: Estates General → Tennis Court Oath → fall of Bastille (14 July).
- 1791 constitutional monarchy; 1792 republic; 1793–94 Reign of Terror under Robespierre.
- Women's clubs fought for rights; vote only in 1946. Slavery abolished in 1848.
- Legacy: liberty, equality, fraternity and the idea of people's sovereignty.

W[3]: Describe the circumstances that led to the outbreak of revolutionary protest in France.
A: (1) French society was divided into three Estates; the clergy and nobility enjoyed privileges and only the Third Estate paid taxes such as taille, tithes and feudal dues.
(2) The treasury was empty because of long wars, the cost of the court at Versailles and help to the American colonies, so the king raised taxes.
(3) Population growth caused a subsistence crisis: bread prices rose faster than wages.
(4) The educated middle class, inspired by Locke, Rousseau and Montesquieu, demanded an end to privileges by birth.
(5) When Louis XVI refused one vote per member at the Estates General (May 1789), the Third Estate formed the National Assembly and the Bastille was stormed on 14 July 1789.

W[5]: What were the main features of the Constitution of 1791? Which groups did it leave out?
A: Features: France became a constitutional monarchy; the powers of the king were separated and given to the legislature, executive and judiciary; the National Assembly was indirectly elected; it began with the Declaration of the Rights of Man and Citizen (right to life, freedom of speech and opinion, equality before law).
Groups left out: only men above 25 paying taxes equal to 3 days' wages were active citizens who could vote. Women, children, the poor and men who paid no tax were passive citizens with no vote. So the constitution did not give political rights to all.

=== socialism-in-europe-and-the-russian-revolution ===
## The big idea
After the French Revolution, Europeans argued about how much society should change. Some wanted almost nothing to change, some wanted gradual reform, and some wanted to overturn society completely. In **Russia in 1917**, a revolution brought the **Bolsheviks** to power and created the world's first **socialist** state, which then influenced the whole twentieth century.

## Age of social change: liberals, radicals, conservatives
| Group | What they wanted |
|---|---|
| **Liberals** | A nation tolerating all religions; individual rights against governments; elected parliament and independent judiciary. But they were **not democrats**: they wanted voting rights only for **men with property**. |
| **Radicals** | Government based on the majority; supported women's suffrage; opposed privileges of big landowners and factory owners (but did not oppose private property itself). |
| **Conservatives** | Opposed radicals and liberals; after the French Revolution accepted that some change was inevitable but wanted it **slow**, respecting the past. |

**Industrial society** brought long working hours, poor wages, unemployment, and housing and sanitation problems in fast-growing towns. This raised the "social question".

**Socialism:** socialists were against **private property**, which they saw as the root of social ills. They wanted property to be controlled by society as a whole.
- **Robert Owen** (England) built a cooperative community called **New Harmony** in Indiana (USA).
- **Louis Blanc** (France) wanted the government to encourage cooperatives.
- **Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels** argued that industrial society was **capitalist**: capitalists owned the capital invested in factories, while workers' labour created the profit. Workers had to overthrow capitalism and build a **communist society** where all property is socially controlled.

By the 1870s socialists formed the **Second International**. In Britain socialists and trade unionists formed the **Labour Party** (1905); in Germany, the **Social Democratic Party (SPD)**.

## The Russian Revolution
**Russia under the Tsar:** In 1914 **Tsar Nicholas II** ruled Russia and its empire. About **85%** of the people were farmers. Industry was small (St Petersburg and Moscow), and workers worked up to 15 hours. Peasants wanted the nobles' land and had their own commune, the **mir**.

The **Russian Social Democratic Workers Party** (founded 1898) split into the **Bolsheviks**, led by **Vladimir Lenin**, who wanted a disciplined party of committed revolutionaries, and the **Mensheviks**, who wanted a party open to all, as in Germany.

**1905 Revolution:** On **Bloody Sunday** (January 1905) workers led by **Father Gapon** marched to the Winter Palace and over 100 were killed by police. Strikes followed and the Tsar allowed an elected parliament, the **Duma**, but he dismissed it repeatedly.

**First World War (1914):** Russia lost heavily; by 1917, about 7 million Russians had died. Retreating soldiers destroyed crops, refugees flooded in, and bread shortages and food riots spread. Industries closed.

**February Revolution (1917):** In Petrograd, workers struck (women led many strikes), and soldiers refused to fire on them. On **2 March 1917** the Tsar **abdicated**. A **Provisional Government** of Duma leaders and the **Petrograd Soviet** (council of workers and soldiers) took charge.

**April Theses:** Lenin returned in April 1917 and put forward three demands: end the war, transfer land to peasants, and nationalise banks. He also wanted the Bolsheviks renamed the **Communist Party**.

**October Revolution (1917):** On **24 October 1917**, the Bolsheviks, through a **Military Revolutionary Committee** led by **Leon Trotsky**, seized government offices; the Provisional Government fell. (Under the old Julian calendar these dates are February and October; by our calendar they fall in March and November.)

**After October:** industries and banks were nationalised (November 1917); land became social property and peasants seized nobles' land; old titles banned; Bolsheviks renamed the **Russian Communist Party (Bolshevik)**; the **Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (March 1918)** ended the war with Germany. A **civil war** followed between the "**reds**" (Bolsheviks) and the "**whites**" (pro-Tsarists) and "**greens**" (Socialist Revolutionaries). In **1922** the **USSR** was created. Russia became a one-party state, and centralised planning began with **Five Year Plans** (from 1927).

> [!MISTAKE] February vs October
> The **February** Revolution removed the Tsar. The **October** Revolution brought the **Bolsheviks** to power by overthrowing the Provisional Government. Do not swap them.

## Stalinism and collectivisation
After Lenin died (1924), **Joseph Stalin** led the party. Grain shortages in towns (1927–28) led him to blame rich peasants, the **kulaks**. From **1929** he forced **collectivisation**: peasants had to work on large state-controlled farms called **kolkhoz**, sharing the profit. Those who resisted were punished, deported or exiled. Production did not immediately rise; the famine of **1930–33** killed over **4 million** people. Many critics within the party were falsely accused, jailed or executed.

## Global influence
- Communist parties formed in many countries, such as the Communist Party of Great Britain.
- The Bolsheviks encouraged colonial peoples; the **Conference of the Peoples of the East (1920)** and the **Comintern** (an international union of pro-Bolshevik socialist parties) were set up.
- **Indians** were influenced too: **M.N. Roy** was a founder of the Mexican Communist Party; the **Communist Party of India** was formed in the 1920s. **Rabindranath Tagore** wrote "Letters from Russia" (1930–31).
- By the 1950s it was clear that the USSR, although industrialised, denied freedom to its citizens; it collapsed in **1991**. Yet socialist ideals of fair distribution of wealth still inspire people.

> [!TIP] Board favourite
> "Write the reasons for the collapse of the Tsarist autocracy in 1917" and "Explain the changes brought by the Bolsheviks after October 1917" are classic 5-mark questions.

## Quick recap
- Liberals (property-owning men vote), radicals (majority rule), conservatives (slow change).
- Socialists opposed private property; Marx and Engels predicted a communist society.
- Bloody Sunday 1905 → Duma; WWI hardships → February 1917 (Tsar abdicates) → October 1917 (Bolsheviks under Lenin).
- Stalin: collectivisation from 1929, kulaks targeted, famine 1930–33.
- USSR formed 1922 and broke up 1991; ideas of socialism spread worldwide, including to India.

W[5]: What were the main changes brought about by the Bolsheviks immediately after the October Revolution?
A: (1) Most industry and banks were nationalised in November 1917; the government took over ownership and management.
(2) Land was declared social property and peasants were allowed to seize the land of the nobility.
(3) In cities, large houses were partitioned according to family requirements.
(4) The use of old titles of aristocracy was banned; new uniforms were designed for the army and officials.
(5) The party was renamed the Russian Communist Party (Bolshevik); Russia became a one-party state, and the secret police (Cheka, later OGPU and NKVD) punished critics.
(6) Russia withdrew from the First World War by the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (1918).

W[3]: What was collectivisation? Why did Stalin introduce it?
A: Collectivisation was Stalin's programme from 1929 of forcing peasants to give up their small farms and work together on large state-controlled collective farms (kolkhoz), sharing the profits. Stalin introduced it because towns faced grain shortages in 1927–28; he believed small farms could not be modernised and that rich peasants (kulaks) were hoarding grain. Collectivisation would allow mechanised farming and a steady grain supply. Peasants who resisted were punished or deported, and a famine in 1930–33 killed over 4 million people.

=== nazism-and-the-rise-of-hitler ===
## The big idea
In the 1930s Germany fell under the control of **Adolf Hitler** and his **Nazi** party, who destroyed democracy, glorified war and carried out the **Holocaust**, the murder of about six million Jews. This chapter explains how a democratic republic collapsed into dictatorship and what the Nazis believed.

> [!KEY] Key dates
> **1918** Germany defeated in WWI; Weimar Republic formed · **1919** Treaty of Versailles · **1923** hyperinflation · **1929** Great Depression · **30 Jan 1933** Hitler becomes Chancellor · **28 Feb 1933** Fire Decree · **3 Mar 1933** Enabling Act · **1939** Germany invades Poland; WWII begins · **1941** Germany attacks USSR; USA joins war after Pearl Harbor · **May 1945** Germany surrenders; **Aug 1945** atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

## Birth of the Weimar Republic
Germany fought the First World War (1914–18) alongside Austria against the **Allies** (England, France, Russia). Defeated, the emperor abdicated and a National Assembly met at **Weimar** to set up a democratic, federal constitution. Deputies were elected with equal and universal votes, including women.

**Problems of the Republic:**
- The **Treaty of Versailles (1919)** was harsh: Germany lost its overseas colonies, **13% of its territory**, 75% of its iron and 26% of its coal; it had to demilitarise and pay **war compensation of £6 billion**. The Allies occupied the resource-rich **Rhineland**. Many Germans blamed the Republic and called its supporters the "**November criminals**".
- **Political radicalism:** the **Spartacist League**, a Bolshevik-style uprising, was crushed by the Republic with the help of the **Free Corps**.
- **Hyperinflation (1923):** when Germany could not pay, France occupied the **Ruhr** (its coal area). Germany printed paper money recklessly; the mark collapsed (a dollar cost 4 trillion marks). The USA rescued Germany with the **Dawes Plan**.
- **Great Depression (1929):** after the **Wall Street crash** American loans were withdrawn. By 1932 German industrial production fell to 40% of 1929 levels, and about **6 million** were unemployed.
- **Weak constitution:** **proportional representation** made it nearly impossible for one party to win a majority, leading to coalitions. **Article 48** let the President impose emergency, suspend civil rights and rule by decree. People lost faith in democracy.

## Hitler's rise to power
Hitler, born in Austria in 1889, joined the **German Workers' Party**, renamed it the **National Socialist German Workers' Party (Nazi Party)**. In 1923 he tried to seize Bavaria and capture Berlin but failed. During the Depression Nazism became a mass movement: in **1932** the Nazis got **37%** of the vote and became the largest party.

Why people supported him: he was a powerful speaker, promised to build a strong nation, undo Versailles, restore dignity, give jobs and a secure future to the young. The Nazis used **propaganda**: massive rallies, red banners with the **swastika**, the Nazi salute, and Hitler projected as a saviour.

**Destruction of democracy:**
- **30 January 1933**: President **Hindenburg** offered Hitler the Chancellorship.
- **Fire Decree (28 February 1933)**: after a mysterious fire in the parliament building (Reichstag), civic rights such as freedom of speech, press and assembly were suspended indefinitely.
- Communists were hurriedly packed off to **concentration camps**.
- **Enabling Act (3 March 1933)**: established dictatorship; Hitler could bypass parliament and rule by decree. All parties except the Nazi party were banned; trade unions too.
- Special forces: **SA (Storm Troopers)**, **Gestapo** (secret state police), **SS** (protection squads), criminal police and the Security Service.

**Economy and foreign policy:** **Hjalmar Schacht** aimed at full production and employment through state-funded work projects (the famous **Volkswagen** and **autobahns**). Germany pulled out of the **League of Nations** (1933), reoccupied the Rhineland (1936), integrated Austria (1938) under the slogan "**One people, One empire, One leader**", and took the German-speaking Sudetenland of Czechoslovakia. In **September 1939** Germany invaded **Poland**, starting the Second World War. In 1940 a **Tripartite Pact** was signed between Germany, Italy and Japan. Hitler's attack on the **USSR in 1941** was a historic blunder; the defeat at **Stalingrad** turned the tide. After Japan bombed **Pearl Harbor**, the USA entered the war. It ended in **May 1945** with Hitler's defeat and the US atomic bombs on Japan in August.

## The Nazi worldview
- **No equality**: there was a **racial hierarchy**. **Nordic German Aryans** were at the top; **Jews** were at the bottom, seen as an "anti-race". Blacks, Russians and Poles also lay low.
- Borrowed from **Charles Darwin** and **Herbert Spencer** ("survival of the fittest"), twisted to justify that the strongest race survives.
- **Lebensraum** (living space): acquire new territories for settlement, especially in the East (Poland).
- Only "pure and healthy Nordic Aryans" were desirable. Others, even Germans seen as impure or abnormal, were killed under the **Euthanasia Programme**; Gypsies and Blacks were persecuted.

**The Holocaust:** Jews were excluded step by step: the **Nuremberg Laws (1935)** took away citizenship and banned marriages between Jews and Germans; the **Kristallnacht** ("night of broken glass", November 1938) saw synagogues and shops attacked; Jews were moved to **ghettos** and then to gas chambers in camps such as **Auschwitz**. About six million Jews were killed. The Nazis used **euphemisms**: "**Final Solution**" for genocide, "**special treatment**" and "**evacuation**" for killing and deportation, "**disinfection areas**" for gas chambers.

## Youth and women in Nazi Germany
**Youth:** schools were "cleansed" of Jewish and "politically unreliable" teachers; textbooks rewritten. At **10**, boys entered **Jungvolk**; at **14**, the **Hitler Youth**; at 18, the Labour Service, then the armed forces. They were taught to hate Jews, communists and Gypsies and to worship war.

**Women:** boys were taught to be aggressive; girls were told to be good mothers and rear **pure-blooded Aryan children**. Women who bore racially desirable children were rewarded with privileges and the **Honour Cross** (bronze for four children, silver for six, gold for eight or more). Women who married Jews or other "undesirables" were publicly punished.

> [!MISTAKE] Nazism is not just "dictatorship"
> The key exam word is **racism**. The Nazi state was built on a racial hierarchy and the idea of Lebensraum. Mention both in any "Nazi ideology" answer.

> [!TIP] Board favourite
> "Why did Nazism become popular in Germany by 1930?" Combine: Versailles humiliation, economic crises (1923 and 1929), weak Weimar constitution (proportional representation, Article 48), Hitler's oratory and propaganda.

## Quick recap
- Weimar Republic burdened by Versailles, hyperinflation and the Depression; Article 48 and proportional representation weakened it.
- Hitler became Chancellor in 1933; Fire Decree and Enabling Act ended democracy.
- Nazi ideology: racial hierarchy, Lebensraum, hatred of Jews → Holocaust.
- Children were trained through Jungvolk and Hitler Youth; women were valued as mothers of Aryan children.
- WWII: 1939 (Poland) to 1945.

W[5]: Discuss why Nazism became popular in Germany by 1930.
A: (1) Germans felt humiliated by the harsh Treaty of Versailles (loss of colonies and territory, huge war compensation) and blamed the Weimar Republic.

Women who did not follow this code, or married Jews, Poles or Russians, were publicly condemned and severely punished.

=== forest-society-and-colonialism ===
## The big idea
Forests give us wood, fruit, medicines, fodder and a home to millions of forest dwellers. Under colonial rule, forests across India and Indonesia (Java) were cut down on a huge scale and forest people lost their traditional rights. This chapter shows **why** deforestation happened, **how** commercial forestry changed lives, and **how** people resisted.

> [!KEY] Deforestation
> **Deforestation** means the disappearance of forests. Between **1880 and 1920**, cultivated area in India rose by about **6.7 million hectares**, much of it carved out of forests.

## Why deforestation?
1. **Land to be improved:** In 1600 about one-sixth of India was under cultivation; by 1980 it was about half. The British encouraged growing **commercial crops** like jute, sugar, wheat and cotton to feed Europe's growing population and supply raw material for industry. They also saw forests as "unproductive wilderness".
2. **Sleepers on the tracks:** By the early 19th century, **oak forests in England** were disappearing, creating a shortage of timber for the **Royal Navy**. Search parties went to India. From the 1850s, the spread of **railways** created a new demand: wooden **sleepers** to hold the tracks, and wood to fuel locomotives. Each mile of track needed **1,760 to 2,000 sleepers**. By 1890, about 25,500 km of track had been laid; by 1946, over 7,65,000 km.
3. **Plantations:** large areas of natural forest were cleared to make way for **tea, coffee and rubber** plantations, given to Europeans at cheap rates.

## Commercial forestry
The British were worried that local people and traders would destroy forests. They invited a German expert, **Dietrich Brandis**, who became the first **Inspector General of Forests in India**. He set up:
- the **Indian Forest Service** in **1864**,
- the **Indian Forest Act** of **1865**,
- the **Imperial Forest Research Institute at Dehradun** in **1906**, which taught "**scientific forestry**".

In scientific forestry, natural forests with many kinds of trees were cut down and replaced by **plantations** of one type of tree, planted in straight rows. Forest officials planned how much to cut and how much to replant.

The **Forest Act was amended in 1878 and 1927**. The 1878 Act divided forests into three categories:
- **Reserved forests** (the best): villagers could not take anything from them, even for their own use.
- **Protected forests**.
- **Village forests**.

**Effects on people:** Villagers needed forests for fuelwood, fodder, fruits, roots, herbs, bamboo for fences and baskets, and the **mahua** tree for oil and cooking. Now cutting wood, grazing cattle, collecting fruits, hunting and fishing became illegal. People were forced to steal wood; forest guards harassed them and demanded bribes.

**Shifting cultivation** (also called **swidden** agriculture; known locally as **lading** in South East Asia, **milpa** in Central America, **chitemene or tavy** in Africa, **chena** in Sri Lanka, **dhya, penda, bewar, nevad, jhum, podu, khandad and kumri** in India) was banned. Parts of the forest are cut and burnt in rotation; seeds are sown in the ashes after the first monsoon rains. The British said it made it hard to grow timber and risked forest fires, and it was hard to tax.

**Hunting:** Earlier, hunting by forest dwellers was banned, while hunting big game became a sport for rulers and the British. The British gave rewards for killing tigers, wolves and other animals as "dangerous". Over **80,000 tigers, 1,50,000 leopards and 2,00,000 wolves** were killed for reward between 1875 and 1925.

**New trades and work:** some groups lost their livelihood; others, like the **Banjaras** (graziers) and the **Santhals and Oraons** of Jharkhand and the Gonds of Chhattisgarh, were recruited to work on tea plantations in Assam on very low wages and could not easily return home.

## Rebellion in the forest: Bastar
**Bastar** is located in the southernmost part of **Chhattisgarh**, bordering Andhra Pradesh, Odisha and Maharashtra. The **Indrawati river** flows across it from east to west. Communities include Maria and Muria Gonds, Dhurwas, Bhatras and Halbas.

When the colonial government proposed to reserve **two-thirds** of the forest in **1905**, stop shifting cultivation, hunting and collection of forest produce, people were angry. Some villages were allowed to stay in reserved forests only if they worked free for the forest department (**forest villages**). After famines in 1899–1900 and 1907–08, people rebelled in **1910**. Leaders included **Gunda Dhur** of village Nethanar. Bazaars were looted, houses of officials and traders burnt. The British sent troops and crushed the rebellion in three months, but **work on reservation was suspended** and the area to be reserved was reduced to roughly half.

## Forest transformations in Java
**Java** in Indonesia was colonised by the **Dutch**, who also started forest management there. The **Kalang** people were skilled forest cutters and shifting cultivators; in 1755 they were divided between the two kingdoms of Java. The Dutch needed Javan timber to build ships.

- In the 19th century the Dutch enacted forest laws restricting villagers' access.
- They imposed rents on land being cultivated in the forest and then exempted some villages if they collectively provided free labour and buffaloes for cutting and transporting timber. This was called the **blandongdiensten** system.
- Later, villagers were given small wages but their right to cultivate forest land was restricted.

**Samin's challenge:** Around **1890**, **Surontiko Samin** of Randublatung village (a teak forest village) began questioning state ownership of the forest, arguing that the state had not created the wind, water, earth and wood. By 1907, about **3,000 families** followed his ideas; some lay down on their land when surveyors came, others refused to pay taxes or fines.

**World Wars:** the Dutch followed a "**scorched earth**" policy, destroying sawmills and burning huge piles of teak logs so they would not fall into Japanese hands. The Japanese then exploited the forests recklessly.

=== pastoralists-in-the-modern-world ===
## The big idea
**Pastoralists** are people who rear and herd animals such as sheep, goats, camels and cattle. Many are **nomads**, who move from place to place with their herds in search of pasture. Their movements are not random; they are a clever adaptation to seasons and environment. Colonial rule, however, made their lives much harder in both **India** and **Africa**.

## Pastoral nomads and their movements
| Community | Region | Animals | Movement |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gujjar Bakarwals** | Jammu and Kashmir | goats and sheep | Winter in the low hills of the **Siwalik** range; in April move north to summer pastures; groups called **kafila** |
| **Gaddi** shepherds | Himachal Pradesh | sheep | Winter in the low Siwalik hills; summer in **Lahul and Spiti** and higher meadows |
| **Gujjar** cattle herders | Garhwal and Kumaon | cattle | Winter in the dry forests of the **bhabar**; summer in high meadows called **bugyals** |
| **Bhotiyas, Sherpas, Kinnauris** | Himalayas | various | Similar cyclic seasonal movement |
| **Dhangars** | Maharashtra | sheep, buffaloes; some weave blankets | Monsoon in the central plateau (dry, semi-arid); after the October harvest move west to the **Konkan**, where farmers welcome their manure |
| **Gollas, Kurumas, Kurubas** | Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh | cattle, sheep and goats | Move between dry plateau (dry season) and wooded areas, coast (monsoon); some sold woven blankets |
| **Banjaras** | UP, Punjab, Rajasthan, MP, Maharashtra | cattle | Long journeys selling plough cattle and goods for grain and fodder |
| **Raikas** | Rajasthan desert | camels, goats (Maru Raikas) | Combine cultivation with herding; leave villages when monsoon pastures dry up |

**Why move?** Movement allows pastures to **recover** (regenerate) and prevents overgrazing. Herders combine many activities: cultivation, trade and herding. They must judge how long to stay, where to find water and pasture, and maintain relationships with farmers along the way.

## Colonial rule and pastoral life
Pastoralists' lives changed dramatically under colonial rule:
1. **Waste Land Rules:** the colonial state saw uncultivated land as "wasteland" that gave no revenue. From the mid-19th century **Waste Land Rules** were enacted; such land was given to select individuals for cultivation. Often this land was pastoral grazing land, so pasture shrank.
2. **Forest Acts:** by the mid-19th century forests that produced valuable timber (deodar, sal) were declared **Reserved**; pastoralists were barred. In **Protected** forests some grazing rights were allowed but movements were restricted. They needed permits and could stay only for a fixed number of days.
3. **Criminal Tribes Act (1871):** many communities of craftsmen, traders and pastoralists were classified as **criminal tribes**, "criminal by nature and birth". They had to live in notified village settlements and could not move without a permit; police watched them.
4. **Grazing tax:** introduced in the mid-19th century; tax per head of cattle. Pastoralists had to carry a pass; the right to collect tax was auctioned to contractors, who extracted high taxes.

**How did they cope?** Some reduced the number of cattle; others found new pastures (after **1947** some Raikas could no longer go to Sindh, now in Pakistan, and moved to Haryana where farmers welcomed them after harvest). Some bought land and settled; some, poorer ones, became labourers. Many continue to adapt, and still survive today.

## Pastoralism in Africa
More than half the world's pastoral population lives in **Africa**: over **22 million** Africans depend on some form of pastoral activity. Communities include **Bedouins, Berbers, Maasai, Somali, Boran and Turkana**.

**The Maasai** live in **East Africa** (about 3,00,000 in southern **Kenya** and around 1,50,000 in **Tanzania**). Their name for themselves derives from *Maa*; "Maasai" means "my people".
- **Loss of land:** In **1885** Maasailand was cut in half by an international boundary between **British Kenya** and **German Tanganyika**. Maasai lost about **60%** of their pre-colonial land and were confined to arid zones with poor rainfall and pasture.
- **Game reserves:** large areas were turned into reserves such as the **Maasai Mara** and **Samburu National Park** (Kenya) and the **Serengeti National Park** (Tanzania, created over 14,760 sq km of Maasai grazing land), where pastoralists could not enter.
- **Drought:** confined to a fixed area, they could not move in drought. In 1933 and 1934 more than half the cattle in Maasai Reserve died.
- **Social changes:** traditionally society had **elders** (who decided disputes) and **warriors** (younger people who protected the tribe and organised cattle raids). The British appointed **chiefs** of different sub-groups and banned raiding and warfare, which weakened warriors' authority. Chiefs became wealthy, lent money and bought land, while poor pastoralists became labourers. The gap between rich and poor grew.

> [!MISTAKE] "Wasteland" was not useless land
> For the colonial state "wasteland" meant land that paid no revenue. For pastoralists it was vital grazing land. Use this contrast in your answer.

> [!TIP] Map point
> On an outline map of India you may be asked to locate the regions of the Gujjar Bakarwals (Jammu and Kashmir), Gaddis (Himachal Pradesh), Dhangars (Maharashtra), Raikas (Rajasthan) and Gollas/Kurumas (Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh).

## Quick recap
- Nomads move seasonally to let pastures recover; movement is planned, not random.
- Colonial laws hurt pastoralists: Waste Land Rules, Forest Acts, Criminal Tribes Act (1871), grazing tax.
- Pastoralists adapted by reducing herds, finding new pastures or settling.
- Maasai lost about 60% of land; game reserves and drought hurt them; British-appointed chiefs changed society.

W[5]: How did colonial rule affect the lives of pastoralists in India?

=== india-size-and-location ===
## The big idea
India is a large country lying entirely in the **Northern Hemisphere**, at the head of the Indian Ocean. Its size, its position between East and West Asia and its long coastline have shaped its climate, trade, culture and history.

> [!KEY] India's numbers
> **Latitudinal extent:** 8°4'N to 37°6'N · **Longitudinal extent:** 68°7'E to 97°25'E · **Area:** 3.28 million sq km (about **2.4%** of the world's land area) · **7th largest** country in the world · **Land boundary:** about **15,200 km** · **Coastline** (mainland plus islands): **7,516.6 km** · **Standard Meridian:** **82°30'E**, passing through **Mirzapur** (Uttar Pradesh).

## Location and size
The mainland of India extends between **8°4'N and 37°6'N** latitudes and **68°7'E and 97°25'E** longitudes. The **Tropic of Cancer (23°30'N)** divides the country into almost two equal parts. To the south-east and south-west of the mainland lie the **Andaman and Nicobar Islands** (Bay of Bengal) and the **Lakshadweep Islands** (Arabian Sea).

The **southernmost point** of the Indian Union is **Indira Point** (6°45'N) in the Great Nicobar Island; it got submerged under seawater in the **2004 tsunami**. The southernmost point of the mainland is **Kanyakumari**.

**Size:** India is the seventh largest country after Russia, Canada, USA, China, Brazil and Australia. The Himalayas lie in the north-west, north and north-east; south of about 22°N latitude India tapers and extends into the Indian Ocean, dividing it into the **Arabian Sea** (west) and the **Bay of Bengal** (east).

**East–west and north–south extent:** both are about **30 degrees**, yet the actual **north–south distance (about 3,214 km)** is greater than the east–west distance (about **2,933 km**). This is because the distance between longitudes decreases towards the poles, while the distance between latitudes stays the same.

**Time difference:** From **Gujarat** (west) to **Arunachal Pradesh** (east) there is a time lag of about **two hours** (30° × 4 minutes = 120 minutes). So India accepts the time along **82°30'E** as its **Indian Standard Time (IST)**. Latitude also matters: the duration of day and night varies more as we move from south to north (Kanyakumari has nearly equal day and night; Kashmir has big variations).

> [!EXAMPLE] Why is 82°30'E chosen?
> It lies roughly in the middle of India's longitudinal extent (68°7'E to 97°25'E), and it is a multiple of 7°30' (time zones are set at 7.5° intervals = 30 minutes). IST is 5 hours 30 minutes ahead of GMT.

## India and the world
- India lies centrally between **East and West Asia**, a southward extension of the Asian continent.
- The **trans-Indian Ocean routes** connecting Europe in the west and East Asia pass through India. The **Deccan Peninsula** protrudes into the Indian Ocean, helping India keep close contact with West Asia, Africa and Europe on the western coast and South-east and East Asia on the eastern coast.
- **No other country has a long coastline on the Indian Ocean as India**, and the ocean is named after India.
- Since the opening of the **Suez Canal in 1869**, India's distance from Europe has been reduced by **7,000 km**.
- India's contacts with the world are very old: ideas of the **Upanishads and the Ramayana**, the stories of the **Panchtantra**, **Indian numerals and the decimal system** reached many parts of the world. Spices and muslin were exported. In turn, Greek sculpture and the architecture of domes and minarets from West Asia came to India.
- The land routes, through mountain **passes** in the north (Khyber, Bolan, Shipkila, Nathula and others), have been used by travellers since ancient times.

## India's neighbours
India has **28 states and 8 Union Territories**. India shares land boundaries with:
- **Pakistan** and **Afghanistan** in the north-west,
- **China (Tibet), Nepal and Bhutan** in the north,
- **Myanmar** and **Bangladesh** in the east.

Southern neighbours across the sea are the island countries **Sri Lanka** and **Maldives**. **Sri Lanka** is separated from India by a narrow channel formed by the **Palk Strait** and the **Gulf of Mannar**. **Maldives** lies to the south of the Lakshadweep Islands.

- India lies wholly in the Northern Hemisphere, 8°4'N–37°6'N and 68°7'E–97°25'E.

=== physical-features-of-india ===
## The big idea
India has every kind of landform: the lofty **Himalayas**, the flat fertile **Northern Plains**, the ancient **Peninsular Plateau**, the sandy **Indian Desert**, long **Coastal Plains** and two groups of **islands**. Each was formed differently and each supports a different way of life.

## How the landforms were formed
According to the **Theory of Plate Tectonics**, the Earth's crust is made of large plates that move. Millions of years ago the **Indo-Australian plate** broke off from the super-continent **Gondwana land** and drifted north, colliding with the much larger **Eurasian plate**. The sedimentary rocks of the **Tethys** sea between them were folded up to form the **Himalayas**. The deep depression left was filled by sediments from rivers, forming the **Northern Plains**. The **Peninsular Plateau** is the oldest landmass, a part of Gondwana land, made of igneous and metamorphic rocks.

> [!KEY] Types of plate boundaries
> **Convergent** (plates come together; fold mountains like the Himalayas), **Divergent** (plates move apart), **Transform** (plates slide past each other).

## The Himalayan mountains
The Himalayas are **young fold mountains** stretching about **2,400 km** in an arc from the Indus to the Brahmaputra. Their width is **400 km in Kashmir** to **150 km in Arunachal Pradesh**. They have three parallel ranges:
1. **Great or Inner Himalayas (Himadri):** the most continuous range, average height **6,000 m**, with the loftiest peaks; core of granite; perennially snow-bound; many glaciers. Highest peaks: Mt. Everest (8,848 m), Kanchenjunga (8,598 m), Nanga Parbat, Nanda Devi.
2. **Lesser Himalaya (Himachal):** altitude **3,700–4,500 m**, average width 50 km. Ranges: **Pir Panjal** (longest), **Dhauladhar** and **Mahabharat**. Famous valleys: **Kashmir, Kangra and Kullu**, and well-known hill stations.
3. **Shiwaliks (Outer Himalayas):** altitude **900–1,100 m**, width 10–50 km; made of unconsolidated sediments. Longitudinal valleys between the Himachal and the Shiwaliks are called **Duns** (Dehra Dun, Kotli Dun, Patli Dun).

**Regional divisions (from west to east, by river valleys):**
- **Punjab Himalaya**: between Indus and Satluj (also Kashmir and Himachal Himalaya); Karakoram, Ladakh, Zaskar, Pir Panjal ranges.
- **Kumaon Himalaya**: between Satluj and Kali.
- **Nepal Himalaya**: between Kali and Teesta.
- **Assam Himalaya**: between Teesta and Dihang.

East of the **Dihang gorge** (Brahmaputra), the Himalayas bend south and spread along India's eastern boundary as the **Purvachal** or Eastern hills: **Patkai hills, Naga hills, Manipur hills and Mizo hills**, mostly sandstone.

## The Northern Plains
Formed by three river systems, the **Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra** and their tributaries. It covers about **7 lakh sq km**, about 2,400 km long and 240–320 km wide. It is densely populated and agriculturally very productive.
- **Punjab Plains** (west): formed by the Indus and its tributaries; much of it lies in Pakistan. Has many **doabs** (doab = land between two rivers; "do" = two, "ab" = water).
- **Ganga Plains**: between the Ghaggar and Teesta rivers; spread over north Indian states, Haryana, Delhi, UP, Bihar, part of Jharkhand and West Bengal.
- **Brahmaputra Plains**: in Assam.

**Relief features (north to south):**
| Belt | Feature |
|---|---|
| **Bhabar** | Narrow belt, 8–16 km wide, at the foot of the Shiwaliks; pebbles deposited; streams disappear |
| **Terai** | Streams re-emerge; wet, swampy, marshy; thick forests and wildlife (much cleared for agriculture) |
| **Bhangar** | Older alluvium, above flood level; contains calcareous deposits called **kankar** |
| **Khadar** | Newer, younger alluvium of flood plains; renewed every year; very fertile |

**Riverine islands** (like **Majuli** in the Brahmaputra, the largest inhabited riverine island) and **distributaries** are also features.

## The Peninsular Plateau
A **tableland** of old crystalline, igneous and metamorphic rocks; formed from Gondwana land. It has two divisions:
- **Central Highlands**: north of the **Narmada**; bounded by the **Vindhyan range** in the south and the **Aravalis** in the north-west. Wider in the west, narrower in the east. Eastward extensions are the **Bundelkhand and Baghelkhand**; the **Chotanagpur plateau** is drained by the Damodar. Rivers here flow from south-west to north-east.
- **Deccan Plateau**: triangular, south of the Narmada; bounded by the **Satpura range** in the north, the **Mahadev, Kaimur hills and Maikal range** in the east. It is higher in the west and slopes gently east. An extension is the **Meghalaya (Shillong) plateau** and **Karbi Anglong plateau** in the north-east, separated by a fault from Chotanagpur; Garo, Khasi and Jaintia hills.

**Western Ghats vs Eastern Ghats**:
| Western Ghats | Eastern Ghats |
|---|---|
| Lie parallel to the western coast | Stretch from Mahanadi valley to the Nilgiris in the south |
| **Continuous**; can be crossed only through passes (**Thal, Bhor and Pal Ghats**) | **Discontinuous** and irregular; dissected by rivers draining into the Bay of Bengal |
| Higher: average 900–1,600 m | Lower: average 600 m |
| Highest peaks: **Anai Mudi (2,695 m)**, **Doda Betta (2,637 m)** | Highest peak: **Mahendragiri (1,501 m)** |
| Cause orographic rain from moist winds | Receive less rain |

The **Eastern and Western Ghats meet at the Nilgiri hills**. The Deccan trap is a region of **black soil** of volcanic origin.

## The Indian Desert
Lies to the west of the **Aravali hills**. An undulating sandy plain covered with **sand dunes**; low rainfall, **below 150 mm** per year; arid climate with little vegetation. Streams appear in the rainy season and then disappear into the sand. **Luni** is the only large river. **Barchans** (crescent-shaped dunes) cover larger areas; longitudinal dunes are more prominent near the Indo-Pakistan border.

## The coastal plains and islands
- **Western coast** (between the Western Ghats and the Arabian Sea): narrow. Three sections: **Konkan** (Mumbai–Goa), **Kannad plain** (central), **Malabar coast** (south).
- **Eastern coast** (along the Bay of Bengal): wide and level. **Northern Circar** (northern part) and **Coromandel Coast** (southern part). Large rivers (Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri) form extensive **deltas**. **Chilika Lake** (Odisha) is the largest saltwater lake in India.
- **Lakshadweep Islands**: in the Arabian Sea, close to the Malabar coast, made of small **coral** islands (earlier Laccadive, Minicoy and Amindive); area 32 sq km; administrative headquarters **Kavaratti**. Pitti island is uninhabited and has a bird sanctuary.
- **Andaman and Nicobar Islands**: an elongated chain in the Bay of Bengal, bigger and more numerous; believed to be elevated portions of submarine mountains. Divided into **Andaman** in the north and **Nicobar** in the south. **Barren Island**, India's only active volcano, is here. Rich in biodiversity; equatorial climate.

> [!MISTAKE] Bhabar vs Bhangar
> **Bhabar** is the pebble belt at the foot of the Shiwaliks. **Bhangar** is the older alluvial terrace of the plains. They sound alike but are very different.

> [!TIP] Map points
> Mountain ranges (Karakoram, Zaskar, Shiwalik, Aravali, Vindhya, Satpura, Western and Eastern Ghats); peaks (K2, Kanchenjunga, Anai Mudi); plateaus (Deccan, Chotanagpur, Malwa); coastal plains (Konkan, Malabar, Coromandel, Northern Circar).

## Quick recap
- Himalayas: young fold mountains with Himadri, Himachal and Shiwalik ranges; Duns; Purvachal in the east.
- Northern Plains: alluvial; bhabar, terai, bhangar, khadar; doabs.
- Peninsular Plateau: oldest; Central Highlands and Deccan; Western Ghats continuous, Eastern Ghats discontinuous.
- Indian Desert: west of Aravalis, barchans, Luni river.
- Coasts: Konkan, Kannad, Malabar (west); Northern Circar, Coromandel (east). Islands: Lakshadweep (coral), Andaman and Nicobar (volcanic origin, Barren Island).

=== drainage ===
## The big idea
**Drainage** describes the river system of an area. Water from rain and melting snow flows downhill into streams and rivers, which together form a network. India's rivers fall into two big groups, the **Himalayan** and the **Peninsular** rivers, which behave very differently.

> [!KEY] Key terms
> **Drainage basin:** the area drained by a single river system. **Water divide:** an upland (mountain or high ground) that separates two neighbouring drainage basins; for example, the **Ambala** area lies on the divide between the Indus and Ganga systems. **Perennial river:** flows all year. **Seasonal river:** flows mainly in the rainy season.

## Drainage patterns
- **Dendritic**: like the branches of a tree; forms where the rock is uniform (e.g. rivers of the northern plains).
- **Trellis**: tributaries join the main river at nearly right angles where hard and soft rocks lie parallel.
- **Rectangular**: develops on strongly jointed rocky terrain.
- **Radial**: streams flow in all directions from a central peak or dome (e.g. **Amarkantak** range).

## The Himalayan rivers
The major Himalayan rivers are the **Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra**. They are **perennial**, fed by both rain and **melted snow** from glaciers. They carve deep **gorges**, carry huge loads of silt and sand, form **meanders, oxbow lakes**, flood plains and **deltas** in their lower courses.

**The Indus system**
- Rises in **Tibet near Lake Mansarovar**. Flows west, enters India in **Ladakh** (Jammu and Kashmir/Ladakh), forming a picturesque gorge.
- Tributaries in Kashmir: **Zaskar, Nubra, Shyok and Hunza**. From the Punjab region: **Satluj, Beas, Ravi, Chenab and Jhelum** join together near **Mithankot** in Pakistan.
- Length: **2,900 km**; one of the longest rivers in the world. Only a third of the basin lies in India (J&K, Himachal, Punjab); the rest in Pakistan.
- **Indus Water Treaty (1960):** India can use only **20%** of the total water carried by the Indus system.

**The Ganga system**
- Headwaters called the **Bhagirathi** are fed by the **Gangotri Glacier**; it joins the **Alaknanda** at **Devprayag** in Uttarakhand. The Ganga emerges onto the plains at **Haridwar**.
- Tributaries: **Yamuna** (rises from the Yamunotri Glacier, joins at **Prayagraj** (Allahabad)), Ghaghara, Gandak, Kosi (from Nepal; the Kosi is called the "sorrow of Bihar" for its floods). From the Peninsular uplands: **Chambal, Betwa and Son**.
- At **Farakka** in West Bengal it bifurcates: the **Bhagirathi-Hooghly** flows south through the deltaic plains to the Bay of Bengal; the main stream flows into **Bangladesh** and is joined by the Brahmaputra; further down it is called the **Meghna**.
- Length: over **2,500 km**. The **Ambala** is on the water divide between the Indus and Ganga systems.
- The **Sundarban Delta**, the world's **largest and fastest growing delta**, is formed by the Ganga and Brahmaputra; named after the **Sundari** tree that grows in marshland; home to the Royal Bengal tiger.

**The Brahmaputra system**
- Rises in **Tibet** east of Mansarovar lake, very close to the sources of the Indus and Satluj. Slightly longer than the Indus; most of its course lies outside India.
- Flows east parallel to the Himalayas in Tibet, where it is called **Tsang Po**. Reaching **Namcha Barwa** (7,757 m), it takes a U-turn and enters **Arunachal Pradesh** through a gorge. Here it is called the **Dihang**. It is joined by the **Dibang, Lohit** and many tributaries to form the Brahmaputra in **Assam**.
- In Tibet it carries less water and silt (cold, dry area); in India it carries a large volume of water and silt (heavy rainfall). It forms a **braided channel** with many riverine islands, including **Majuli**, the largest inhabited riverine island in the world. Floods in Assam and Bangladesh are common.

## The Peninsular rivers
The main water divide in Peninsular India is formed by the **Western Ghats**. Most rivers flow **east** into the Bay of Bengal and form **deltas** (Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri). Only the **Narmada and Tapi** flow **west** and make **estuaries**. Peninsular rivers are mostly **seasonal**, with shorter and shallower courses.

| River | Source | Key facts |
|---|---|---|
| **Narmada** | **Amarkantak hills**, Madhya Pradesh | Flows west in a **rift valley** formed by faulting; creates the **Marble Rocks** near Jabalpur and the **Dhuadhar falls**; tributaries are short and join at right angles; MP and Gujarat |
| **Tapi** | **Satpura ranges**, Betul district (MP) | Flows west in a rift valley parallel to the Narmada; MP, Gujarat and Maharashtra |
| **Godavari** | Slopes of Western Ghats, **Nasik** district (Maharashtra) | **Largest Peninsular river**, about **1,500 km**; called **Dakshin Ganga**; tributaries Purna, Wardha, Pranhita, Manjra, Wainganga, Penganga |

Smaller west-flowing rivers include the Sabarmati, Mahi, Bharathpuzha and Periyar.

## Lakes
India has many lakes, differing in size and character.
- **Permanent** vs **seasonal** (in inland drainage basins of semi-arid regions).
- **Oxbow lakes**: formed when a meandering river is cut off.
- **Lagoons**: formed by spits and bars in coastal areas, e.g. **Chilika** (Odisha), **Pulicat** (Andhra Pradesh–Tamil Nadu), **Kolleru** (Andhra Pradesh).
- **Salt lakes**: in inland drainage, e.g. **Sambhar Lake** in Rajasthan; its water is used for producing salt.
- **Freshwater lakes**: most are in the Himalayan region, mainly of **glacial** origin. **Wular Lake** (J&K) is the **largest freshwater lake**, formed by **tectonic activity**. Others: **Dal Lake, Bhimtal, Nainital, Loktak** and **Barapani**.
- **Man-made lakes**: created by damming rivers for hydel power, e.g. **Guru Gobind Sagar** (Bhakra Nangal project).

**Importance of lakes:** regulate river flow and prevent floods; maintain an even flow in dry seasons; generate hydel power; moderate climate; maintain aquatic ecosystems; enhance natural beauty; promote tourism and recreation.

## Role of rivers in the economy and river pollution
Rivers have been central to human history: settlements grew on their banks. River water is used for **irrigation, navigation, hydro-power generation**, drinking water and fishing. In India, an agricultural country, rivers are especially important.

**River pollution:** growing domestic, municipal, industrial and agricultural demand affects water quality. Untreated sewage and industrial waste are dumped into rivers. The **National River Conservation Plan (NRCP)** and the **Namami Gange** programme aim to clean rivers.

> [!MISTAKE] Delta vs estuary
> Narmada and Tapi flow into the Arabian Sea through **rift valleys** and form **estuaries**, not deltas, because they carry little silt and flow through hard rock with a steep gradient. East-flowing rivers form **deltas**.

> Rivers: Indus, Ganga, Yamuna, Brahmaputra, Narmada, Tapi, Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri, Mahanadi. Lakes: Wular, Dal, Sambhar, Chilika, Pulicat, Kolleru, Loktak.

=== climate ===
## The big idea
India has a **monsoon type of climate**. The word *monsoon* comes from the Arabic **mausim**, meaning season. Winds reverse their direction with the seasons, and almost all of India's rainfall comes in a few months. Understanding the monsoon explains India's seasons, its farming calendar, and even its festivals.

> [!KEY] Weather vs climate
> **Weather** is the state of the atmosphere over an area at any point of time. **Climate** is the sum total of weather conditions and variations over a large area for a long period of time (more than **thirty years**). Elements of both: temperature, atmospheric pressure, wind, humidity and precipitation.

## Variations within India
- **Temperature:** in summer the Rajasthan desert can touch **50°C**, while **Pahalgam** in Kashmir may be around 20°C. On a winter night **Drass** in Ladakh may drop to **minus 45°C**, while Thiruvananthapuram has about 22°C.
- **Precipitation:** upper Himalayas get snowfall; elsewhere rain. Annual rainfall ranges from over **400 cm in Meghalaya** to **less than 10 cm in Ladakh and western Rajasthan**. Most of India gets rain from **June to September**, but the **Tamil Nadu coast** gets much of its rain in **October and November**.
- Coastal areas have less contrast in temperature; interior areas have seasonal contrast (**continentality**).

## Factors affecting climate (climatic controls)
1. **Latitude:** temperature falls from the Equator to the poles.
2. **Altitude:** temperature decreases as height increases; hills are cooler.
3. **Pressure and wind system:** these depend on latitude and altitude and influence temperature and rainfall.
4. **Distance from the sea (continentality):** the sea moderates climate; interior places have extreme climate.
5. **Ocean currents** and **relief features** also matter: high mountains block cold or hot winds and cause rain on the windward side.

**Factors affecting India's climate:**
- **Latitude:** the **Tropic of Cancer** passes through the middle; the south is tropical, the north sub-tropical.
- **Altitude:** the Himalayas (average height about 6,000 m) **prevent cold winds from Central Asia** entering India, so the subcontinent has milder winters.
- **Pressure and winds:** India lies in the region of the **north-easterly winds**. These blow from the subtropical high-pressure belt of the Northern Hemisphere. In summer, winds reverse, and the **south-west monsoon** winds blow.
- **Upper air circulation:** **westerly jet stream** (south of the Himalayas in winter) brings **western cyclonic disturbances** to the north-west in winter; an **easterly jet stream** blows over the Peninsula in summer at about 14°N.
- **Western cyclonic disturbances:** low-pressure systems from the Mediterranean region that bring winter rain to the north-west.
- **Tropical cyclones:** form in the Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea in the monsoon and post-monsoon periods; affect the coasts.

> [!KEY] Jet streams
> **Jet streams** are a narrow belt of high-altitude (above 12,000 m) **westerly winds** in the troposphere. Their speed varies from about **110 km/h in summer to about 184 km/h in winter**.

## The Indian monsoon
The monsoon is experienced in the tropical area roughly between **20°N and 20°S**. Factors that explain its mechanism:
1. **Differential heating and cooling of land and water** creates low pressure over the Indian landmass and high pressure over the surrounding seas.
2. The shift of the **Inter Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ)** in summer over the **Ganga plain** (this is the equatorial trough, normally about 5°N; also called the **monsoon trough** during the monsoon).
3. The presence of a **high-pressure area east of Madagascar**, about 20°S over the Indian Ocean; its intensity and position affect the Indian monsoon.
4. The **Tibetan plateau** gets intensely heated in summer, causing strong vertical air currents and low pressure over it at about 9 km above sea level.
5. The movement of the **westerly jet stream** to the north of the Himalayas and the presence of the **tropical easterly jet stream** over the Peninsula during summer.
6. **El Niño:** warm ocean currents that flow past the coast of Peru instead of the cold Peruvian current, every two to five years; associated with weaker monsoons.
7. **Southern Oscillation (SO):** changes in pressure between the Indian Ocean and the south Pacific. The difference between pressure at **Tahiti** (French Polynesia, Pacific) and **Darwin** (northern Australia) is used to predict the strength of the monsoon. Together these are called **ENSO**.

**Onset and withdrawal:** The monsoon arrives on the southern tip (Kerala) by about **1 June**. It splits into two branches: the **Arabian Sea branch** and the **Bay of Bengal branch**. The Arabian Sea branch reaches Mumbai about ten days later, around **10 June**. Both branches merge over the north-western part of the Ganga plains. Delhi gets monsoon showers from the Bay of Bengal branch by the end of June (about 29 June). By mid-July the monsoon covers the whole country (about **8 July**).
Withdrawal begins in the north-western states by early **September**; by mid-October it withdraws from northern India completely, and from the Peninsula by early December.

**Breaks in the monsoon:** the monsoon has wet and dry spells; rain falls for a few days at a time, then dry spells (breaks) occur. These are linked with the movement of the monsoon trough. The monsoon is known for its **uncertainties**: it may cause floods in one place and drought in another; its onset and withdrawal may be irregular.

## The seasons
India has four main seasons:

| Season | Months | Key features |
|---|---|---|
| **Cold weather (winter)** | Mid-November to February | December and January coldest; north-east trade winds blow from land to sea, so most of India is dry; **Tamil Nadu coast gets rain** as these winds blow from sea to land there; north gets light winter rain from **western cyclonic disturbances**, locally called **mahawat**, very useful for **rabi crops**; snowfall in the Himalayas |
| **Hot weather (summer)** | March to May | Heat belt moves north; in May up to 45°C in the north-west; **loo**: strong, gusty, hot dry winds over north and north-west India; dust storms; **mango showers** (pre-monsoon rain in Kerala and Karnataka that help early ripening of mangoes); **Kaal Baisakhi** (calamity of the month of Baisakh): evening thunderstorms in West Bengal; **blossom showers** in Kerala (coffee flowers) |
| **Advancing monsoon (rainy season)** | June to September | South-west monsoon brings heavy rain; windward side of the Western Ghats gets over 250 cm; **Mawsynram** in the **Khasi hills** receives the highest average rainfall in the world; breaks in rainfall |
| **Retreating/post-monsoon** | October and November | Low-pressure trough weakens; skies clear; temperature rises; high temperature and humidity make weather oppressive: "**October heat**"; low-pressure conditions move to the Bay of Bengal, causing **cyclonic depressions** that strike the east coast (deltas of Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri); much rain in the Coromandel coast |

> [!MISTAKE] Tamil Nadu's rain
> Tamil Nadu gets most of its rainfall in **winter (October–December)** from the retreating monsoon and north-east winds, not in June–September, because it lies in the rain-shadow of the Arabian Sea branch.

## Distribution of rainfall
- **Heavy rainfall** (over 400 cm): western coast and north-east.
- **Less than 60 cm**: western Rajasthan and adjoining parts of Gujarat, Haryana and Punjab; also the interior of the Deccan plateau and east of the Sahyadris; Leh in Jammu and Kashmir.
- Rest of the country gets moderate rainfall. Snowfall is restricted to the Himalayan region.
- Rainfall is **highly variable** from year to year.

## The monsoon as a unifying bond
The Himalayas protect the subcontinent from cold winds; the Peninsular plateau, under the influence of the sea, has a moderate climate. The entire agricultural calendar, life of people and their festivals revolve around the monsoon. Year after year people wait eagerly for its arrival; the monsoon winds bind the whole country together by giving water for farming.

- Onset in Kerala about 1 June; whole country by mid-July; withdrawal from September.
- Seasons: winter, summer (loo, mango showers, Kaal Baisakhi), rainy, retreating (October heat).
- Mawsynram gets the world's highest average rainfall; western Rajasthan very little.

(5) Movement of the westerly jet stream north of the Himalayas and the tropical easterly jet stream over the Peninsula in summer. (6) El Niño and the Southern Oscillation: changes in pressure between the Pacific and Indian Oceans (Tahiti and Darwin) affect the strength of the monsoon.

W[3]: Why is the Indian monsoon considered a unifying bond?
A: (1) The Himalayas protect the whole subcontinent from cold Central Asian winds, giving it fairly uniform climatic conditions. (2) The seasonal alternation of wind systems and associated weather gives rhythm to the year for all regions. (3) The entire agricultural calendar, the life of people and festivals across India revolve around the monsoon. (4) From north to south and east to west, people eagerly await its arrival because it brings water for farming; thus it binds the whole country together.

=== natural-vegetation-and-wildlife ===
## The big idea
India is one of the **12 mega bio-diversity countries** of the world, with about **47,000 plant species** (10th in the world, 4th in Asia) and about **90,000 animal species**. **Natural vegetation** means plant communities that grow naturally without human help and have been left undisturbed for a long time; this is called **virgin vegetation**. Its type depends on climate, soil and relief.

> [!KEY] Key terms
> **Flora:** plants of a region or period. **Fauna:** species of animals. **Endemic species:** purely Indian species found only here. **Exotic species:** plants which came from outside India. **Ecosystem:** all plants and animals in an area, interdependent and connected with their physical environment. **Biome:** a very large ecosystem on land with distinct vegetation and animal life.

## Factors affecting vegetation
- **Relief:** **land** (fertile plains for agriculture, rugged terrain for grassland and forests) and **soil** (sandy desert soil supports cactus and thorny bushes; wet marshy deltaic soil supports mangroves; hill slopes with deep soil have conifers).
- **Climate:** **temperature** (falls with height, changing vegetation from tropical to sub-tropical, temperate and alpine), **photoperiod** (sunlight: trees grow faster in summer), and **precipitation** (heavy rainfall areas have dense forests).

## Wildlife
India has more than **90,000 animal species**, about **2,000 species of birds** (13% of the world total), **2,546 species of fish** (nearly 12% of the world stock), and about 5–8% of the world's amphibians, reptiles and mammals.
- **Elephants**: hot wet forests of Assam, Karnataka and Kerala.
- **One-horned rhinoceroses**: swampy and marshy lands of **Assam and West Bengal**.
- **Wild ass**: arid areas of the **Rann of Kachchh**; **camels** in the Thar Desert.
- **Indian bison, nilgai (blue bull), chousingha (four-horned antelope), gazel** and different species of deer.
- **India is the only country in the world that has both tigers and lions.** The natural habitat of the **Indian lion** is the **Gir forest in Gujarat**. **Tigers** are found in the forests of MP, the **Sundarbans** of West Bengal and the Himalayan region.
- Himalayan animals: yak, shaggy-horned wild ox, Tibetan antelope, bharal (blue sheep), wild sheep, kiang (Tibetan wild ass), ibex, bear, snow leopard, red panda.
- Birds: peacocks, pheasants, ducks, parakeets, cranes and pigeons.

**Threats:** hunting by greedy hunters for commercial purposes; pollution from chemicals and industrial waste; acid deposits; introduction of alien species; reckless cutting of forests for agriculture and habitation. About **1,300 plant species are endangered** and **20 species are extinct**.

## Conservation
**Why conserve?** Every species has a role in the ecosystem; conservation keeps the ecological balance, which supports life itself.

**Steps taken by the government:**
- **18 biosphere reserves** set up (for example, **Sunderbans, Nanda Devi, Gulf of Mannar, Nilgiris** and others such as Nokrek, Great Nicobar, Manas, Simlipal, Dihang-Dibang, Dibru Saikhowa, Agasthyamalai, Kanchenjunga, Pachmarhi, Achanakmar-Amarkantak). Several are included in UNESCO's World Network of Biosphere Reserves.
- Financial and technical help to many **botanical gardens** since 1992.
- **Project Tiger**, **Project Rhino**, **Project Great Indian Bustard** and many other eco-developmental projects.
- **National Parks, wildlife sanctuaries and zoological gardens**: there are about 100+ national parks and 500+ wildlife sanctuaries.
- **Wildlife Protection Act (1972)**.

Common people can help by planting trees (**Vanmahotsava**), not buying products of endangered animals and protecting habitats.

> [!MISTAKE] Evergreen does not mean no leaf fall
> Evergreen trees **do shed leaves**, but at different times, so the forest always looks green. Deciduous trees shed leaves **together** in the dry season.

=== population ===
## The big idea
People are the most important element of a country: they produce and consume resources, and they are themselves a resource. Geographers study population through **three questions**: how many people there are (**size**), where they live (**distribution**), and how the population changes (**growth and processes**). India's data come from the **Census**, held every **10 years**.

> [!KEY] The Census
> The **census** is an official enumeration of population done periodically. The first census in India was in **1872**; the first complete census was in **1881**. Since then it has been held every ten years. The figures below are from the **2011 Census**.

## Size and distribution
- On **1 March 2011**, India's population was **1,210.6 million** (about 1.21 billion), about **17.5%** of the world's population, while India covers only **2.4%** of the world's area.
- **Uttar Pradesh** is the most populous state (about **199 million**, about 16% of India's population). Sikkim has a very small population; **Lakshadweep** has only about 64,429 people.
- Almost **half** of India's population lives in just **five states**: Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, Bihar, West Bengal and Andhra Pradesh. **Rajasthan**, the biggest state by area, has only about 5.5% of the population.

**Population density** is the number of persons per unit area (per sq km). India's density in 2011 was **382 persons per sq km**.
- Highest: **Bihar (1,102)**; lowest: **Arunachal Pradesh (17)**.
- **Low density** (below 250 persons per sq km): rugged terrain and unfavourable climate keep population sparse, e.g. in Arunachal Pradesh and other hilly areas.
- **Moderate density**: Assam and most Peninsular states, with hilly, dissected terrain and moderate rainfall.
- **High density**: the Northern plains and **Kerala** in the south have high to very high densities because of flat plains with fertile soils and abundant rainfall.

> [!MISTAKE] Biggest area is not biggest population
> Rajasthan is the largest state by area, but Uttar Pradesh has the largest population. Density compares people to area.

## Population growth
**Population growth** is the change in the number of inhabitants of a country or territory during a specific period.
- **Absolute increase** = later population minus earlier population.
- **Rate of growth** (annual growth rate) is studied in per cent per annum, e.g. 2% per annum means 2 persons added per 100 people per year.

**Three phases:**
1. **1901–1951:** slow growth; population rose from 238 million to 361 million. Both birth and death rates were high.
2. **1951–1981:** **population explosion**: the rate of growth was rising; death rate fell rapidly while birth rate remained high. Population rose to 683 million by 1981.
3. **Since 1981:** the rate of growth has started **declining gradually** because birth rates fell. Yet, as the base is huge, the absolute numbers added each year are still enormous (about 182 million were added between 2001 and 2011).

> [!EXAMPLE] Why do absolute numbers keep rising?
> Even if the growth rate falls from 2% to 1.6%, 1.6% of 1,200 million (19.2 million) is still more than 2% of 600 million (12 million). A large base means big additions every year.

## Processes of change
Three main processes change population: **birth rates, death rates and migration**.
- **Birth rate:** number of live births per **thousand** persons in a year. Main cause of growth because India's birth rates have always been high.
- **Death rate:** number of deaths per thousand persons in a year. The rapid **fall in death rate** has been the main cause of rapid growth since 1951 (better health, sanitation, control of epidemics, better food supply).
- **Natural growth rate** = birth rate minus death rate.
- **Migration:** movement of people across regions and territories. It can be **internal** (within the country) or **international**. Internal migration does not change the size of the population, but it changes its **distribution**. In India most migration is **rural to urban** because of **push factors** (poverty and unemployment in villages) and **pull factors** (jobs and better living conditions in cities). Urban population increased from 17.29% in 1951 to **31.80% in 2011**. The number of "million plus cities" rose to **53** in 2011.

## Population composition
**Age composition:** people are grouped as
- **Children** (below 15 years): economically unproductive, need food, clothing, education and health care.
- **Working age** (15–59 years): economically productive and biologically reproductive.
- **Aged** (above 59 years): may be economically productive but are mostly retired.
A high proportion of children and aged means a high **dependency** on the working population.

**Sex ratio:** the number of **females per 1,000 males**. India's sex ratio in 2011 was **940** (it has always been unfavourable to females). **Kerala** has the highest sex ratio (**1,084**), and Puducherry 1,038. Delhi has 866 and Haryana only 877.

**Literacy:** a person aged **7 years and above** who can read and write with understanding in any language is considered literate. Literacy rate in 2011: **74.04%**; males **82.14%**, females **65.46%**.

**Occupational structure:** activities are grouped as **primary** (agriculture, animal husbandry, forestry, fishing, mining), **secondary** (manufacturing, building and construction) and **tertiary** (transport, communication, commerce, administration, services). In developed countries a high proportion work in secondary and tertiary sectors; in India about 64% still work in agriculture.

**Health:** life expectancy rose from **36.7 years in 1951 to 67.9 years in 2011**; death rate fell from 25 to about 7.2 per thousand.

**Adolescent population:** people aged **10 to 19 years**, about one-fifth of India's population. They need good nutrition, especially adolescent girls; many suffer from anaemia.

## National Population Policy
India began a comprehensive **Family Planning Programme in 1952**. The **National Population Policy (NPP) 2000** provides a policy framework for:
- free and compulsory school education up to 14 years of age;
- reducing infant mortality rate to below **30 per 1,000 live births**;
- universal immunisation of children against all vaccine-preventable diseases;
- promoting delayed marriage for girls;
- making family welfare a people-centred programme.

**NPP 2000 and adolescents:** it identified adolescents as a group needing attention: protection from unwanted pregnancies and sexually transmitted diseases, encouraging delayed marriage and child-bearing, education about risky sexual behaviour, providing food supplements and nutritional services, and strengthening legal measures to prevent child marriage.

> [!TIP] Board favourite
> "What are the three processes of population change?" and "Explain the significance of the age composition of a population" are common. Always define birth rate and death rate "per thousand persons in a year".

## Quick recap
- 2011: 1,210.6 million people, 17.5% of the world's population on 2.4% of the area.
- Density 382 per sq km; Bihar highest (1,102), Arunachal lowest (17).
- Growth: slow (1901–51), explosion (1951–81), declining rate (after 1981).
- Processes of change: birth rate, death rate, migration (rural to urban).
- Sex ratio 940; Kerala highest. Literacy 74.04%. NPP 2000 targets health, education and adolescents.

=== what-is-democracy-why-democracy ===
## The big idea
Democracy is the most common form of government today, but not every country that calls itself a democracy really is one. This chapter builds a clear **definition of democracy** step by step, using real examples, and then weighs the **arguments for and against** it.

> [!KEY] Simple definition
> **Democracy is a form of government in which the rulers are elected by the people.** The word comes from the Greek *demokratia*: *demos* (people) and *kratia* (rule). Abraham Lincoln described it as government "of the people, by the people, for the people".

## Features of democracy
The simple definition is not enough: we must separate democracies from non-democracies that hold sham elections. Four features complete the definition.

**1. Major decisions by elected leaders**
In **Pakistan**, General **Pervez Musharraf** led a military coup in **October 1999**, declared himself "Chief Executive" and later President by a **referendum** (2002). He changed the constitution through a "Legal Framework Order"; elected representatives had some powers but the final power rested with military officers and Musharraf.
> Lesson: in a democracy, the final decision-making power must rest with those **elected by the people**.

**2. Free and fair electoral competition**
In **China**, elections are held regularly for the National People's Congress, but candidates need the approval of the **Chinese Communist Party**; the government is always formed by the Communist Party. In **Mexico**, elections for President have been held every six years since **1930** and the country was never under military rule, yet until **2000** every election was won by the **PRI (Institutional Revolutionary Party)**, using many dirty tricks.
> Lesson: elections must offer a **real choice** between political alternatives, and those in power should be able to lose.

**3. One person, one vote, one value**
Democracy is based on **political equality**. Yet in **Saudi Arabia women did not have the right to vote until 2015**; in **Estonia**, citizenship rules made it difficult for people of the Russian minority to vote; in **Fiji** the electoral system gave more value to the vote of an indigenous Fijian than to an Indian-Fijian.
> Lesson: each adult citizen must have **one vote** and each vote must have **equal value**.

**4. Rule of law and respect for rights**
**Zimbabwe** became independent in **1980** (from white minority rule). Since then it was ruled by **ZANU-PF**, led by **Robert Mugabe**. Elections were held but Mugabe used unfair practices; opposition workers were harassed, public protests declared illegal, critics punished, the constitution amended to increase the President's powers, and court judgments ignored. He was removed in 2017.
> Lesson: a democratic government rules **within limits set by constitutional law and citizens' rights**. Popular approval alone does not make a government democratic.

> [!REMEMBER] The full definition
> Democracy is a form of government in which: (a) rulers elected by the people take all major decisions; (b) elections offer a free and fair choice and opportunity to change rulers; (c) this choice and opportunity is available to all the people on an equal basis; and (d) the exercise of this choice leads to a government limited by basic rules of the constitution and citizens' rights.

## Arguments against democracy
- Leaders keep changing, which leads to **instability**.
- Democracy is all about political **competition and power play**; there is no scope for morality.
- So many people have to be consulted that it leads to **delays**.
- Elected leaders do not know the best interest of the people, leading to **bad decisions**.
- It leads to **corruption** because it is based on electoral competition.
- Ordinary people don't know what is good for them; they should not decide anything.

## Arguments for democracy
Take the example of the **famine in China (1958–1961)**: about **three crore** people died, the worst famine in world history. India's economic condition then was not much better, but India did not have such a famine, because a democratic government responds to people's needs, and a free press and opposition would have raised the alarm.

1. A democratic government is a **better government** because it is more **accountable** and responsive to the needs of the people.
2. Democracy **improves the quality of decision-making**: it is based on consultation and discussion; many people put their minds together.
3. Democracy provides a method to **deal with differences and conflicts** peacefully, important in a diverse country like India.
4. Democracy **enhances the dignity of citizens**: it is based on political equality; the poorest and the richest have the same status.
5. Democracy **allows us to correct our own mistakes**: there is space for public discussion, and the rulers can be changed.

> [!KEY] Broader meanings of democracy
> Most common form is **representative democracy**: the majority rule through their elected representatives, because modern democracies have too many people to sit together and decide, and citizens lack time and knowledge to decide everything. Democracy can also mean democratic values in families, schools and organisations. **No country is a perfect democracy**; it is an ideal we keep working towards.

> [!MISTAKE] Elections alone do not make a democracy
> China, Pakistan under Musharraf and Zimbabwe all held elections. Elections must be free, fair, give equal votes and lead to a government that respects the rule of law and rights.

## Quick recap
- Democracy: rulers elected by the people, plus four conditions.
- Examples: Pakistan (elected leaders), China and Mexico (free and fair competition), Saudi Arabia, Estonia, Fiji (one person one vote), Zimbabwe (rule of law).
- Arguments for: accountability, better decisions, handling conflict, dignity, correcting mistakes.
- Arguments against: instability, delays, corruption, bad decisions.
- Chinese famine (1958–61) shows why accountable government matters.

=== constitutional-design ===
## The big idea
A **constitution** is a written set of rules that are accepted by all people living together in a country. It is the supreme law that decides the relationship between the people and the government. This chapter looks at how South Africa and India wrote their constitutions after long struggles, and at the values that guide India's Constitution.

> [!KEY] What a constitution does
> (1) Generates a degree of **trust and coordination** among different groups. (2) Specifies **how the government will be constituted** and who has power to take which decisions. (3) Lays down **limits on the powers** of government and states the rights of citizens. (4) Expresses the **aspirations** of the people about creating a good society.

## Democratic constitution in South Africa
**Apartheid** was the name of a system of racial discrimination unique to South Africa, imposed by white Europeans. The native people (**blacks**, about three-fourths of the population), people of mixed races (**coloured**) and people of **Indian** origin were discriminated against. Non-whites had no voting rights; they had separate trains, buses, hospitals, schools, beaches, even churches and cemeteries.

**Struggle:** From 1950, blacks, coloured and Indians fought against apartheid, led by the **African National Congress (ANC)**. **Nelson Mandela** and seven others were sentenced to **life imprisonment in 1964** for daring to oppose apartheid; he spent **28 years** in South Africa's most dreaded prison, **Robben Island**.

**Towards a new constitution:** Protests and international pressure made the white government change its policies. Discriminatory laws were repealed, the ban on political parties lifted and Mandela freed in **February 1990**. At midnight of **26 April 1994** the new national flag was unfurled, and South Africa became a democracy. Mandela became the first President. The black leaders appealed for forgiveness; the whites agreed to majority rule and one person one vote, and the black majority agreed that whites' property would not be taken away. After two years of discussion, the country got a constitution that gave its citizens the most extensive rights available in any country. Its Preamble promises to heal past divisions.

## Making of the Indian Constitution
**Challenges faced:**
- India was born through the **Partition** on the basis of religious differences; at least ten lakh people were killed in violence.
- The British had left it to the rulers of **princely states** to decide whether to merge with India, Pakistan or remain independent. Merger was difficult.
- The future was uncertain: India was a huge and diverse country, and poverty was widespread.

**The path to the Constitution:**
- In **1928**, **Motilal Nehru** and eight other Congress leaders drafted a constitution (Nehru Report).
- In **1931**, the **Karachi session** of the Indian National Congress passed a resolution on how independent India's constitution should look: universal adult franchise, right to freedom and equality, protecting minority rights.
- The **Government of India Act, 1935** and experience of working legislatures from 1937 helped.
- Ideals of the **French Revolution**, parliamentary democracy in Britain, the **Bill of Rights in the US**, and the **socialist revolution in Russia** inspired leaders.

**The Constituent Assembly:** the body of elected representatives that wrote the Constitution. Elections were held in **July 1946**; its first meeting was in **December 1946**. After Partition, it had **299 members**. **Dr. Rajendra Prasad** was its President and **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** was Chairman of the **Drafting Committee**. The Assembly **adopted the Constitution on 26 November 1949** and it came into effect on **26 January 1950** (we celebrate Republic Day). It met for **114 days** spread over **three years**, and every document of its proceedings is recorded in the **Constituent Assembly Debates**.

Why do we accept a constitution made so long ago? (1) It expresses a broad consensus of its time and has been accepted by people of all parties. (2) The Assembly represented the people of India; it had members from all regions, languages, castes, classes, religions and occupations. (3) It worked in a systematic, open and consensual manner.

## Guiding values of the Indian Constitution
The values that inspired the freedom struggle are written in the **Preamble**. The Preamble draws inspiration from the American model.
- **Mahatma Gandhi** in *Young India* (1931) dreamt of an India where the poorest feel it is their country, with no high and low classes, no untouchability, and equal rights for women.
- **Dr. Ambedkar** spoke of the contradiction of entering "a life of contradictions" on 26 January 1950: political equality but social and economic inequality.
- **Jawaharlal Nehru**'s speech "**Tryst with Destiny**" (14–15 August 1947, midnight) spoke of the service of millions who suffer, and wiping every tear from every eye.

**Key words of the Preamble:**
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **We, the people of India** | The Constitution is drawn up by the people through their representatives |
| **Sovereign** | People have the supreme right to make decisions on internal and external matters; no external power can dictate |
| **Socialist** | Wealth is generated socially and should be shared equally; government should reduce inequalities |
| **Secular** | Citizens have complete freedom to follow any religion; no official religion; government treats all religions equally |
| **Democratic** | People enjoy equal political rights, elect their rulers and hold them accountable |
| **Republic** | The head of the state is an **elected** person, not a hereditary position |
| **Justice** | Citizens cannot be discriminated against on grounds of caste, religion and gender; social, economic and political |
| **Liberty** | No unreasonable restrictions on what citizens think, how they express and act |
| **Equality** | All are equal before law; equal opportunities |
| **Fraternity** | All of us should behave as if we are members of the same family |

**Institutional design:** the Constitution lays down procedures for choosing rulers, defines who has how much power, puts limits on government and states citizens' rights. It is a long and detailed document, so it needs to be **amended** regularly; changes are called **constitutional amendments**. It has about 395 articles in its original form.

> [!MISTAKE] Adopted vs came into force
> The Constitution was **adopted on 26 November 1949** (now observed as Constitution Day) and **came into force on 26 January 1950** (Republic Day).

=== electoral-politics ===
## The big idea
In a large democracy, all citizens cannot sit together and make every decision. So they choose **representatives** through **elections**. This chapter explains why elections are needed, how India's election system works, the role of the **Election Commission**, and how we can judge whether elections are **free and fair**.

## Why elections?
An **election** is a mechanism by which people choose their representatives at regular intervals and change them if they wish. In an election voters can:
- choose who will make laws for them;
- choose who will form the government and take major decisions;
- choose the party whose policies will guide the government and law-making.

**Example: Haryana, 1987.** **Chaudhary Devi Lal**, leader of the **Lok Dal**, led the "**Nyaya Yudh**" (struggle for justice) and promised to waive the loans of farmers and small businessmen. His party and its partners won **76 out of 90 seats**. He became Chief Minister and waived loans of up to Rs 10,000. In 1991, the party lost popularity and lost the election. This shows how elections let people reward or punish rulers.

**What makes an election democratic?**
1. Everyone should be able to choose: **one person, one vote, every vote of equal value**.
2. There should be something to choose from: parties and candidates should be free to contest, offering real choice.
3. The choice should be offered at **regular intervals** (every few years).
4. The candidate preferred by the people should get elected.
5. Elections should be conducted in a **free and fair** manner.

**Is political competition good?** Demerits: it creates disunity and **factionalism**; parties and leaders level allegations against each other; it encourages dirty tricks; long-term policies are ignored. Merits: regular electoral competition gives incentives to parties and leaders to serve people; they know they will be judged by voters. Our Constitution makers chose competition because it works like an incentive, just as businesses compete for customers.

## Election system in India
- **Lok Sabha** and **Vidhan Sabha** (Assembly) elections are held every **five years**. When elections are held in all constituencies at the same time, it is a **general election**. When held for one constituency to fill a vacancy (death or resignation of a member), it is a **by-election**.

**Electoral constituencies:** the country is divided into areas for elections, called **constituencies**. Voters in one area elect one representative.
- **Lok Sabha**: India is divided into **543** constituencies; the representative is a **Member of Parliament (MP)**.
- Each state is divided into **Assembly constituencies**; the representative is a **Member of Legislative Assembly (MLA)**.
- Each Parliamentary constituency has several Assembly constituencies within it. Panchayats and municipalities also have wards.
- Constituencies should have roughly equal population, so every vote has equal value.

**Reserved constituencies:** some constituencies are reserved for **Scheduled Castes (SC)** and **Scheduled Tribes (ST)**: only someone belonging to these groups can stand there, but all voters vote. In the Lok Sabha, **84 seats** are reserved for SC and **47** for ST (as of 2019). In many states, one-third of seats in rural and urban local bodies are reserved for **women**; similar reservation exists for OBCs in some states.

**Voters' list (electoral roll):** a list of those eligible to vote, prepared well before the election. All citizens aged **18 years and above** can vote, irrespective of caste, religion or gender (**universal adult franchise**). Some criminals and persons of unsound mind can be denied the vote in rare cases. The list is revised regularly. The government has introduced the **Election Photo Identity Card (EPIC)**; voters may also show other identity proofs such as ration card or driving licence.

**Nomination of candidates:** anyone who can be a voter can also contest if they are **25 years** or older (for Lok Sabha and Vidhan Sabha). Parties nominate candidates, who get the party's **symbol** and support; this nomination is called a party **"ticket"**. Every candidate must fill a nomination form, pay a **security deposit**, and give a legal declaration with full details of:
- serious **criminal cases** pending against them;
- details of **assets and liabilities** of the candidate and family;
- **educational qualifications**.
This information is made public. There is no educational qualification required to contest, because it would deny the right to contest to many citizens and is not relevant to representing people.

**Election campaign:** usually lasts about **two weeks** between the announcement of the final list of candidates and the date of polling. Famous slogans: **"Garibi Hatao"** (Remove poverty) by the Congress under **Indira Gandhi** in **1971**; **"Save Democracy"** by the **Janata Party** in **1977** (after the Emergency); "**Land to the tiller**" by the Left Front in West Bengal (1977); "**Protect the self-respect of the Telugus**" by **N.T. Rama Rao** of the **Telugu Desam Party** in Andhra Pradesh (1983).

**Code of Conduct:** According to election law, no party or candidate can bribe or threaten voters, appeal on the basis of caste or religion, use government resources for campaigning, or spend more than **Rs 25 lakh** in a Lok Sabha constituency or **Rs 10 lakh** in an Assembly constituency (limits in the textbook; revised since). Under the **Model Code of Conduct**, no party can use places of worship for propaganda, or use government vehicles, aircraft or officials; once elections are announced, ministers cannot lay foundation stones, take big policy decisions or make promises of public facilities.

**Polling and counting:** voters go to the **polling booth**, their identity is checked, a mark is put on their finger with **indelible ink**, and they cast their vote. Earlier ballot papers were used; now **Electronic Voting Machines (EVMs)** are used. After polling, EVMs are sealed and kept safe; votes are counted, and the candidate with the **highest votes** in a constituency is declared elected (first-past-the-post).

## The Election Commission
India's elections are conducted by an **independent and very powerful Election Commission (EC)**. It enjoys the same independence as the judiciary. The **Chief Election Commissioner (CEC)** is appointed by the **President of India**, but once appointed is **not answerable** to the President or the government; it is very difficult to remove the CEC.

**Powers and functions:**
- Takes decisions on every aspect of conduct and control of elections, from the announcement to the declaration of results.
- Implements the **Code of Conduct** and punishes any candidate or party that violates it.
- During elections, can order the government to follow guidelines, to prevent misuse of government power, or transfer government officials.
- Government officers on election duty work under the **control of the EC**, not the government.
- Can order a **re-poll** if polling was unfair in some booths or constituencies.

## Free and fair elections
**Popular participation:** in India, the **turnout** (percentage of eligible voters who actually vote) has been stable or rising, unlike in many Western democracies. In India the poor, illiterate and underprivileged vote in larger proportion than the rich. Common people attach great importance to elections and feel they can influence the government. Interest in election-related activities has been increasing.

**Acceptance of results:** ruling parties routinely lose elections in India at national and state level (about half the time). Candidates who spend a lot of money or have criminal links often lose. Losing parties accept the people's verdict, barring some disputed cases.

**Challenges to free and fair elections:**
- Candidates and parties with a lot of **money** may not be sure of victory but enjoy an unfair advantage.
- Candidates with **criminal connections** have been able to push others out.
- Some families tend to dominate parties; tickets go to relatives.
- Very often elections offer little choice to ordinary citizens, as major parties have similar policies.
- Smaller parties and independent candidates suffer a huge disadvantage compared to bigger parties.

> [!MISTAKE] Reserved seats do not mean reserved voters
> In a constituency reserved for SC, only an SC person can **contest**, but **all** registered voters of that area vote.

=== working-of-institutions ===
## The big idea
In a democracy, decisions are not taken by one person. They pass through **institutions**: the **legislature** (Parliament) makes laws, the **executive** (President, Prime Minister, ministers, civil servants) implements them, and the **judiciary** settles disputes. This chapter follows how a major decision is taken and how these institutions work and check each other.

## How is a major policy decision taken?
**Example: the Mandal Commission and Office Memorandum of 1990.** The Government of India set up the **Second Backward Classes Commission** in **1979**, headed by **B.P. Mandal** (hence the **Mandal Commission**). It submitted its report in **1980**, recommending **27% reservation** in government jobs for the **socially and educationally backward classes (SEBC)**. In **1990**, the **Janata Dal** government under Prime Minister **V.P. Singh** decided to implement it. On **13 August 1990**, an **Office Memorandum (O.M.)** was issued, signed by a Joint Secretary of the Department of Personnel and Training.

The decision followed: President's address to Parliament (announcing intention), Union Cabinet's formal decision (6 August 1990), PM's statement in Parliament, then the O.M. There were protests and counter-protests; cases were filed in courts. In the **Indira Sawhney and others vs Union of India** case (**1992**), the **Supreme Court** upheld the order but asked the government to modify it so that well-to-do persons among the backward classes (the "**creamy layer**") were excluded from the benefit.

## Parliament
**Parliament** is the national assembly of elected representatives. In India it consists of the **President** and two Houses: the **Lok Sabha** (House of the People) and the **Rajya Sabha** (Council of States).

**Why do we need Parliament?**
1. It is the **final authority for making laws**.
2. It exercises **control over those who run the government**; in India the government can stay in office only as long as it enjoys the support of the Lok Sabha.
3. It **controls all the money** the government has: public money can be spent only when Parliament sanctions it.
4. It is the **highest forum of discussion and debate** on public issues and national policy.

**Lok Sabha vs Rajya Sabha**
| Lok Sabha | Rajya Sabha |
|---|---|
| Directly elected by the people | Elected indirectly by the elected members of State Legislative Assemblies (and some nominated) |
| Usually called the **lower house**, but it is more powerful | Called the **upper house** |
| 543 elected members | Up to 250 members (12 nominated by the President) |
| Term of **5 years** (can be dissolved earlier) | **Permanent house**; one-third members retire every 2 years; members have 6-year terms |
| **Money bills** can be introduced only here; Rajya Sabha can delay a money bill only by **14 days** | Can only suggest changes in money bills |
| Controls the Council of Ministers; can pass a **no-confidence motion** to remove the government | Cannot remove the government |
| In a joint sitting on an ordinary bill, it usually prevails because of larger numbers | Has special powers over state matters |

> [!MISTAKE] "Upper house" is not the more powerful one
> The Rajya Sabha is called the upper house, but the **Lok Sabha** exercises more real power: money bills, control over the Council of Ministers, and larger numbers in joint sittings.

## Political executive
The **executive** is the body of persons having authority to execute policies. It has two parts:
- **Political executive**: elected by the people for a specific period (e.g. ministers). They take the big decisions; they are answerable to the people.
- **Permanent executive (civil services)**: appointed on a long-term basis; they remain in office even when the ruling party changes. They are more educated and have expertise but work under the political executive.

**Prime Minister:** The President appoints the leader of the majority party or coalition that commands a majority in the Lok Sabha as PM. The PM has no fixed tenure; he or she continues as long as they remain leader of the majority. The PM chairs Cabinet meetings, coordinates departments, distributes and redistributes work to ministers, and can dismiss ministers. When the PM quits, the entire ministry quits.

**Council of Ministers** (60 to 80 ministers of different ranks):
- **Cabinet Ministers**: top-level leaders in charge of major ministries; the **Cabinet** (about 25 ministers) is the inner ring of the Council of Ministers.
- **Ministers of State with independent charge**: in charge of smaller ministries; attend Cabinet meetings only when invited.
- **Ministers of State**: attached to and assist Cabinet Ministers.
The Cabinet works as a team; ministers may have different views but every decision is owned by all (**collective responsibility**). In coalition governments, the PM cannot take decisions as freely and must accommodate partners.

**The President:** the **head of the State**, with only nominal powers, like the Queen/King of Britain. Elected indirectly by an electoral college of **elected MPs and elected MLAs**. All government activities take place in the President's name; all laws and major policy decisions are issued in his or her name; the President appoints the CJI, judges, governors, election commissioners and ambassadors; is the supreme commander of the defence forces. But the President exercises these powers on the **advice of the Council of Ministers**. The President can ask the Council to reconsider advice once, but must sign if it is sent back. A bill passed by Parliament becomes law only after the President's assent; the President can delay it and send it back once. The most important discretion: appointing the PM when no party has a clear majority.

> [!KEY] Parliamentary vs presidential system
> India has a **parliamentary system**: the executive is drawn from and answerable to the legislature, and the PM is the real head. In a **presidential system** (like the USA), the President is both head of state and head of government and is directly elected.

## The Judiciary
India has an **integrated judiciary**: the **Supreme Court** at the top controls judicial administration in the country; its decisions are binding on all other courts. Below it are **High Courts** in states, then **District Courts** and courts at local level.

**The Supreme Court can take up disputes:**
- between citizens of the country;
- between citizens and the government;
- between two or more state governments;
- between governments at the Union and state level.
It is the **highest court of appeal** in civil and criminal cases.

**Independence of the judiciary:** it is not under the control of the legislature or the executive. Judges are appointed by the President on the advice of the PM and in consultation with the Chief Justice; in practice senior judges select new judges. Judges can be removed only by an **impeachment** motion passed separately by **two-thirds** of members present in **both Houses** of Parliament; this has never happened to a Supreme Court judge.

**Powers:** the Supreme Court and High Courts are the guardians of the Constitution and Fundamental Rights. **Judicial review**: they can declare invalid any law of the legislature or action of the executive that is against the Constitution. They can also stop amendments that change the **basic structure** of the Constitution. **Public Interest Litigation (PIL)**: anyone can approach the courts if public interest is hurt by government actions.

## Quick recap
- Mandal Commission (1979; report 1980) recommended 27% reservation for SEBC; O.M. of 13 August 1990; Indira Sawhney case (1992) excluded the creamy layer.
- Parliament = President + Lok Sabha + Rajya Sabha; Lok Sabha is more powerful.
- Political executive (elected) vs permanent executive (civil servants).
- PM is the real head; President is the nominal head, acting on advice.
- Integrated, independent judiciary; judicial review and PIL protect rights.

W[5]: How is the Lok Sabha more powerful than the Rajya Sabha?
A: (1) Any ordinary law needs to be passed by both Houses, but if there is a difference, a joint sitting is held where the Lok Sabha's view usually prevails because it has more members. (2) The Lok Sabha exercises more powers in money matters: a money bill can be introduced only there; the Rajya Sabha can delay it by only 14 days or suggest changes, which the Lok Sabha may or may not accept. (3) Political executives change with elections; civil servants remain in office even when the ruling party changes. (4) Civil servants are usually more educated and have expert knowledge, but the political executive has more power because in a democracy the will of the people is supreme.

=== democratic-rights ===
## The big idea
Elections and institutions are not enough to make a democracy. Citizens must have **rights**: reasonable claims of persons, recognised by society and sanctioned by law. Rights protect us from the government and from each other, and the courts make sure they are respected.

## Life without rights
**1. Prison in Guantanamo Bay:** About **600 people** were secretly picked up by US forces from around the world and put in a prison in **Guantanamo Bay**, an area near **Cuba** controlled by the American Navy. The US government said they were enemies linked to the 9/11 attack in New York (2001). Their families were not informed; they were not produced before a magistrate; US courts could not hear their case. **Amnesty International**, an international human rights organisation, reported that prisoners were being tortured. The **UN Secretary General** said the prison should be closed. The US government refused.

**2. Citizens' rights in Saudi Arabia:** The country is ruled by a **hereditary king**; people have no role in electing or changing rulers. The king selects the legislature and executive and appoints judges. Citizens cannot form political parties; there is no freedom of religion for non-Muslims; women are subjected to many public restrictions (they got the vote in local elections only in 2015).

**3. Ethnic massacre in Kosovo:** Kosovo was a province of **Yugoslavia** with an ethnic **Albanian** majority. **Slobodan Milosevic**, a Serb nationalist leader, came to power through elections, and his government targeted Kosovo Albanians; thousands were massacred. In 1999 several countries intervened; Milosevic later lost power and was tried by an **International Court of Justice** for crimes against humanity.

> [!KEY] What are rights?
> A **right** is a claim of a person over other fellow beings, over society and over the government. Claims must be **reasonable**, **recognised by society** and **sanctioned by law**. Rights are needed in a democracy because: every citizen must be able to vote and contest; they protect minorities from oppression by the majority; they act as a guarantee when things go wrong, so the majority cannot take them away.

## Rights in the Indian Constitution
The Constitution lists **Fundamental Rights**, which are a basic feature of the Constitution. They are **six**:

**1. Right to Equality (Articles 14–18)**
- The government shall not deny any person **equality before the law** or **equal protection of the laws** (**rule of law**: no person is above the law).
- No discrimination on grounds of religion, caste, ethnicity, sex or place of birth in access to public places like shops, restaurants, hotels, wells and bathing ghats.
- **Equality of opportunity** in public employment; reservations for SC, ST and OBC are not a violation but a way to ensure real equality.
- **Untouchability** is abolished in any form and its practice is a punishable offence.
- Abolition of titles (except military and academic distinctions).

**2. Right to Freedom (Articles 19–22)**
Citizens have the freedom of: (a) **speech and expression**; (b) **assembly** in a peaceful manner; (c) **forming associations** and unions; (d) **moving freely** throughout the country; (e) **residing** in any part of the country; (f) practising any **profession** or carrying on any occupation, trade or business.
These are subject to reasonable restrictions (public order, peace, morality). Also: **no person can be deprived of life or personal liberty** except according to procedure established by law. A person arrested must be informed of the reasons, must be **produced before the nearest magistrate within 24 hours**, and has the right to consult a lawyer.

**3. Right against Exploitation (Articles 23–24)**
- Prohibits **traffic in human beings** (selling and buying of people, mostly women, for immoral purposes).
- Prohibits **forced labour or begar** (forcing a worker to work for little or no pay); also bonded labour.
- Prohibits **child labour**: no one can employ a child below **14 years** in any factory, mine or hazardous work like railways and ports.

**4. Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25–28)**
India is a **secular** state: there is no official religion and the government treats all religions with equal respect. Every person has the right to profess, practise and propagate the religion he or she believes in. But nobody can force another to convert. No special taxes to promote any religion. No religious instruction in government educational institutions.

**5. Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29–30)**
- Any section of citizens with a **distinct language or culture** has a right to conserve it.
- Admission to government or government-aided institutions cannot be denied on the grounds of religion or language.
- All **minorities**, religious or linguistic, can set up their own **educational institutions**.

**6. Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32)**
This is the right to **seek the enforcement** of the other Fundamental Rights. If any right is violated, a citizen can directly approach the **Supreme Court** or a **High Court**. **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** called it the "**heart and soul**" of our Constitution. Courts can issue directions, orders or **writs**. They can also award compensation to victims. Through **Public Interest Litigation (PIL)**, any person or group can approach the courts against violation of rights of others, even through a simple letter or postcard.

**Other bodies:** the **National Human Rights Commission (NHRC)**, set up by law in **1993**, is an independent body that investigates violations of human rights (it cannot punish, but presents findings and recommends action). Its members include retired judges and officers.

> [!MISTAKE] There are six, not seven, Fundamental Rights
> The **Right to Property** was originally a Fundamental Right but was removed in 1978 (44th Amendment). It is now only a **legal/constitutional right** (Article 300A).

## Expanding scope of rights
- Courts have expanded the meaning of rights: the **right to life** now includes the right to **food**, **health**, a decent environment and dignity.
- The **Right to Education** became a Fundamental Right: free and compulsory education for children aged **6 to 14 years**.
- **Right to Information (RTI) Act, 2005**: citizens can seek information from government offices; this is part of freedom of thought and expression.
- Constitutional rights outside Part III: **right to property** (not a fundamental right but a legal one) and the **right to vote** in elections (a constitutional right).
- The **South African Constitution** gives rights such as the right to **privacy**, adequate **housing**, health care, sufficient food and water. Many people now demand that such claims also become rights.
- **Human rights**: universal moral claims that may not yet be recognised by law. The **International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights** recognises the right to work, safe and healthy working conditions, adequate standard of living, social security, health and education.

> [!TIP] Board favourite
> "Why is the Right to Constitutional Remedies called the heart and soul of the Constitution?" and "Explain any two Fundamental Rights" are asked very often. Always name the right and give its main provisions.

## Quick recap
- Life without rights: Guantanamo Bay, Saudi Arabia, Kosovo.
- Rights are reasonable claims recognised by society and sanctioned by law.
- Six Fundamental Rights: Equality, Freedom, against Exploitation, Freedom of Religion, Cultural and Educational, Constitutional Remedies.
- Article 32 (Constitutional Remedies) = "heart and soul" (Ambedkar); PIL widens access.
- NHRC (1993); scope of rights is expanding: education, information (RTI 2005), food.

=== the-story-of-village-palampur ===
## The big idea
**Palampur** is a hypothetical (imaginary) village used to explain the basic ideas of **production**. Like most Indian villages, its main activity is **farming**, but people also run small manufacturing units, shops, dairies and transport. Looking at Palampur helps us understand what is needed to produce goods and services.

## About Palampur
- Well connected with neighbouring villages and towns. **Raiganj**, a big village, is **3 km** away; an all-weather road connects it to **Shahpur**, the nearest small town.
- About **450 families**. About 80 families of **upper castes** own most of the land; their houses are large, made of brick with cement plastering. **SCs (dalits)** make up one-third of the population and live in one corner of the village in much smaller houses of mud and straw.
- Most houses have **electricity**, which powers tubewells and small businesses. There are **two primary schools and one high school**, a **primary health centre** run by the government and a **private dispensary**.

## Factors of production
Production of goods and services requires four things:
1. **Land**, and other natural resources such as water, forests and minerals.
2. **Labour**: people who do the work. Some activities need highly educated workers; others need workers who can do manual work.
3. **Physical capital**: the inputs required at every stage of production:
   - **Fixed capital**: tools, machines and buildings that can be used for production over **many years** (e.g. farmer's plough, generators, turbines, computers).
   - **Working capital**: raw materials and **money in hand**, which are **used up** in production (e.g. yarn for a weaver, clay for a potter, seeds and fertiliser for a farmer).
4. **Human capital**: the **knowledge and enterprise** to put together land, labour and physical capital and produce an output (entrepreneurship).

> [!MISTAKE] Fixed vs working capital
> A tractor is **fixed capital** (used for many years). The diesel it uses is **working capital** (used up in one round of production).

## Farming in Palampur
**Land is fixed.** Since **1960** there has been no expansion of land under cultivation in Palampur. All land is cultivated; there is no wasteland. So to grow more, farmers must produce more from the same land.

**Ways to increase production:**
- **Multiple cropping**: growing more than one crop on a piece of land during the year. This is the **most common way**. Palampur farmers grow three different crops: **jowar and bajra** in the **rainy season (kharif)**; **potato** between October and December; **wheat** in **winter (rabi)**. Part of the land grows **sugarcane**, harvested once a year.
- **Modern farming methods / higher yield**: **yield** is the crop produced on a given piece of land during a single season.

Well-developed irrigation makes multiple cropping possible. Electricity came early to Palampur; **electric-run tubewells** replaced the **Persian wheel** and irrigated much larger areas. By the mid-1970s the entire cultivated area of 200 hectares was irrigated. (Only about **40%** of India's cultivated area is irrigated.)

**Green Revolution:** in the late 1960s, the Green Revolution introduced Indian farmers to **HYV (high yielding variety) seeds** of wheat and rice. HYV seeds produce much more grain on a single plant but need plenty of water, **chemical fertilisers and pesticides**. Farmers of **Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh** were the first to try them. In Palampur, the yield of wheat rose from **1,300 kg per hectare** (traditional seeds) to **3,200 kg per hectare** with HYV seeds. Farmers also used tractors and threshers.

**Sustainability:** modern farming has overused natural resources. Chemical fertilisers dissolve in water and drain away, polluting groundwater and rivers, and kill bacteria and micro-organisms in the soil, reducing fertility. Continuous use of groundwater for tubewells has lowered the **water table** (e.g. in Punjab and western UP).

**Distribution of land:** in Palampur, about **150 families** (one-third of 450) are **landless**, mostly dalits. **240 families** cultivate small plots less than **2 hectares** in size. There are **60 families of medium and large farmers** who cultivate more than 2 hectares; a few own over 10 hectares. Land distribution is unequal in India too.

**Labour:** small farmers and their families cultivate their own fields. Medium and large farmers hire **farm labourers**, who come from landless families or families on small plots. Farm labourers are paid in cash or kind (crops), sometimes meals. **Dala** is a landless farm labourer in Palampur who works for **Rs 160** per day, below the government minimum wage of **Rs 300** per day for a farm labourer. There is heavy competition for work, so labourers accept low wages. **Ramkali**, Dala's neighbour, is also a landless labourer.

**Capital:** small farmers borrow money from big farmers, village moneylenders or traders who supply inputs, at very high rates of interest. **Savita**, a small farmer, borrowed **Rs 3,000** from **Tejpal Singh**, a large farmer, at **24% interest for four months** and promised to work on his field during harvest for **Rs 100 per day** (a low wage). Medium and large farmers have surplus from farming to arrange capital.

**Sale of surplus:** small farmers keep a small part of the wheat for the family and have little surplus; medium and large farmers sell surplus wheat in the **Raiganj market**. **Tejpal Singh** deposits earnings in a bank, lends money to small farmers and buys capital (another tractor, more land).

## Non-farm activities in Palampur
Only about **25%** of people working in Palampur are engaged in non-farm activities.
- **Dairy**: people feed buffaloes on grass and jowar/bajra. Milk is sold in Raiganj; two traders from Shahpur have set up collection-cum-chilling centres there.
- **Small-scale manufacturing**: done mostly at home or in fields with family labour, using simple methods; e.g. **Mishrilal** bought a mechanical **sugarcane crushing machine** run on electricity, makes **jaggery** (gur) and sells it to traders in Shahpur.
- **Shopkeepers**: traders buy goods from wholesale markets in cities (rice, wheat, sugar, tea, oil, biscuits, soap, toothpaste, batteries) and sell them in the village. **Kareem** opened a **computer class centre** in the village, employing two women from the village who were trained in Shahpur.
- **Transport**: rickshaw-wallahs, tongawallahs, jeep, tractor, truck drivers and bullock cart drivers carry people and goods. **Kishora**, a farm labourer, bought a buffalo with a loan from a bank under a government scheme and uses his **buffalo cart** to carry goods; he also sells milk.

> [!TIP] Board favourite
> "What are the factors of production? Explain with examples" and "Why is multiple cropping important?" are very common. Also "Modern farming methods require more inputs; explain" and the environmental effects of the Green Revolution.

## Quick recap
- Four factors of production: land, labour, physical capital (fixed and working), human capital.
- Land in Palampur is fixed since 1960; multiple cropping and HYV seeds raised output.
- Green Revolution: HYV seeds, irrigation, fertilisers; wheat yield 1,300 to 3,200 kg/ha; but soil and water damage.
- Land is unequally distributed; small farmers borrow at high interest (Savita); labourers get low wages (Dala).
- Non-farm activities: dairy, small manufacturing (Mishrilal), shops (Kareem), transport (Kishora).

=== people-as-resource ===
## The big idea
**People as resource** means looking at a country's working people in terms of their existing productive **skills and abilities**. A large population is not just a burden: when people are educated, trained and healthy, they become an asset, called **human capital**, that produces goods and services and drives economic growth.

> [!KEY] Human capital formation
> **Human capital** is the stock of skill and productive knowledge embodied in people. When the existing human resource is further developed by spending on **education, training and medical care**, it becomes human capital. This investment is called **human capital formation**, and it adds to the productive power of the country just like physical capital formation.

## Human capital
- Human capital is in one way **superior** to other resources like land and physical capital: human resource can make use of land and capital; land and capital cannot become useful on their own.
- Investment in human capital (through education, training, health care) yields a return, just like investment in physical capital. Educated and healthy people earn higher incomes and contribute more to society.
- **Japan** is an example: it has no rich natural resources, yet it is a developed country because it **invested in its people**, especially in education and health. These people made efficient use of other resources like land and capital, and developed technology.

**Sakal and Vilas** (a story from the textbook): Sakal went to school, did a course in **computer programming**, and got a job in a private firm; he later designed new software and earned well. Vilas, his neighbour, could not go to school because of his father's death and poverty; he was sick (arthritis) and sold fish like his mother, earning a meagre income. The difference was **investment in education and health**.

> [!EXAMPLE] A virtuous cycle
> Educated parents invest more in their children's education and health; such children become productive adults and invest in their own children. Uneducated, unhealthy parents may keep their children in a similar disadvantaged state, creating a vicious cycle.

## Economic activities by men and women
Activities that add value to the **national income** are called **economic activities**. They are of two kinds:
- **Market activities**: performed for pay or profit, i.e. production of goods or services, including government services.
- **Non-market activities**: production for **self-consumption**, e.g. consumption and processing of primary products and own-account production of fixed assets.

Activities are classified into three sectors:
| Sector | Activities |
|---|---|
| **Primary** | Agriculture, forestry, animal husbandry, fishing, poultry farming, mining, quarrying |
| **Secondary** | Manufacturing (industry) |
| **Tertiary (service)** | Trade, transport, communication, banking, education, health, tourism, services, insurance |

**Women's work:** due to historical and cultural reasons, there is a division of labour between men and women in the family. Women generally look after domestic chores, and men work in fields. Women are **not paid** for their household services; this work is not counted in the national income. Women in the organised sector are usually educated and get equal pay; most women in the unorganised sector are less educated, earn low wages, lack job security and legal protection.

## Quality of population: education and health
The quality of population depends on the **literacy rate**, **health** of a person indicated by **life expectancy**, and **skill formation** acquired by the people.

**Education:**
- Education opens new horizons, provides new aspirations and develops values of life; it contributes to the growth of society, national income, cultural richness and efficiency of governance.
- Steps: provision of **universal access, retention and quality** in elementary education, with focus on girls. **Sarva Shiksha Abhiyan** gives elementary education to all children aged **6–14** years by 2010 (a time-bound initiative of the central government with states, local government and community). **Bridge courses** and **back-to-school camps** to increase enrolment; the **mid-day meal scheme** to encourage attendance and retention and improve nutritional status.
- Literacy rate has increased from **18% in 1951 to 74% in 2011**; literacy is higher among males (about 82%) than females (about 65%), and higher in urban than rural areas. It varies from 94% in Kerala to about 62% in Bihar.
- Expansion of higher education: more **vocational streams**, **navodaya vidyalayas** in each district. The plan outlay on education increased substantially.

**Health:**
- The health of a person helps him realise potential and fight illness. An unhealthy worker becomes a liability for an organisation.
- India has built a vast health infrastructure: primary health centres, community health centres, hospitals and medical colleges. **Life expectancy** has increased to over **68 years in 2014**; **infant mortality rate (IMR)** (death of a child under one year of age per 1,000 live births) has fallen from 147 in 1951 to **37 in 2014**. **Birth rate** fell to 21.8 and **death rate** to 7.1 in 2014. Smallpox has been eradicated, and malaria, polio and other diseases are controlled.
- **Health care, family welfare, nutrition** are national policy priorities, aiming to improve access to health services, especially for underprivileged sections.

## Unemployment
**Unemployment** exists when people who are **willing to work at the going wages cannot find jobs**. The workforce includes people aged **15–59 years**; children below 15 and the aged above 60 are not considered unemployed.

**Types of unemployment:**
- **Seasonal unemployment** (rural): people are not able to find jobs during some months of the year; those dependent on agriculture face this because there are busy seasons (sowing, harvesting, weeding, threshing) and slack seasons.
- **Disguised unemployment** (rural): people **appear to be employed** but more people work on a job than needed. For example, a family of eight working on a small plot where only five are needed; if three are removed, production does not fall. Their **marginal productivity is zero**.
- **Educated unemployment** (urban): many youth with matriculation, graduation and post-graduation degrees cannot find jobs. There is a paradox: a surplus of manpower in certain categories coexists with a shortage of manpower in others (e.g. technical skills).

**Effects of unemployment:**
- It leads to **wastage of manpower resource**; people who are an asset turn into a liability.
- It increases economic overload: dependence of the unemployed on the working population increases; quality of life falls.
- Feeling of **hopelessness and despair** among youth; social unrest.
- It has a detrimental impact on the overall growth of the economy and reduces the quality of life of a family and society.

**Employment structure:** In India, the **primary sector** (agriculture) is the most labour-absorbing, but in recent years there has been a decline in dependence on agriculture partly due to disguised unemployment. Some surplus labour has moved to the secondary or tertiary sector. Unemployment rate in India is low statistically because people with low income and productivity are counted as employed: they work all year but their earnings are low.

> [!MISTAKE] Disguised does not mean jobless
> A person in disguised unemployment **does work** and appears employed, but contributes nothing extra to output. A seasonally unemployed person has **no work** for part of the year.

> [!TIP] Board favourite
> "Distinguish between disguised and seasonal unemployment", "How is human resource different from other resources?" and "Explain the role of education in human capital formation" are standard questions. Use the Sakal–Vilas story as an example.

## Quick recap
- Population becomes an asset when invested in through education, training and health: human capital formation.
- Japan developed by investing in people.
- Economic activities: market and non-market; primary, secondary, tertiary sectors. Women's household work is unpaid.
- Education: literacy 18% (1951) to 74% (2011); Sarva Shiksha Abhiyan, mid-day meal.
- Unemployment: seasonal and disguised (rural), educated (urban); it wastes resources and causes despair.

=== poverty-as-a-challenge ===
## The big idea
Poverty is one of the most difficult challenges independent India has faced. It means **hunger, lack of shelter, illiteracy, ill health, lack of clean water and sanitation, lack of a regular job**, and also a feeling of **helplessness**. This chapter explains how poverty is measured, who is poor, why poverty exists, and how India fights it.

## Poverty seen through case studies
The textbook describes people such as **Ram Saran** (a daily-wage labourer in a flour mill near Ranchi, Jharkhand), **Lakha Singh** (a landless labourer in a village near Meerut, UP), **Santosh** (a widow working as a domestic help in Delhi) and **Kailash** (a city construction worker). They lack regular work, enough food, education for their children, health care and decent housing. The poor are also treated badly at almost every place: in farms, factories, government offices, hospitals and schools.

**Social exclusion:** poverty must be seen as the poor having to live only in a poor surrounding with other poor people, **excluded** from enjoying social equality with better-off people. The caste system in India is a typical example, where people of certain castes are excluded from equal opportunities.

**Vulnerability:** **vulnerability to poverty** describes the greater probability of certain communities (members of a backward caste) or individuals (widows, physically handicapped) becoming, or remaining, poor in the coming years. It is determined by the options available to different communities for finding an alternative living, in times of natural disasters or job losses.

## Poverty line
A common method to measure poverty is based on **income or consumption levels**. A person is considered poor if his or her income or consumption level falls below a "minimum level" necessary to fulfil basic needs. This level is the **poverty line**.
- In India, the poverty line considers a **minimum level of food requirement**, clothing, footwear, fuel and light, educational and medical requirement. These physical quantities are multiplied by their prices in rupees.
- **Calorie requirement:** the accepted average calorie requirement is **2,400 calories per person per day in rural areas** and **2,100 calories in urban areas**. Rural people do more physical work, so they need more calories.
- On the basis of these calculations, for the year **2011–12**, the poverty line was fixed at **Rs 816 per person per month for rural areas** and **Rs 1,000 for urban areas**. A family of five in rural areas earning less than about Rs 4,080 per month is below the poverty line.
- The poverty line is estimated periodically (normally every five years) by conducting sample surveys, carried out by the **National Sample Survey Organisation (NSSO)**.
- For international comparison, the **World Bank** uses a uniform standard: minimum availability of the equivalent of **$1.90 per person per day** (2011 PPP).

> [!MISTAKE] Same poverty line everywhere? No
> The poverty line varies with **time and place**. Each country uses an imaginary line suited to its own development level and accepted minimum social norms. Rural and urban lines also differ.

## Poverty estimates
- The proportion of people below the poverty line fell from about **45% in 1993–94 to 37.2% in 2004–05**, and further to **21.9% in 2011–12**.
- The number of poor fell from **407 million in 2004–05 to 270 million in 2011–12**. If the trend continues, people below the poverty line may come down to less than 20% in the next few years.

**Vulnerable groups:** **Scheduled Castes** and **Scheduled Tribes** households are most vulnerable; among economic groups, **rural agricultural labour** households and **urban casual labour** households. Within a poor family, women, elderly people and **female infants** suffer more (intra-household inequality); they are often denied equal access to resources.

**Inter-state disparities:** poverty varies across states. **Madhya Pradesh, Assam, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar and Odisha** have poverty ratios above the all-India level; **Bihar and Odisha** remain the two poorest states (about 33.7% and 32.6% in 2011–12). States that reduced poverty significantly: **Kerala, Jammu and Kashmir, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Gujarat and West Bengal**. Reasons: **Punjab and Haryana** used high agricultural growth rates; **Kerala** focused on **human resource development**; **West Bengal** used **land reform** measures; **Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu** used public distribution of food grains.

**Global poverty:** the proportion of people in developing countries living in extreme poverty (below $1.90 a day) fell from 36% in 1990 to about 10% in 2015. Reduction has been large in **China and South-East Asia** due to rapid growth and investment in human resources. In **Sub-Saharan Africa**, poverty declined from 54% in 1990 to 41% in 2015. In Latin America it remained about the same. The **Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)** of the UN propose to end poverty of all types by **2030**.

## Causes of poverty
1. **Low level of economic development under British colonial rule**: policies ruined traditional handicrafts and discouraged textile industries.
2. **Low rate of growth** after independence, until the 1980s, accompanied by a **high growth rate of population**, so the growth rate of per capita income was low.
3. The Green Revolution created jobs, but limited to some parts of India; industries could not provide enough jobs. Unable to find proper jobs in cities, many became rickshaw pullers, vendors, construction workers and domestic servants, living in slums.
4. **Huge income inequalities**: **unequal distribution of land** and other resources. Land reforms were not implemented properly by most state governments.
5. **Socio-cultural factors**: to fulfil social obligations and religious ceremonies, the very poor spend a lot of money, often borrowing.
6. **Indebtedness**: small farmers need inputs; they borrow at high interest, and when crops fail they cannot repay and fall deeper into poverty.

## Anti-poverty measures
The government's strategy has two planks: (1) **promotion of economic growth**, and (2) **targeted anti-poverty programmes**.

**Economic growth:** since the 1980s India's growth rate has been one of the fastest in the world (about 6% in the 2000s). Growth widens opportunities and provides resources for investment in human development. But the poor may not benefit directly from growth, so targeted programmes are needed.

**Important programmes:**
| Programme | Year | Aim |
|---|---|---|
| **Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA)** | **2005** | **100 days** of assured wage employment every year to every rural household in 200 districts (later extended to all); one-third of jobs reserved for women; if not given work within 15 days, an unemployment allowance |
| **Prime Minister Rozgar Yojana (PMRY)** | 1993 | Self-employment opportunities for educated unemployed youth in rural areas and small towns |
| **Rural Employment Generation Programme (REGP)** | 1995 | Self-employment opportunities in rural areas and small towns; target of 25 lakh new jobs under the Tenth Five Year Plan |
| **Swarnajayanti Gram Swarozgar Yojana (SGSY)** | 1999 | Bring assisted poor families above the poverty line by organising them into **self-help groups** through bank credit and government subsidy |
| **Pradhan Mantri Gramodaya Yojana (PMGY)** | 2000 | Additional central assistance to states for basic services such as primary health, primary education, rural shelter, drinking water and electrification |
| **Antyodaya Anna Yojana (AAY)** | 2000 | Food grains at highly subsidised rates to the poorest of the poor (see Food Security) |
| **Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (PMJDY)** | 2014 | Bank accounts for all, with insurance |

The results have been **mixed**: one major reason is the lack of proper implementation and right targeting; there has been a lot of overlapping of schemes.

## The challenges ahead
Poverty has certainly declined, but removing it remains a major challenge. The concept of poverty is expanding: from "minimum subsistence level" to "**reasonable level of living**", including health, education, job security, self-confidence, avoiding discrimination, and equality of opportunity. **Wider access to healthcare, education, job security**, gender equality and dignity for the poor must be achieved.

=== food-security-in-india ===
## The big idea
Food is as essential for living as air is for breathing. **Food security** means that **all people at all times** have enough nutritious food. India has worked hard to become self-sufficient in food grains, and it runs one of the world's largest systems for getting food to the poor: the **buffer stock** and the **Public Distribution System (PDS)**.

## What is food security?
Food security has three dimensions:
1. **Availability** of food: food production within the country, food imports and the previous years' stock stored in government granaries.
2. **Accessibility**: food is within reach of every person.
3. **Affordability**: an individual has enough money to buy sufficient, safe and nutritious food to meet dietary needs.

So food security is ensured in a country only if (a) enough food is available for all, (b) all persons have the capacity to buy food of acceptable quality, and (c) there is no barrier to access food.

**Why is food security needed?** The poorest section may be food insecure most of the time, but even people above the poverty line may become food insecure during a **national disaster or calamity** like an earthquake, drought, flood, tsunami or widespread crop failure. Production falls, food becomes scarce, prices rise, and people cannot buy. If such a calamity lasts long, it may cause **famine**: widespread deaths due to starvation and epidemics caused by contaminated water or decaying food and weakened bodies. The most devastating famine in India was the **Bengal Famine of 1943**, which killed about **30 lakh people** in the province of Bengal. Rice production in Bengal fell, and prices rose sharply. Most victims were agricultural labourers, fishermen, transport workers and other casual labourers.

**Who are food insecure?**
- In **rural areas**: **landless people** with little or no land, **traditional artisans**, providers of traditional services, **petty self-employed workers** and **destitutes including beggars**.
- In **urban areas**: families whose working members are employed in **ill-paid occupations** and the **casual labour market**, mostly seasonal, earning very low wages.
- **SC, ST and some OBC** sections with poor land base; people affected by natural disasters who migrate for work.
- A large proportion of **pregnant and nursing mothers and children under five** suffer from malnutrition.
- **Regions**: economically backward states with high poverty, tribal and remote areas, and regions prone to natural disasters. Large numbers of food-insecure people are in **Uttar Pradesh** (eastern and south-eastern parts), **Bihar, Jharkhand, Odisha, West Bengal, Chhattisgarh**, parts of Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra.

> [!KEY] Hunger
> **Chronic hunger** is a consequence of diets persistently inadequate in quantity and/or quality; poor people suffer from it because of very low income. **Seasonal hunger** is related to cycles of food growing and harvesting; it is prevalent in rural areas because of the seasonal nature of agricultural activities, and in urban areas because of casual labour (e.g. less work in the rainy season for construction workers). The percentage of seasonal as well as chronic hunger has declined in India.

## Self-sufficiency in food grains
After independence, India adopted a new strategy in agriculture which resulted in the **Green Revolution**, especially in the production of **wheat and rice**. Indira Gandhi released a special stamp titled "**Wheat Revolution**" in July 1968. Wheat's success was later replicated in rice. **Punjab and Haryana** recorded the highest growth (food grain production rose from 7.23 million tonnes in 1964–65 to 30.33 million tonnes in 1995–96); **Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh** were significant in rice yield. Since the advent of the Green Revolution, India has avoided famine even during adverse weather conditions and has become **self-sufficient** in food grains.

## Buffer stock
**Buffer stock** is the stock of food grains, namely **wheat and rice**, procured by the government through the **Food Corporation of India (FCI)**.
- FCI purchases wheat and rice from farmers in states where there is **surplus production**.
- Farmers are paid a pre-announced price called the **Minimum Support Price (MSP)**. The MSP is declared every year **before the sowing season** to give farmers an incentive to raise production.
- The purchased grains are stored in **granaries**.
- The buffer stock is created to **distribute food grains in deficit areas** and among the poorer strata at a price lower than the market price, known as the **issue price**. It also helps resolve shortage during adverse weather or calamity.

## Public Distribution System (PDS)
The food procured by FCI is distributed among the poorer sections through government-regulated **ration shops** (also called **fair price shops**). This system is called the **Public Distribution System (PDS)**. Ration shops are present in most localities, villages, towns and cities; about **5.5 lakh** ration shops exist all over the country. They stock food grains, **sugar and kerosene** for cooking, sold at prices lower than market prices. Any family with a **ration card** can buy a stipulated amount (e.g. 35 kg of grains, 5 litres of kerosene, 5 kg of sugar) every month.

**Ration cards:** three kinds: **Antyodaya cards** for the poorest of the poor; **BPL cards** for those below the poverty line; **APL cards** for all others.

**History of rationing:** rationing was introduced in India in the **1940s** against the backdrop of the **Bengal famine**. It was revived in the 1960s during an acute food shortage, before the Green Revolution. In the wake of high poverty in the mid-1970s (NSSO reports), three important food intervention programmes were introduced: **PDS** for food grains (already existing, strengthened), **Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS)** (introduced in **1975** on an experimental basis), and **Food-for-Work (FFW)** (introduced in **1977–78**). Over the years, several new programmes have been launched; there are many poverty alleviation programmes (PAPs) with food components, e.g. **mid-day meals**.

**Revamped PDS (RPDS):** introduced in **1992** in **1,700 blocks** in the country, targeting remote and backward areas.
**Targeted PDS (TPDS):** introduced in **June 1997** to adopt the principle of **targeting the poor in all areas**. For the first time a **differential price policy** was adopted for poor and non-poor.
**Antyodaya Anna Yojana (AAY):** launched in **December 2000**. One crore of the poorest among BPL families covered under TPDS were identified. **25 kg** of food grains were made available to each eligible family at a highly subsidised rate of **Rs 2 per kg for wheat** and **Rs 3 per kg for rice**. The quantity was enhanced from 25 to **35 kg** from **April 2002**. The scheme was further expanded twice by additional 50 lakh BPL families in June 2003 and August 2004, covering **2 crore** families.
**Annapurna Scheme (APS)** (2000): for **indigent senior citizens**; 10 kg of food grains free of cost.
**National Food Security Act (NFSA), 2013:** provides food and nutritional security at affordable prices and enables people to live a life with dignity; **75% of the rural population** and **50% of the urban population** are categorised as eligible households.

**Problems with the PDS:**
- FCI granaries are **overflowing** with grain, some of it rotting or eaten by rats, while many people go hungry. High buffer stocks are undesirable as they involve huge **carrying costs** (maintenance) and waste.
- Increase in MSP has encouraged farmers (especially in surplus states) to divert land from coarse grains (the food of the poor) to rice and wheat. Intensive water use in rice cultivation has led to **environmental degradation** and fall in the water table.
- **Malpractices**: PDS dealers divert grains to the open market for better margins, sell poor-quality grain at ration shops, and open shops irregularly.
- Poor quality grain is often left unsold, leading to large stocks with FCI.
- With three different prices (APL, BPL, AAY), APL families get little discount, so they have little incentive to buy from ration shops.

## Role of cooperatives in food security
Cooperatives play an important role, especially in the **southern and western** parts of India.
- In **Tamil Nadu**, about **94%** of the ration shops are run by cooperatives.
- In **Delhi**, **Mother Dairy** provides milk and vegetables to consumers at controlled rates decided by the Delhi Government.
- **Amul** (Gujarat) is a success story of cooperatives in milk and milk products; it brought about the **White Revolution** in India.
- In **Maharashtra**, the **Academy of Development Science (ADS)** has facilitated a network of NGOs for setting up **grain banks** in different regions; it organises training and capacity building programmes on food security for NGOs. ADS grain banks are now being replicated in different parts of Maharashtra.

> [!MISTAKE] MSP vs issue price
> **MSP** is the price at which the government **buys** grain from farmers. **Issue price** is the lower price at which grain is **sold** to the poor through ration shops.
