=== the-fun-they-had ===
## The big idea
**Isaac Asimov's** science-fiction story is set in the year **2157**. Eleven-year-old **Margie** studies alone at home with a **mechanical teacher**. When her friend **Tommy** (13) finds a real printed book about the schools of "centuries ago", Margie starts dreaming about how much fun children must have had learning together. The story makes us value what we already have: human teachers, classmates and real books.

> [!KEY] Genre
> **Science fiction** imagines how science and technology might change life in the future. Asimov wrote this story in 1951, long before personal computers and online classes existed.

## Summary
1. On **17 May 2157**, Margie writes in her diary that Tommy found a **real book**. Its pages were yellow and crinkly, and the words stood still instead of moving on a screen. Their grandparents' grandparents had read such books.
2. Margie hates school. Her mechanical teacher kept giving her geography tests and she was doing worse and worse. Her mother sent for the **County Inspector**, a little red-faced man, who took the teacher apart and found that the **geography sector was geared a little too quick**. He slowed it to an average ten-year level.
3. Tommy says the old book is about **school**, a special building where a **man** was the teacher and all kids of the same age learned the same things together.
4. Margie is surprised that a man could know enough to teach. She cannot imagine a stranger in her house teaching her.
5. Her mother calls her to school. Margie goes to her schoolroom next to her bedroom. Today's lesson is **arithmetic, the addition of proper fractions**. She puts her homework into the **slot** (written in a punch code she learnt at age six).
6. While the machine drones on, Margie thinks about the old schools, where children laughed and shouted in the schoolyard and went home together. She thinks about **"the fun they had."**

## Characters
- **Margie**: curious, imaginative, lonely; dislikes the dull mechanical teacher and longs for company.
- **Tommy**: 13, a bit boastful and superior ("Because it's not our kind of school, stupid"), but knowledgeable.
- **The County Inspector**: cheerful and practical; he pats Margie's head and fixes the machine.
- **Margie's mother**: strict about regular school hours.

## Themes
- **Human contact in learning**: a machine can give information, but it cannot give friendship, warmth or fun.
- **Technology's limits**: the teacher works perfectly as a machine, yet Margie is unhappy.
- **Irony**: we often complain about school; Margie envies exactly what we have.

> [!REMEMBER] Key numbers and names
> Year 2157 · Margie 11, Tommy 13 · geography sector too quick · lesson on proper fractions · homework written in punch code.

> [!MISTAKE] Margie does not hate learning
> She hates the **mechanical** way of learning, alone and without friends. Do not write that she was lazy.

> [!TIP] Likely questions
> Compare Margie's school with your school (5 marks). What did the Inspector do? Why did Margie hate school? Write a diary entry as Margie. Use value points: isolation, no peer group, rigid timing, no fun, human teacher understands feelings.

## Quick recap
- Future school = machine teacher at home, alone, fixed hours.
- Old school = building, human teacher, classmates, fun.
- Message: technology cannot replace the human touch in education.

=== the-sound-of-music ===
## The big idea
This chapter has two parts about musicians who overcame obstacles. **Part I** (by Deborah Cowley) is about **Evelyn Glennie**, a Scottish percussionist who became profoundly deaf yet became a world-class musician. **Part II** is about **Ustad Bismillah Khan**, who made the **shehnai** a classical instrument and loved India deeply.

## Part I: Evelyn Glennie – listening without hearing
- Her hearing loss was noticed when she was about **eight**; by **eleven** she was **profoundly deaf**. A specialist advised hearing aids and a school for the deaf.
- At school, percussionist **Ron Forbes** noticed her potential. He tuned two large drums to different notes and asked her to feel the sound. She discovered she could sense **higher notes in the upper body and lower notes in the legs**.
- She auditioned for the **Royal Academy of Music, London**, was accepted and scored some of the highest marks in its history.
- She performs **barefoot** to feel vibrations through the floor, and plays about a thousand instruments. She also gives free concerts in hospitals and prisons and was named **Soloist of the Year (Royal Philharmonic Society, 1991)**.
- She inspires deaf children, showing that a disability need not stop you.

> [!KEY] Evelyn's message
> Hard work and determination can turn a disability into a different ability. "If you work hard and know where you are going, you'll get there."

## Part II: Bismillah Khan – the shehnai of India
- **Origin of the shehnai:** Emperor **Aurangzeb** banned the shrill **pungi** in the royal residence. A barber from a family of musicians improved it, using a hollow, longer, broader pipe with holes. Because it was first played in the **Shah's chamber** by a **nai** (barber), it was called the **shehnai**. It became an auspicious instrument at weddings and temples.
- Bismillah Khan was born on **21 March 1916** at **Dumraon, Bihar**. His grandfather **Rasool Bux Khan** played the shehnai for the king of Bhojpur.
- He learnt from his uncle **Ali Bux**, practised at the **Balaji and Mangala Maiya temples** and on the banks of the **Ganga** in Benaras, which inspired him to create new ragas.
- He joined **All India Radio, Lucknow, in 1938**.
- On **15 August 1947** he played from the **Red Fort**, the first Indian to greet independent India with his shehnai (Raag Kafi).
- He played in films (*Gunj Uthi Shehnai*) but disliked film-world artificiality. He was the first Indian invited to the **Lincoln Center Hall, USA**, and an auditorium in **Tehran** was named after him.
- He received the **Padma Shri, Padma Bhushan, Padma Vibhushan** and the **Bharat Ratna (2001)**.
- When a student asked him to settle in the USA, he refused: he could not take the **Ganga** and **Benaras/Dumraon** with him.

> [!MISTAKE] Who named the shehnai?
> It was not Bismillah Khan. The name came centuries earlier, from "shah" + "nai" in Aurangzeb's time.

## Themes and values
- **Determination** against odds (Evelyn's deafness, Bismillah's modest start).
- **Dedication and practice**.
- **Patriotism and secularism**: a Muslim musician who practised in temples and loved Benaras.
- **Humility and simplicity** despite fame.

> [!TIP] Likely questions
> How did Evelyn learn to "hear" music? How was the shehnai transformed? Why did Bismillah Khan refuse to settle in the USA? What qualities do the two musicians share? Value points: perseverance, passion, rootedness, inspiration to others.

## Quick recap
- Evelyn Glennie: deaf by 11, Ron Forbes, Royal Academy of Music, plays barefoot.
- Bismillah Khan: born 1916 Dumraon, AIR 1938, Red Fort 1947, Bharat Ratna 2001.
- Shehnai = played in the Shah's chamber by a nai.

Bismillah Khan rose from a family of shehnai players, practised daily in temples and by the Ganga, and took the shehnai to the classical stage and the world, winning the Bharat Ratna. Both stayed humble and gave back: Evelyn plays free concerts and inspires the disabled, while Bismillah Khan remained rooted in Benaras and stood for India's composite culture. Their lives teach perseverance, devotion and humility.

=== the-little-girl ===
## The big idea
In **Katherine Mansfield's** story, a little girl named **Kezia** is terrified of her stern father. One frightening night shows her that behind his strict exterior he has a loving heart. The story is about a **father–daughter relationship**, misunderstanding, and how children need affection as well as discipline.

## Summary
1. Kezia is afraid of her father. In the morning he comes to say goodbye before work; in the evening the house relaxes only after he has had his tea. When she talks to him she **stammers**, and he scolds her for it.
2. Her **grandmother** suggests she make a **pincushion** for his birthday. Looking for stuffing, Kezia tears up some papers from his bedside table, not knowing they are his **great speech for the Port Authority**.
3. Father is furious. He **beats her palms with a ruler**. Kezia sobs and asks her grandmother why God made fathers.
4. Next door, **Mr Macdonald** plays happily with his five children. Kezia sees that not all fathers are alike.
5. One night her mother is in **hospital** and Grandmother is away. Kezia has a **nightmare** about a butcher with a knife and rope. Her father comes, carries her to his bed and comforts her.
6. As she lies on his arm, she realises he is tired from working hard for the family. She thinks, "Poor father! Not so big, after all", and asks him to keep his heart warm for her.

## Characters
- **Kezia**: sensitive, timid, observant; her fear turns to understanding and love.
- **Father**: strict and hot-tempered on the surface, but caring; he works hard and has little time for play.
- **Grandmother**: affectionate and gentle; Kezia's refuge.
- **Mr Macdonald**: a contrast, a playful, cheerful father.

## Themes
- **Communication gap** between parents and children.
- **Appearances can deceive**: the father's harshness hid love.
- **Discipline versus affection**: children need both.
- **Growing up**: Kezia matures by seeing the world from her father's side.

> [!KEY] Turning point

> [!MISTAKE] The father was not cruel

## The big idea
This biographical piece traces the life of **Albert Einstein**: a slow-talking child who became the greatest physicist of the twentieth century, and a scientist who later worked tirelessly for **world peace**. "Beautiful mind" refers to both his genius and his humanity.

=== the-snake-and-the-mirror ===
## The big idea
**Vaikom Muhammad Basheer**'s humorous story (translated from Malayalam) is narrated by a young **homeopath** who is full of **vanity** about his looks. A sudden encounter with a snake shows him how small and helpless he really is. It is a gentle satire on human pride, told with self-mocking humour.

## Summary
1. The poor homeopath rents a small room with a tiled roof but no ceiling, where **rats** run about. He has no electricity and comes home at night with a lamp.
2. One hot night he hears a dull sound, thinks it is a rat, and sits in front of his **mirror** admiring his face. He decides to shave every day, grow a thin **moustache** and always keep a charming smile.
3. He even plans to marry a **fat lady doctor** who has plenty of money, so that she can support him.
4. Suddenly a **snake** drops from the rafters onto his shoulder and coils around his left arm, its hood near his face. He freezes "like a stone image" and prays silently to God.
5. The snake turns its attention to the **mirror** and seems to admire its own reflection. The narrator jokes that it was perhaps wondering whether to grow a moustache or use make-up too!
6. Seizing the chance, the doctor slips out of the room and runs out of the house. When people return with a snake-catcher, the snake is gone, and with it the narrator's dreams of vanity.

> [!KEY] Source of humour
> The humour comes from **irony and exaggeration**: a man dreaming of beauty and a rich wife suddenly becomes a trembling statue, and the snake becomes another vain creature admiring itself.

## Characters
- **The homeopath**: poor, vain, imaginative, but honest enough to laugh at himself. Fear wipes out his pride.
- **The snake**: almost a comic character, as vain as the man.

## Themes
- **Vanity and pride**: looks and money are unimportant before death.
- **Fear and presence of mind**: staying still saves him.
- **Humour in a crisis**.

> [!MISTAKE] The snake did not bite
> The snake never harmed him; it only coiled round his arm and then went to the mirror.

=== my-childhood ===
## The big idea
In this extract from his autobiography ***Wings of Fire***, **Dr A. P. J. Abdul Kalam** (former President of India and missile scientist) remembers growing up in **Rameswaram, Tamil Nadu**. He describes a simple, secure home and a society where Hindus and Muslims lived in harmony, and a few people who tried to divide them and were corrected.

## Family and home
- He was born into a middle-class **Tamil Muslim** family. His father **Jainulabdeen** was neither educated nor rich but was generous, wise and disciplined. His mother **Ashiamma** was kind; she fed more outsiders every day than family members.
- They lived in an **ancestral house** built in the mid-nineteenth century on Mosque Street. Kalam inherited **honesty and self-discipline** from his father and **faith in goodness and deep kindness** from his mother.

## The war and his first earnings
- When **World War II** broke out in **1939**, there was a sudden demand for **tamarind seeds**; Kalam collected and sold them to earn a little money.
- The train stopped halting at Rameswaram, so newspapers were thrown from the moving train. His cousin **Samsuddin** gave him the job of catching the bundles, and Kalam earned his **first wages**, which made him proud.

## Friends and secular upbringing
- His three close friends were **Ramanadha Sastry, Aravindan and Sivaprakasan**, all from orthodox Hindu families. They never felt any difference of religion.
- His mother and grandmother told him stories from the **Ramayana** and the life of the Prophet.

## Three incidents about prejudice
1. **The new teacher**: In class five, a new teacher could not stand a Muslim boy (wearing a cap) sitting with **Ramanadha Sastry**, son of the temple priest, and sent Kalam to the back bench. **Pakshi Lakshmana Sastry** (Ramanadha's father, high priest of the Rameswaram temple) summoned the teacher and told him to apologise or leave the island. The teacher regretted and reformed.
2. **The science teacher**: **Sivasubramania Iyer**, an orthodox Brahmin, invited Kalam home for a meal. His wife refused to serve a Muslim boy in her kitchen. Iyer **served Kalam himself** and invited him again the next week; this time his wife served Kalam inside the kitchen. Iyer said that when you want to change the system, you have to confront such problems.
3. **Leaving home**: Kalam wanted to study at **Ramanathapuram**, the district headquarters. His father gave permission, quoting **Khalil Gibran** that children are not their parents' possessions but have their own thoughts.

> [!KEY] Theme
> **Secularism and social harmony**: prejudice exists, but wise and courageous people can overcome it.

> [!MISTAKE] Who rebuked the new teacher?
> It was Pakshi Lakshmana Sastry (the priest), not Sivasubramania Iyer. Iyer is the science teacher who invited Kalam for a meal.

> [!TIP] Likely questions

=== the-road-not-taken ===
## The big idea
**Robert Frost**, the American poet, describes a traveller who stands where a road divides in a **yellow wood** and must choose one path. The two roads stand for **choices in life**. We cannot try every option, our choices shape our future, and we often look back on them with mixed feelings.

## Summary, stanza by stanza
1. In an autumn wood, two roads diverge. The traveller is sorry he cannot travel both, so he stands a long time looking down one as far as he can see, to where it bends into the undergrowth.
2. He takes the other road, which seems to have a better claim because it is **grassy and wanted wear**. Yet he admits that both were actually worn about the same.
3. That morning both roads lay equally covered with leaves that no step had trodden black. He keeps the first road "for another day", though he doubts he will ever come back, because one way leads on to another.
4. He imagines that **"ages and ages hence"** he will tell this story **"with a sigh"**: two roads diverged, he took **the one less travelled by**, and that has **made all the difference**.

## Poetic devices
| Device | Example |
|---|---|
| Metaphor (extended) | The **roads** = choices in life; the **wood** = the world/life's journey |
| Imagery | "yellow wood", "grassy and wanted wear", "leaves no step had trodden black" |
| Personification | the road "wanted wear" |
| Repetition | "And looked down one... Then took the other"; "ages and ages" |
| Inversion | "long I stood" |
| Enjambment | lines run on without pause, e.g. stanza 1 |

> [!REMEMBER] Form
> Four stanzas of five lines (quintains). Rhyme scheme **ABAAB**, e.g. wood / both / stood / could / undergrowth.

## Themes and message
- **Decision-making**: life presents choices; we must choose and accept consequences.
- **Individuality**: choosing the less-travelled path suggests courage to be different.
- **Regret and reflection**: the "sigh" can show nostalgia, satisfaction or regret; Frost leaves it ambiguous.

> [!MISTAKE] The roads were not very different
> Students often write that one road was clearly untrodden. The poet says they were "really about the same"; the difference lies in how we later describe our choices.

> [!TIP] Likely questions
> Why did the poet feel sorry? Why did he choose the second road? What does "that has made all the difference" mean? Identify the rhyme scheme and a metaphor.

## Quick recap
- Two roads in a yellow wood = choices in life.
- He took the "less travelled" road and it shaped his life.
- ABAAB, extended metaphor, imagery.

## The big idea
In **Coates Kinney's** poem, the speaker lies in bed in a cottage on a rainy night. The **patter of rain** on the roof brings back sweet **memories of his childhood**, especially of his loving **mother**. The poem is about **nostalgia**, the pleasure of remembering the past.

## Summary
- **Stanza 1**: Dark, humid clouds cover the starry sky and the darkness seems to weep in rainy tears. What a joy it is to lie in the bed of a cottage room and listen to the patter of soft rain overhead.
- **Stanza 2**: Every **tinkle on the shingles** has an **echo in the heart**. Many dreamy fancies come alive and a thousand recollections weave their **air-threads into woof**.
- **Stanza 3**: He remembers his **mother**, who used to look fondly at her dreamers before they slept. As he listens to the rain, he imagines her loving look again.

> [!KEY] Shingles and woof

## Poetic devices

| Alliteration | "**h**umid... **h**over"; "**s**oft rain over**h**ead" |

| Transferred epithet | "melancholy darkness", "dreamy fancies" |

> [!REMEMBER] Form

## Themes

- **Mother's love** as the poet's most precious memory.

## The big idea
**Ruskin Bond** humorously narrates the mischief of **Toto**, a pet **monkey** his grandfather bought. Toto destroys things, scares the family and creates chaos until Grandfather finally decides he cannot keep him. The story shows the **joys and troubles of keeping wild animals as pets**.

=== iswaran-the-storyteller ===
## The big idea
In **R. K. Laxman's** story, **Iswaran** is the cook and helper of **Mahendra**, a junior supervisor who is sent to remote construction sites. Iswaran's real talent is **storytelling**: he narrates thrilling tales of adventure, horror and ghosts with suspense and drama. One of his ghost stories finally frightens Mahendra away.

## Summary
1. **Mahendra**, a bachelor, works for a firm that sends him to faraway construction sites, where he lives in sheds or tents. Iswaran follows him everywhere, cooks well, finds vegetables in the wilderness and makes him comfortable.
2. Every night Iswaran tells **stories**, which are better than television. He borrows from the Tamil thrillers he reads, building suspense with pauses, gestures and facial expressions.
3. **The elephant story**: Iswaran claims that when he was a schoolboy, a wild **tusker** escaped from the **timber yard** and ran through the town. While everyone fled, he picked up a **stick** and struck the elephant on its **third toenail**; the elephant collapsed. Mahendra is sceptical but enjoys the tale.
4. Iswaran says the region where they now live is **haunted**, as the site was once a **burial ground**. He describes a **female ghost**, a skeleton carrying a **foetus** in her arms, who appears on full-moon nights. Mahendra laughs at this as nonsense.
5. One full-moon night, Mahendra wakes to a moaning sound and sees a dark, cloud-like shape near his window, holding a bundle. Terrified, he hides and sweats.
6. The next morning he decides to **leave the haunted place**, and Iswaran's storytelling has won.

## Characters
- **Iswaran**: resourceful, cheerful, a cook and helper with a vivid imagination; a born storyteller who exaggerates.
- **Mahendra**: practical, rational, mild-tempered; he enjoys stories but believes he is too sensible to fear ghosts, until the end.

## Themes
- **Power of imagination and storytelling**.
- **Suspense and suggestion** can affect even rational minds.
- Humour in human fear.

> [!MISTAKE] Is the ghost real?
> The story never confirms a real ghost. What Mahendra "sees" may be the effect of Iswaran's suggestion on his mind. Write this ambiguity in answers.

> [!TIP] Likely questions
> How did Iswaran make his stories interesting? Narrate the elephant episode. Why did Mahendra leave? What qualities made Iswaran an asset to Mahendra?

## Quick recap
- Mahendra: junior supervisor at remote sites; Iswaran: cook and storyteller.
- Elephant tale (stick on third toenail) and female ghost tale.
- Ghost story scares Mahendra; he leaves.

=== in-the-kingdom-of-fools ===
## The big idea
This Kannada **folk tale** (retold by **A. K. Ramanujan**) is set in a kingdom where the king and minister are **idiots**. Night is day, everything costs the same, and justice is absurd. A wise **guru** saves his foolish **disciple** with a clever trick. It shows the value of **wisdom** and the danger of living in a foolish system for selfish gain.

## The absurd rules
- People must **sleep during the day and work at night**; anyone caught working by day is punished.
- **Everything costs one duddu**: a measure of rice or a bunch of bananas, the same price.

## Summary
1. A **guru and his disciple** arrive. Seeing that food is so cheap, the disciple wants to stay and grow fat. The guru warns that a kingdom of fools is dangerous and leaves, but the disciple stays.
2. A **thief** breaks into a **merchant's** house; an old **wall collapses** and kills him. His **brother** complains to the king.
3. The king summons the merchant, who blames the **bricklayer** who built the wall (in the merchant's father's time).
4. The bricklayer blames a **dancing girl** whose jingling anklets distracted him. She blames the **goldsmith**, who made her walk up and down by not finishing her jewellery. The goldsmith says he was busy with jewellery ordered by the **merchant's father**.
5. The king concludes the **merchant** is guilty (as his father's heir). A stake is prepared, but the merchant is **too thin** to be impaled properly. The king orders that a **fat man** be found instead. The servants find the **disciple**, who has grown fat on cheap food.
6. The disciple prays to his guru, who arrives. The guru whispers a plan. At the stake, the guru and disciple **argue about who should die first**. Asked why, the guru says the stake is "the stake of the god of justice": **whoever dies on it now will be reborn as the king** of this country, and the next will be reborn as the minister.
7. Not wanting to lose his throne in his next life, the **king** has himself and his **minister** impaled. The people then ask the guru and disciple to rule. The guru agrees on condition that the old laws are changed.

> [!KEY] Moral
> **Wisdom saves; greed traps.** The disciple's greed for cheap food nearly killed him; the guru's presence of mind saved him and freed the kingdom from foolish rulers.

## Characters
- **King and minister**: foolish, irrational, cruel in judgement.
- **Guru**: wise, far-sighted, quick-witted.
- **Disciple**: simple, greedy, but devoted to his guru.

## Themes
- Wisdom versus foolishness; the importance of rational justice.
- Greed leads to danger.
- Humour and satire of absurd rulers.

> [!MISTAKE] Order of blame
> Merchant → bricklayer → dancing girl → goldsmith → merchant's father. Students often mix up the dancing girl and the goldsmith.

> [!TIP] Likely questions
> What were the two strange things about the kingdom? Why did the guru not want to stay? How did he save his disciple? How did the king die?

## Quick recap
- Night is day; everything costs one duddu.
- Absurd trial over the thief's death; merchant too thin, fat disciple chosen.
- Guru's trick: rebirth as king; foolish king and minister die.

W[3]: How did the guru save his disciple?
A: When the disciple was about to be impaled, the guru whispered a plan to him. They began quarrelling about who would die first on the stake. The puzzled king asked why, and the guru said that whoever died on this stake would be reborn as the king, and the next as the minister. The foolish king did not want anyone else to become king, so he and his minister went to the stake themselves, and the disciple was saved.

W[5]: Why did the guru say the Kingdom of Fools was not a good place to live? Was he right?
A: The guru saw that the king and minister were idiots who had reversed night and day and priced everything at one duddu. He said that in such a place one cannot predict what will happen, as there is no logic or justice; one may be punished for no fault. He was proved right: the trial over the thief's death passed blame absurdly from the merchant to the bricklayer, dancing girl and goldsmith, and finally the innocent disciple was chosen to die only because he was fat. Only the guru's wit saved him. The story teaches that we should not live where reason is absent just for comfort or greed.
