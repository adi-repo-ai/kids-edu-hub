## Section B | 2 | Very short answer questions.
W[2]: What was the Zollverein? Write any one of its effects.
A: The Zollverein was a customs union formed in 1834 at the initiative of Prussia, joined by most German states. (1)
It abolished tariff barriers and reduced the number of currencies from over thirty to two, encouraging economic unity and national feeling. (1)

W[2]: Why is resource planning essential in India? Give two reasons.
A: India has enormous diversity in the availability of resources: some regions are rich in some resources and poor in others. (1)
Planning ensures balanced and judicious use, prevents over-exploitation and conserves resources for future generations (sustainable development). (1)

W[2]: Give one prudential and one moral reason why power sharing is desirable.
A: Prudential: power sharing reduces the possibility of conflict between social groups and ensures political stability. (1)
Moral: power sharing is the very spirit of democracy; people who are affected by policies have a right to be consulted. (1)

W[2]: What is disguised unemployment? Give an example.
A: A situation in which more people are working than needed, so that even if some are removed, production does not fall; everyone appears to be employed but no one is fully employed. (1)
Example: five members of a family work on a small farm that needs only three; removing two would not reduce output. (1)

## Section C | 3 | Short answer questions.
W[3]: How did the First World War create a new economic and political situation in India?
A: Defence expenditure rose greatly, financed by war loans and increased taxes; customs duties were raised and income tax was introduced. (1)
Prices nearly doubled between 1913 and 1918, causing extreme hardship for common people. (1)
Villagers were forcibly recruited into the army; crop failures in 1918–19 and 1920–21 and an influenza epidemic caused acute food shortages and deaths. This anger fuelled the national movement. (1)

W[3]: Explain three effects of the Great Depression (1929) on the Indian economy.
A: India's exports and imports nearly halved between 1928 and 1934; agricultural prices fell sharply (wheat prices fell by about 50% between 1928 and 1934). (1)
Peasants and farmers suffered badly, but the colonial government refused to reduce revenue demands, so peasants fell into debt and used up their savings. (1)
India became an exporter of precious metals, especially gold; there was widespread rural unrest, leading to the Civil Disobedience Movement. (1)

W[3]: Describe any three features of black soil.
A: Black soil (regur) is black in colour, formed from lava (basalt) rocks; ideal for growing cotton, so it is called black cotton soil. It is found in the Deccan trap – Maharashtra, Saurashtra, Malwa, Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh. (1)
It is made of extremely fine clayey material and has a high capacity to hold moisture. (1)
It is rich in calcium carbonate, magnesium, potash and lime but poor in phosphoric content; it develops deep cracks in summer, helping aeration, and becomes sticky when wet. (1)

W[3]: Describe any three features of the 1992 constitutional amendment that made the third tier of democracy more powerful.
A: It is now constitutionally mandatory to hold regular elections to local government bodies. (1)
Seats are reserved in the elected bodies and executive heads for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Other Backward Classes, and at least one-third of all positions are reserved for women. (1)
An independent State Election Commission conducts elections, and state governments must share some powers and revenue with local bodies. (1)

W[3]: Why are formal sources of credit better than informal sources for poor households?
A: Informal lenders such as moneylenders charge very high interest rates, so a large part of earnings goes in repaying loans. (1)
High interest can push borrowers into a debt trap, where they borrow again to repay old loans. (1)
Formal sources (banks, cooperatives) are supervised by the RBI, charge lower interest and have fair terms, so cheap and affordable credit helps development. (1)

## Section D | 5 | Long answer questions (the last question is a name-and-locate question based on the political map of India).
W[5]: Describe the process of unification of Germany.
A: In the 1800s, middle-class Germans tried to unite the German confederation into a nation-state through the Frankfurt Parliament (1848), but it was suppressed by the monarchy and military supported by the large landowners (Junkers) of Prussia. (1)
Prussia took the leadership of the movement for national unification. (1)
Its chief minister, Otto von Bismarck, was the architect of this process, carried out with the help of the Prussian army and bureaucracy. (1)
Three wars over seven years – with Denmark, Austria and France – ended in Prussian victory and completed the process. (1)
In January 1871, the Prussian king William I was proclaimed German Emperor in a ceremony at the Hall of Mirrors in Versailles. (1)

W[5]: What are multipurpose river projects? Explain their advantages and the reasons why they are being opposed.
A: Projects that use the water of a river for many purposes – irrigation, hydroelectricity, flood control, water supply, fish breeding, inland navigation and recreation – are multipurpose projects. Jawaharlal Nehru called dams the "temples of modern India". (1)
Advantages (any two): irrigation, electricity, flood control, water supply for domestic and industrial use, fish breeding, tourism. (2)
Opposition (any two): regulating river flow affects sediment flow and aquatic life; large-scale displacement of local communities; submergence of forests and vegetation; reservoirs can cause floods and induce earthquakes; excessive irrigation leads to salinisation of soil; benefits often go to large landowners. Examples: Narmada Bachao Andolan, Tehri Dam Andolan. (2)

W[5]: Explain any five functions of political parties in a democracy.
A: Any five, 1 mark each:
Parties contest elections by putting up candidates.
Parties put forward different policies and programmes for voters to choose from.
Parties play a decisive role in making laws in the legislature.
Parties form and run governments.
Parties that lose elections play the role of opposition, criticise the government and voice different views.
Parties shape public opinion by raising and highlighting issues.
Parties provide people access to government machinery and welfare schemes.

W[5]: Explain how technology and the liberalisation of trade and investment policies have stimulated the process of globalisation.
A: Technology: rapid improvement in transportation (containers, faster and cheaper delivery) has made it possible to move goods across long distances at lower cost. (1)
Information and communication technology – telecommunications, computers, the Internet, mobile phones, satellites – allows instant contact and helps services (e.g. designing, call centres) to be produced and delivered across countries. (1)
Liberalisation: removing barriers or restrictions set by the government (such as trade barriers and taxes on imports) is called liberalisation. (1)
Around 1991, India removed many barriers on foreign trade and investment, so goods could be imported and exported easily and foreign companies could set up factories and offices. (1)
International organisations such as the WTO pushed for liberalisation of trade; together, technology and liberalisation encouraged MNCs to spread production across countries, integrating markets. (1)

## Section E | 4 | Case-study questions (1 + 1 + 2 marks).
W[4]: Case study — The salt letter. On 31 January 1930, Mahatma Gandhi sent a letter to Viceroy Irwin stating eleven demands. The most stirring of all was the demand to abolish the salt tax. Irwin was unwilling to negotiate, so Gandhiji started the Salt March accompanied by 78 of his trusted volunteers.
(i) Why did Gandhiji choose salt as a symbol of protest? (1)
(ii) Where did the Salt March end, and what did Gandhiji do there? (1)
(iii) How was the Civil Disobedience Movement different from the Non-Cooperation Movement? (2)
A: (i) Salt was consumed by the rich and the poor alike and was one of the most essential items of food; a tax on it united all sections of society against British rule. (1)
(ii) It ended at Dandi in Gujarat, where Gandhiji violated the law by making salt from boiling sea water (6 April 1930). (1)
(iii) In the Non-Cooperation Movement people were asked only to refuse cooperation with the British. In the Civil Disobedience Movement, they were asked not only to refuse cooperation but also to break colonial laws (e.g. the salt law, forest laws), refuse to pay taxes and boycott foreign goods. (2)

W[4]: Case study — Catching the rain. In the semi-arid and arid regions of Rajasthan, almost all houses traditionally had underground tanks for storing drinking water. In hilly regions, people built diversion channels like the 'guls' or 'kuls' of the Western Himalayas. Today, in Shillong, rooftop rainwater harvesting meets a large part of household needs, and one southern state has made it compulsory for all houses.
(i) What are 'tankas'? (1)
(ii) Name the state that has made rooftop rainwater harvesting compulsory for all houses. (1)
(iii) Give two reasons why rainwater harvesting is important today. (2)
A: (i) Tankas are underground tanks or storage reservoirs in Rajasthan, connected to sloping roofs by pipes, used to store rainwater for drinking. (1)
(ii) Tamil Nadu. (1)
(iii) Any two: it recharges groundwater; it meets water needs in areas of scarcity; it reduces dependence on large dams; it is cheap, eco-friendly and local; it reduces runoff and soil erosion. (2)

W[4]: Case study — From barter to banks. A shoe manufacturer wants to sell shoes and buy wheat. Under barter, he must find a wheat farmer who also wants shoes. Money solves this problem. Today, people also keep money in banks as deposits that they can withdraw on demand, and payments are made by cheques and UPI.
(i) What is meant by 'double coincidence of wants'? (1)
(ii) Who issues currency notes on behalf of the Central Government in India? (1)
(iii) Why are demand deposits considered money? (2)
A: (i) A situation where what one person wants to sell is exactly what the other person wants to buy, and vice versa; it is essential for barter. (1)
(ii) The Reserve Bank of India. (1)
(iii) Deposits in bank accounts can be withdrawn on demand, so they are called demand deposits. They share the essential features of money: they are widely accepted as a means of payment, e.g. through cheques, debit cards and UPI, which transfer money directly from one account to another without using cash. (2)
